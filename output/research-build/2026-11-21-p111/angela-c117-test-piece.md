# Six Neighborhoods Slipped About a Tenth of a Point. The Teachers Describe an Ordinary Week.

Six neighborhoods lost the most ground on the district's school quality index between C116 and C117. The drops were about a tenth of a point each:

| Neighborhood | C116 | C117 |
|---|---|---|
| Fruitvale | 7.3 | 7.2 |
| Jack London | 7.39 | 7.3 |
| Grand Lake | 7.18 | 7.09 |
| Uptown | 7.12 | 7.03 |
| Downtown | 7.28 | 7.2 |
| West Oakland | 8 | 7.92 |

Other neighborhoods barely moved. Adams Point and Laurel each gave up a hundredth, from 7.92 to 7.91. Rockridge still leads the table at 8.36, down from 8.4.

Teacher quality didn't move in any of the six. Fruitvale reads 7.9 and Jack London 8.2. Funding is unchanged too, at 13,000 in Fruitvale and 14,500 in Jack London. Enrollment shifted by one student in most places. The numbers don't say why the index slipped, and I won't guess. I asked three teachers what an ordinary week looks like.

## Fruitvale: the room gets one child fuller

Lila Bennington has taught elementary school in Fruitvale for close to 24 years. The table shows Fruitvale at 427 students, one more than last cycle. She says she can feel that one.

"We've got one more little one in my room than we did last spring, and it doesn't sound like much, but it's the difference between getting to every hand and letting a couple of them wait," Bennington said. The children are "the same bright, wiggly, wonderful chaos they've always been," she said. But "the resources feel tighter, and the families feel more stretched."

Her early-warning system is not a score. "A kid who usually bounces in at 8:15 comes in quiet, or a parent who usually lingers at drop-off just hands me the backpack and hurries off. That's my signal."

Down the hall, ESL teacher Isabel Lopes, 42, watches attendance. She looks at who is in a seat before the bell, who slides in late and who has their head down. "There's this one group that always walks in together, and when one of them's missing, the others notice before I do," she said. "That's the thing about regular — it's not the same as empty. It's a rhythm."

Lopes also admitted to a doubt I recognize from my own last year in the classroom. "Other weeks I look at the calendar and every square is the same shape, and I think, is this peace or is this just... the same shape? I haven't decided. But I know the kids are the ones who tell me which one it is."

I have no evidence that a run of identical weeks is hurting Fruitvale. A tenth of a point could also be noise. Lopes's question is still a fair one to ask about a quiet stretch of weeks.

## Jack London: "I don't see a decline"

Lo Thoop, 68, teaches creative writing in Jack London. That neighborhood's index fell from 7.39 to 7.3 and it has 203 students. Thoop doesn't see the dip in the room.

"My kids are writing more than they have in years — essays about the waterfront, about the oyster bar lights coming on early, about their own blocks," Thoop said. "The dip they're measuring, if it's real, it's not something I feel in the day-to-day."

Thoop credits the Oakland Alternative Response Initiative with calmer streets. "They're less jumpy, more ready to sit and put words on paper. So no, I don't see a decline. I see kids who are present, and that's the foundation for everything else."

That is one teacher's read. I haven't tested the link between the initiative and attendance, and the index can't show it either.

## What the table can't tell us

Two teachers in two classrooms describe different weeks. Bennington's is tighter and more stretched. Thoop's is calmer and full of writing. Neither describes a school in trouble. The index gave six neighborhoods a small push downward, and the teachers describe weeks that mostly held together.

The stable weeks are the story in a steady-state cycle, and the stability is worth watching. Class sizes creeping up by one, and families dropping backpacks and hurrying off, are the kind of thing that shows up in a teacher's notes before it shows up in a table. I'll keep asking the teachers at C118.
