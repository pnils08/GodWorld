At 7:40 at 12th St Oakland City Center, Eleanor Rivera is watching the station adjust to a number that arrived before any announcement could explain it: 23,848 riders this cycle, 5,472 more than in C108. On-time performance was 85%, four points better than the prior cycle. The station is not presented here as broken. It is presented as doing the less visible work of absorbing change.

“At 12th Street, we've definitely felt the uptick in ridership. It's not just numbers - it's people adjusting their routines, and us adjusting right along with them. The team just... adapts. No big announcements, just everyone doing their part to keep things moving smoothly,” said Rivera, a BART station manager.

That is the infrastructure story in its least theatrical form. A station takes a larger movement of people, and the system records whether trains remain on time. The physical work sits between those two statements.

Jerald Jensen, an AC Transit janitor, described the same increase from the floor rather than the schedule. “You wanna know about 12th Street? I see it in the wear and tear—more coffee spills to mop up, more trash cans overflowing by noon. Used to be you could catch your breath between rushes, but now? It's constant. Folks pouring in for work, for games, for whatever's happening downtown.”

His account is not a second ridership counter. It is the maintenance consequence of one. It also puts a limit on the clean interpretation of the 85% figure: on-time performance can improve while the ordinary surfaces of a station require more attention. Those are not contradictory measurements. They describe different parts of the same system.

The increase was not confined to 12th St Oakland City Center. Across eight stations and 10 corridors, total ridership was 99,582, compared with 70,489 in C108. 19th St Oakland rose to 17,104 riders, up 4,800, with on-time performance at 86%, up 10 points. Coliseum rose 4,859 riders to 9,724, with on-time performance at 83%, up five points. Fruitvale rose 3,756 to 10,414, while MacArthur rose 2,923 to 12,030.

The pattern is not uniformly clean. Rockridge rose 2,121 riders to 9,343, but its on-time performance fell two points to 78%. That is the useful warning in a cycle otherwise easy to describe as recovery or momentum: more use and better punctuality can coexist in the same network, but not necessarily at every station.

Joel Wren, an AC Transit mechanic, put the labor underneath the service measure. “You know, I've been seeing more folks hopping on the buses lately—especially down at 12th Street. It's good to see people out and about, trusting the system again. But what really gets me is the crew keeping those buses running. Those mechanics and drivers? They're the ones putting in the long hours to make sure everyone gets where they need to go, especially with all these extra riders. Makes you appreciate the work that goes on behind the scenes.”

Coen Cartier, an AC Transit transit operator, offered the shorter version from the moving side of the network: “I’ve seen more folks hopping on the buses lately, especially around 12th Street. It’s been busy, but it feels good to know we’re helping people get where they need to go. The team’s been working hard to keep things running smoothly, and it’s paying off.”

The cycle gives no basis for saying which intervention produced the stronger numbers, or whether the pattern will persist after the game day and events in Downtown and Lake Merritt. It does show a system carrying more use while its results diverge by station. The forward question is not only whether civic events and infrastructure projects will continue to send people through transit. It is whether the quiet adaptations Rivera described can keep matching the wear Jensen sees, the work Wren describes and the uneven punctuality Rockridge records.
