I leaned against the cinder-block wall outside Deacon Seymour’s office, the one he keeps cracked open even on off days. Benji Dillon hasn’t retired yet. The 5-time Cy Young winner leaves the door open after Keane walks through his.

Mike Paulson stood just inside the frame, hands in his pockets. “I’ve talked to Benji. Not about a role, not about a number — about where his head is at. He’s earned the right to take his time with this. Vinnie walking through that door first changed something for him, and Mark leaving for Colorado changed something for all of us. I’m not going to push a five-time Cy Young winner into a decision because the calendar says we need one. When he’s ready to talk about what 2042 looks like — starter, bullpen, anything — my door’s open. He knows that. He’s always known that.”

Deacon Seymour sat on the edge of his desk, the same spot he’s used since the C100 coverage first caught him settling into the job. “Look, I’m not going to sit here and pretend I’ve got the rotation mapped out like it’s a done deal. Benji hasn’t told us he’s done, and until he does, I’m not closing that door for him. That’s his call, not mine. We’ve got young arms coming up, and we’ve got guys who can step into the bullpen, but you don’t replace a five-time Cy Young winner by just plugging in a name. You leave the light on. That’s what we’re doing. We’re planning like he’s not here, because we have to, but I’ve got a spot in my heart—and in that bullpen—that’s his if he wants it. Vinnie’s gone, Mark’s gone, and that hurts. But Benji’s still here in the city, still here in the community, and if he decides he’s got one more run in him, I want him to know the door’s open. That’s not strategy. That’s just respect.”

Vinnie Keane’s voice carried from the hallway, the same low timbre the C115 piece recorded the day he made it official. “Benji’s not done yet, and I think that’s the thing people keep missing. We came up together, we won together, and when I walked away I felt that weight lift off my shoulders — but Benji’s carrying something different. He’s got the clinic, he’s got Rick, he’s got Maya, and he’s got a city that still sees him as the guy who threw three no-hitters. That’s not pressure, that’s a reason to stay. And for the clubhouse? Look, I was the voice in there for a long time, and Mark was the other one. Now both of us are gone. If Benji comes back, he’s not just pitching — he’s the bridge. He’s the guy who can tell Kevin Clark what it means to wear this uniform in this city, because he’s lived it. If he doesn’t, then the young guys have to figure it out on their own, and that’s a harder road. I hope he takes his time. I hope he listens to what he actually wants, not what the calendar says. Because the game will still be there. The city will still be there. But that feeling of being the last one standing from a group that meant something — that’s a heavy thing to carry alone. I know because I just put it down.”

The office light stayed on. Nobody moved to close it.
