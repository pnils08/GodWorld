# Chinatown's 135 Sick Residents, and the Six Beds on the Record

Akua Adeyemi works with her toolbag set down by the door and her wrench kept quiet. She learned that on Chinatown jobs, where she has often found a person sleeping in the next room. A plumber in the neighborhood, she says she can tell what kind of household she has walked into before she opens the cabinet under the sink.

"I go into a lot of homes, and you can tell pretty quick when someone's down," she said. "It's not the mess or the dishes — it's the quiet. The TV off in the middle of the day. A chair pulled up by the window where somebody's been sitting a long time. Sometimes the medicine gets moved to the kitchen counter so it's right there where someone'll remember it. And you learn to work quiet, not to bang pipes, because somebody's sleeping in the next room."

The count behind what she sees is on the neighborhood sheet for cycle 115. Chinatown carries 135 sick residents, the most of any of the city's 22 neighborhoods. That is 6.1 percent of its 2,205 residents, at or over the 6 percent watch bar. The figure is down 2 from cycle 114. East Oakland follows at 117, then Ivy Hill at 115 and Fruitvale at 109. Citywide the sheet totals 1,771.

## The care trail

The hospital census reads in a short line. Chinatown's 135 sick residents produced 3 admissions this cycle. At the close, 6 Chinatown residents were in care and 6 beds were occupied. The census tracks none of them by name.

Other neighborhoods read much the same. Adams Point has 82 sick residents, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Dimond has 90 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Eastlake has 86 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. East Oakland, with 117 sick, shows 2 admissions and 4 in care. Citywide the hospital took 34 admissions this cycle, none of them tracked residents. It held 69 in care at the close, 69 beds occupied, and 35 people left.

The ledger holds four residents on its hospital and cause records, and none lives in Chinatown. Yu Zen and Maurice Franklin are listed in Rockridge. Deacon Seymour is listed in Lake Merritt. Zen and Seymour were admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. Franklin was admitted in cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu. Their rows say they remain in hospital care. The record says nothing more about them, and this report adds nothing.

The census does not say what happened to the other 129 sick Chinatown residents outside those six beds. It does not say whether they were treated, where, or by whom. The record holds the count of sick people and the count of beds. The space between them is blank.

## What a house does

Adeyemi has her own account of that space, from the jobs she works. She is a plumber, not a clinician, and she does not claim to know who is ill or why.

"I see more of that in Chinatown than anywhere else I work," she said. "People here take care of their own — you'll see a neighbor bring soup, or a cousin come by after their shift. It's not always the hospital that carries the load. It's the family that rearranges the whole house around one sick person."

Deacon Seymour knows the other side of the ledger. His own admission, listed under Lake Merritt, is one of the few rows with a name on it. He said the name is the part most people never get.

"I spent my own stretch in a hospital bed not long ago, and I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you," Seymour said. "But I also know that bed I was in—that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city, and if the ledger doesn't show them, that doesn't mean they're not there. It means nobody's counting. And when nobody's counting, people get missed. That's the part that keeps me up at night."

In clinical terms, a number like 135 with six beds behind it is interesting, which is the word doctors use when they mean concerning. It does not mean Chinatown is in crisis. The count is falling, down 2 this cycle, and the sheet does not say what the 135 are sick with. The care that fills the gap is real. A neighbor brings soup, a cousin comes after a shift, and the medicine sits on the counter where someone will remember it. But none of it appears in a census.

## What the record still lacks

Three records would let the neighborhood be read as something other than a total:

- where Chinatown's sick residents are actually receiving care;
- whether the 3 admissions and 6 beds this cycle reflect the need or only the part that reached a hospital;
- whether any Chinatown resident will appear on the hospital ledger by name, so a count becomes a case someone can follow.

The ledger names no one from Chinatown. What would it take for the neighborhood's sick to be seen, counted and cared for by name? No office on the record has yet answered that.
