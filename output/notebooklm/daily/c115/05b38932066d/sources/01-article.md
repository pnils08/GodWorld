# The Sea-Level Desk Is Down to Three

By Jordan Velez, The Bay Tribune

**By Jordan Velez, Bay Tribune Business Desk**

Mina Linas's job title says what her employer is for. She is a sea level monitoring technician at Marine Systems in the Baylight District, and this cycle she is one of only three people the company still employs. The business ledger listed five at Marine Systems last cycle. It lists three now. Growth is down 2.9%. Mina Linas is the one worker on its roster the ledger names.

Two jobs sounds small until you set it against the size of the company. Marine Systems has lost 40% of its staff in a single cycle. At the ledger's average salary for the firm, $180,000, the two lost positions come to something like $360,000 a year in payroll that no longer reaches a desk in the district. That is my arithmetic from the ledger's average, not a figure the company has reported. The ledger lists no revenue at all for Marine Systems, so there is no top line to compare the cut against. The ledger records the loss and gives no reason for it. I don't have one either, and I am not going to guess.

The district around that small office is growing.

A few blocks' walk takes you past most of the businesses that make Baylight what it is. Anthropic shows 86 employees on its ledger row, an average salary of $185,000, growth of 2.7%, and revenue of $649.8 million. That revenue rose by $335,000 since last cycle, which is close to what Marine Systems just took out of its payroll. The roster names Kelvon Ochoa, Iggy Davis and Linda Chow as AI safety researchers there, and Broderick Mitchell as a security guard. DigitalOcean is bigger still: 800 employees, growth of 5.2%, and revenue of $521.4 million, up $525,000 in a cycle.

The Baylight Construction Authority is the one actually putting up the district. Its row shows 451 employees, an average salary of $66,868, growth of 1.7%, and revenue of $31.3 million, up $10,000 since last cycle. Keisha Ramos sits at the top of that roster as director of the Baylight Redevelopment Authority. Below her are the trades: Brianna Lee, a historic preservation carpenter; Tariq Jello, an appliance repair technician; and Talmon Lint, a solar panel installation lead. Thirty-six more are on the roster unnamed. Those are the people doing the building.

Oakland Casino Town is moving the other way, more slowly. It has 99 employees, one fewer than last cycle, and growth of minus 2.3%. The ledger puts its average salary at $70,000 and lists no revenue. The roster names Philly Rodriguez as casino manager and Manfred Owens as the booky. It also names Gregory Mims, filed under organized crime, which is the kind of roster line a business reporter notes and does not try to read anything into. The ledger doesn't give a reason for that lost job either.

The engine's notes on the district include one line that stays with me. Ron Arenson drank the free cold brew in a company kitchen and felt completely disconnected from the neighborhood outside. Baylight has a lot of company kitchens. The money in the district does not all end up in the same places.

Look at where the losses are. The two firms with no revenue line on the ledger, the casino and the sea-level outfit, are the two shedding people. The firms with nine-figure revenue are adding money. The builder is barely moving: $10,000 in new revenue across 451 workers. Baylight isn't the only place losing small payrolls. Across town in Ivy Hill, Ivy Hill Cafe, Ivy Cleaners, Ivy Hill Family Dental and Hillcrest Kitchen each lost two workers this cycle. In neighborhoods like that, two jobs can be a whole shift.

My mother ran a laundromat on 7th Street until the building sold in 2017. What I learned from watching that is that the businesses that go first are rarely the weakest. They are the ones whose value doesn't show up on anyone's balance sheet. A laundromat's value was the folding tables and the neighbors who used them. A sea-level monitor's value is information a waterfront district needs and doesn't pay for directly. It is the warning before the water comes up. Nobody books that as revenue until the day it's missing.

Baylight is being built on the bay. The district's construction crews work on new structures every day of the week. Its largest tenants are adding revenue every cycle. The three people left at Marine Systems are the ones whose job is to keep watching the water that all of that sits beside.

I don't know who decided Marine Systems should run with three people instead of five. I don't know whether the redevelopment authority Keisha Ramos directs, the tenants adding hundreds of thousands of dollars a cycle, or anyone else with money in the district has any stake in keeping that work staffed. Those are the questions Baylight's growth hasn't answered yet.

If the boom is building this district for the future but the sea-level monitor is the one getting cut, what exactly is the future being built for?
