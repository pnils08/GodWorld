THE CYCLE PULSE — Y3C8
The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

Cycle 112 opened clear after two gray ones, and you could hear the city exhale in the reporting. Noah Tan found Derlik Green taking the long way down Broadway, lingering at the light, nodding at the crew at SpeedyBurger without checking the time. Xavier Ramington in Temescal said the sun didn't change his morning, only made the familiar warmer. Then there was Brian Adams in Jack London, standing in the strip of light by the radiator just long enough to warm his hands before going back to his tasks. Same sun, three ways of using it. I think that's the week in miniature. Everyone is living under the same conditions and getting different amounts of it.

Start with Yu Zen, because he showed up in two places that don't sit comfortably together. Celeste Tran's wire piece caught him on his morning walks through Rockridge, delighted. He'd seen new faces moving into West Oakland and the neighborhoods around it, heard different music drifting out of apartments, and said his family loves it, that the kids are always talking about the latest artists. It's a generous, open-handed account of a city filling up. Then Ines Sorensen's culture desk carried Priya Narang's after-work note, and there he was again. "Yu Zen's file is still open on my desk," the EMS director wrote. Twenty-nine years old, Rockridge, months of rehab ahead for a hand that might never close right. She asked whether they got there fast enough and whether it would ever feel like they did.

I can't tell you how those two pictures line up in time. The record doesn't say whether the morning walks came before the injury or after, and I won't guess. What I can say is that the man who hears new music through apartment windows is also a file on a tired director's desk. A city's buzz and its response times are the same city, and he is standing in both. Narang's question about speed hangs next to Vanessa Tran-Muñoz's note about the Chinatown call, where someone eventually came but the wait was too long, and she knew West Oakland's crews would have been faster. Nobody has answered either of them yet. Clarissa Dane added her own loose thread from West Oakland: Fruitvale's figure sits at eighteen on her internal sheet and twenty-three on the public file, and she can't tell whether it's rounding or lag. A city that can't agree on its own number is going to have trouble agreeing on how fast it moved.

Vinnie Keane crossed our pages too, and his two angles fit each other even less. Elliot Marbury's analysis treats him as a hitter whose future is the question. Keane's 2041 line shows a strikeout only once every twenty-one at-bats and thirteen home runs in thirty-seven games, a rate Marbury calls an outlier on a thin sample, not yet a changed hitter. Next season, he argues, decides whether it's a new baseline. That is how you write about a man with next season in front of him. Then P Slayer walked into the Blue Lattern Bar in Jack London for what was billed as a farewell for Keane and Benji Dillon, and Keane said, plain as a man can, that he's "still getting used to not being as active with the A's." He also said he hadn't been honored the way the question suggested, then described a lively turn-out that felt like nostalgia and celebration. Dillon, two stools down, felt the tension, some people not entirely on board with the farewell.

So which is it? A slugger whose next season is the story, or a legend being toasted on his way out? I don't know, and I'd rather say so than split the difference. Celeste Tran caught him at a community health fair at Or Hashalom Renewal Community, saying the whole neighborhood was rallying around the team. The love is real, and I won't take it from the people in that bar. But a farewell for a man whose analysts are still waiting on his next season is a strange thing for the city to be holding at once. If the farewell is for Keane the player, someone should say so out loud. If it's for something else, the bar should know what it's toasting.

The same gap between the record and the room runs through the money. Carmen Delaine's ledger confirms that month eleven of the West Oakland Stabilization Fund cleared at $400,000, matching month ten, with the 23-day standard held. The mayor's office put out two statements that both praised it. Marcus Webb, who runs the disbursements, wrote a quieter version after work. Six files are still waiting on title search, and the standard doesn't tell him which of those households is one missed payment from losing everything. He walked past a couple of the addresses, saw the lights on, and didn't stop. The spreadsheet says they're ahead. The spreadsheet never has to sit in a kitchen and explain a delay. That is the contradiction I'd put at the top of anyone's list, a program that can be on schedule and still have six kitchens waiting.

Maria Keen found the same shape in East Oakland. Eight programs are active there, two of them, the pantry and the seniors lunch at Foothill Baptist Tabernacle, running since cycle 78. Two of the newer ones are youth leagues named for their founders, Mark Aitken and Marcus Whitfield, and neither founder lives on that block. Keen's point isn't that the programs are lesser. It's that the block has been handed things by people who go home elsewhere, and she wants to hear from a parent. So do I. Bobby Chen-Ramirez, watching the crane's shadows over his Jack London health center site, said much the same about development. The question is who the building is for once it's up.

A few smaller notes. Jade Orion's mentions ticked up to eighteen and she says nothing around Lake Merritt feels different, which is the most grounded thing anyone said about fame all week. Tara Ellison mentioned being asked to redo work, and I'll just say that's part of the job here and she did it. And Keisha Ramos is waiting on soil test results for the C115 review, which will tell us something about what Baylight can promise.

Next week I'm watching four things. The month-twelve disbursement review comes up in Cycle 113, and I want to see whether the six waiting files shrink or just get quieter. I'm watching for any word on what Keane is actually doing, playing or leaving, because the city deserves a straight answer before the next toast. I'm watching Yu Zen's hand, and whether the system that got to him can answer Narang's question. And I'm watching that eighteen and twenty-three in Fruitvale until someone tells us which is true.

By Celeste Tran | Bay Tribune Wire

West Oakland drew the largest share of new arrivals this cycle, eighteen residents shifting in while Jade Orion’s mentions ticked up one more time to eighteen total. The musician’s name is moving through conversations the way the new faces are moving through blocks, and the pattern is pulling other names along with it.

Yu Zen caught the change on morning walks through Rockridge. “I’ve seen more people moving into West Oakland and other neighborhoods around here. It’s changing the vibe. With folks like Jade Orion making waves, it’s like the whole city’s buzzing. I mean, I’ve been out for morning walks and seen new faces, heard different music drifting from apartments. It’s all pretty exciting, to be honest. My family loves it; the kids are always talking about the latest artists and what’s trending.”

Jade Orion, still based in Lake Merritt, shrugged at the count when the number came up. “I’m not sure what you mean by ‘rising fame.’ I’ve been doing my music here in Lake Merritt for a while now, and it’s nice to see people appreciating it. I haven’t really noticed anything out of the ordinary happening around here because of it, though. The neighborhood feels the same to me—people are still out for their evening jogs and walks around the lake, and the volunteers from the Cathedral of the Living Word are still cleaning up the streets. I’ve just been focusing on my craft and enjoying the community.”

Tara Ellison, whose own mentions rose to twelve, kept the same measured tone while noting the activity around her in Rockridge. “To be honest, I’m not sure my work is reflecting or influencing any trends in Oakland. I’ve just been doing my job as a journalist, covering local news and events. I did notice that a manager at Bay Tribune asked me to redo some work recently, which was a bit frustrating. I’ve also seen a lot of activity in Rockridge, like the film crew near the waterfront and people moving around the neighborhood. I’m not sure if that’s related to any larger trends, though.”

Vinnie Keane, whose five mentions this cycle keep the A’s in the mix, pointed to the same current at a recent community health fair. “I’ve seen a lot of love for the A’s around here, and it’s great to see the community rallying around the team. I was at a community health fair at Or Hashalom Renewal Community recently, and it felt like the whole neighborhood was coming together. People were talking about the A’s, and it was clear that the team is still a big part of our identity. I’m proud to be a part of that, even if it’s just as a legend now. The energy around the team is still electric, and it’s something that brings us all together.”

Jack London picked up twelve new residents, Fruitvale, Adams Point, and Grand Lake each added seven. The numbers sit next to the mention spikes without any single event forcing the link. The city is moving toward the names and the blocks that already carry the loudest current conversation, and the movement itself is becoming the thing people notice next.

By Noah Tan | Bay Tribune Civic

Xavier Ramington stood behind the counter in his Temescal shop, measuring dried herbs into small glass jars. The clear light of cycle 112 reached the front window and lit the rows of tincture bottles, but it did not shift the order of his morning. He still measured the same amounts, still reached for the same labels, still watched the same stretch of Telegraph through the glass.  

“The clear sky today — it's not a relief so much as a reminder,” he said. “The cold weeks, I leaned on the same faces, the same counters, the same conversations. That rhythm kept me steady. So when the sun finally breaks through, it doesn't change what I do — I still open the shop, still mix the same tinctures, still nod at the same folks on Telegraph. The clear day just makes the familiar feel a little warmer, that's all. It's not a new day; it's the same day with better light.”  

Outside, Derlik Green moved along Broadway on his delivery route. The two prior cycles of gray and fog had kept his pace tight between stops. Today the air stayed crisp and the sidewalk traffic loosened. He took the longer block past the usual corners, paused at the light, and spoke with the barista at Midnight Bistro and the crew at SpeedyBurger without checking the time.  

“A clear day after a gray stretch? Yeah, it changes things,” Green said. “When the sun's out and the air's crisp like this, I don't rush the same way. I'll take the long way down Broadway, catch a few more folks on the sidewalk, maybe linger a beat longer at the light. The pace softens. People look up more, nod more. I check in with the regulars — the barista at Midnight Bistro, the crew at SpeedyBurger — and it feels less like a transaction and more like we're all just glad the fog lifted. I'm still working, don't get me wrong, but the city breathes different when it's clear, and I breathe with it.”  

Brian Adams remained inside his Jack London apartment. The strip of light that crossed the floor by the radiator reached only as far as his hands when he stood there for a moment. He returned to the tasks he had set for the day without opening the blinds farther.  

“The sun does reach me, but it doesn't come all the way in,” Adams said. “I keep the blinds cracked in the living room, and when it's clear like today, that strip of light lands on the floor by the radiator. I'll stand in it for a minute, warm my hands, then get back to whatever I'm doing. The winter mood — it's like a fog that sits in my chest, not the kind that burns off by noon. The clear sky helps, sure, but it doesn't change the fact that I've been keeping to myself more. I see the light, I know it's there, but I'm not always ready to walk out into it. That's just where I am right now.”  

Jonas Patel had moved through the earlier overcast, fog, and mild cycles while managing a seasonal health concern that kept his own circle narrow. The current clear stretch reached the same streets the others walked, yet the record shows no listed change in how he spent the day.  

What remains unfinished is whether the clear sky actually reaches the people who spent the cold weeks inside — or whether the weather changes the day without changing the pattern.

# Keane's 2041 Strikeout Rate Is an Outlier on 127 At-Bats. Next Season Decides Whether It Is a New Baseline.

Vinnie Keane's 2041 career line for the Athletics shows 6 strikeouts in 127 at-bats, one every 21.2 at-bats. It also shows 13 home runs, one every 9.8 at-bats, and a slugging percentage of .701 over 37 games. No other season in his 17 on the dossier comes near the strikeout figure. The 16 earlier seasons run from one strikeout every 2.0 at-bats to one every 8.4.

**Claim.** The 2041 strikeout rate is an outlier, not yet a changed hitter. The sample is 127 at-bats, and that is a thin sample. Over 16 earlier seasons his best strikeout rate on a full-season workload is one every 8.4 at-bats, in 2028 on 616 at-bats.

## The multi-year line

Keane is listed as DH / 3B / 2B / SS. The seasons that bear on the claim, from his career line:

| Season | G | AB | HR | SO | BB | HR rate | SO rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2041 | 37 | 127 | 13 | 6 | 0 | 1 per 9.8 AB | 1 per 21.2 AB |
| 2040 | 58 | 209 | 14 | 35 | 8 | 1 per 14.9 AB | 1 per 6.0 AB |
| 2039 | 55 | 164 | 17 | 82 | 35 | 1 per 9.6 AB | 1 per 2.0 AB |
| 2038 | 94 | 328 | 49 | 81 | 49 | 1 per 6.7 AB | 1 per 4.0 AB |
| 2037 | 150 | 592 | 34 | 157 | 88 | 1 per 17.4 AB | 1 per 3.8 AB |
| 2028 | 160 | 616 | 40 | 73 | 73 | 1 per 15.4 AB | 1 per 8.4 AB |

The hinge is 2039. In 164 at-bats he struck out 82 times, one every 2.0 at-bats, the worst rate on the dossier, and walked 35 times. The 2038 season is the home-run peak: 49 home runs in 328 at-bats, one every 6.7. The 2038 and 2039 samples are both short of a full workload, so neither fixes an identity.

Neither short season changes the long run. From 2025 through 2037 every season carries between 409 and 647 at-bats, and the strikeout rate stays between one every 3.8 and one every 8.4 at-bats.

## What the peers make ordinary

Two Athletics hitters are in the same 2041 window. Neither plays Keane's primary listed position of DH.

- **Martin Richards** (3B, nine seasons on his dossier; only 2041 pulled here): 463 at-bats, 27 home runs, 70 strikeouts. That is one home run every 17.1 at-bats and one strikeout every 6.6.
- **Darrin Davis** (LF, two seasons on his dossier; only 2041 pulled here): 374 at-bats, 24 home runs, 42 strikeouts. That is one home run every 15.6 at-bats and one strikeout every 8.9.

Keane's 2041 home-run rate is the densest of the three. His strikeout rate is far past both peers. Davis, at one every 8.9, is the only 2041 line in the group beyond Keane's own prior best of 8.4, and he did it on a workload roughly three times Keane's.

## What the line does not show

- **Walks:** the 2041 walk column reads zero in 127 at-bats. A zero over 127 at-bats is blank of plate discipline evidence, not evidence of it.
- **On-base:** OBP reads .308 against an AVG of .315. The dossier carries no hit-by-pitch or sacrifice columns, so the memo does not reconcile that gap.
- **Repeated cells:** the 2037 and 2034 lines share 592 at-bats, 174 hits, 157 strikeouts and the same AVG, OBP and SLG, but differ in home runs, RBI and walks. The 2035 and 2031 lines share 647 at-bats, 192 hits and 88 strikeouts. The memo reports both as they stand on the dossier.
- **Cause:** nothing on the dossier explains why the rate moved. No injury, swing change or opposing approach is on file, and none is asserted here.

## What would kill the claim

The memo's claim fails if Keane's next season, on a full-season workload (the 2028 mark was 616 at-bats), holds a strikeout rate better than one every 8.4 at-bats. That would put the 2041 figure inside a new range rather than outside the old one. A rate at or worse than 8.4 over the same workload keeps 2041 an outlier. The 2041 sample alone cannot settle it either way.

The bar emptied slow. I stayed until the last glass hit the rail. How will this farewell shape the narrative the A’s carry into the off-season? We already know the soft version they want. The question is whether we let them write it.

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

# West Oakland Stabilization Fund: Month Eleven Clears

The mayor's office put it in writing this cycle, in two statements. The first came from the gavel: "Month-eleven, 23-day standard maintained — this fund delivers because Brenda Okoro's team treats every application like family." The second, from the opening, was blunter: "West Oakland families don't need promises—they need checks that clear. Month-eleven disbursement approved."

Both statements describe the same event, a $400,000 month-eleven disbursement from the West Oakland Stabilization Fund, approved in Cycle 112. The tracker records it as the fund's latest milestone, and it is the first thing this ledger can confirm about the fund this cycle.

The fund's paper trail starts in Cycle 78. The Initiative carries a $28 million budget and a 6-3 vote requirement, and it passed that cycle 6-3. The Initiative was proposed by Avery Santana, and the mayor signed it that same cycle. The record names three of the nine council seats on that vote: Ramon Vega voted yes, Leonard Tran voted no, and Elliott Crane voted yes remotely. The supplied record does not place the votes of the other six seats, so this ledger does not reconstruct the tally beyond the 6-3 result.

The money has moved in steps. The tracker lists a $22.2 million remaining balance against the $28 million total, and cumulative disbursements above $5 million. Month-ten, approved in Cycle 111, was also $400,000. Month-eleven, approved this cycle, matches it. The processing standard has held at 23 days across both approvals, and the record shows no change to that figure.

The clock runs forward. The next scheduled action is a month-twelve disbursement review in Cycle 113. That review is the next point at which the record will show whether the pattern of two identical $400,000 approvals continues. The tracker does not yet say what the month-twelve amount will be, and this ledger will not guess.

The city treasury gives the fund its context without explaining it. This cycle the treasury took in $5.1 million: $5 million from the weekly budget allocation and $122,035 in court money from 27 cleared charges. The balance closed at $115.4 million, up from $110.3 million. The record connects none of those figures to the fund's disbursements, and this ledger does not connect them either.

What the record establishes is narrow. The fund is in its disbursement phase. Approvals are on schedule. The processing standard is held. What it does not establish is the far end of the pipe. The supplied record contains no account of how the approved money reached West Oakland households, which applications it covered, or what changed for the people it was meant for. The phrase "treats every application like family" describes an office's conduct. It is not a measure of outcomes, and the record offers no outcome data to set beside it.

That gap sits between two offices. The mayor's statements speak for the approval and the clock. The tracker holds the dollars and the dates. Neither supplies household-level results, and neither is asked in the supplied record to produce them before the month-twelve review.

The Initiative's own success measure is the 23-day standard. The mayor has said it was maintained, and the record confirms it. If that standard is the measure of success, the question this cycle leaves open is the one the supplied record cannot answer: **if the 23-day standard is the measure of success, what is the measure of impact?**

By Maria Keen | Bay Tribune Culture

# Eight Programs, One Church Name Since Cycle 78

The Foothill Baptist Tabernacle runs a food pantry and a seniors lunch program. Both have been on East Oakland's record since cycle 78. I can't tell you what the room smells like at the hour the doors open, because the record doesn't say and I won't borrow a smell. What the record does say is that the two programs have lasted longer than anything else on this block's list. In this cycle's census, East Oakland holds 327 seniors, and the number moved up by one since cycle 111. One more senior is a small thing to count. It is also one more person who might need a lunch.

Eight programs are active in East Oakland this cycle. I can name six. Foothill's pantry is the mutual-aid one, and the lunch program is filed under health. The rest arrived together in cycle 101: the Coliseum District Vendor Incubator, the East Bay Harm Reduction Alliance, and two youth sports leagues that carry their founders' names. The pantry and lunch table are the older furniture. The newer ones are the ones people call "programs."

The founders are the part that stays with me. Mark Aitken, the First Baseman and A's legend, now Player Rep and Community Liaison, lives in Jack London. His Mark Aitken Youth Baseball League is in East Oakland. Marcus Whitfield coaches youth basketball and lives in Fruitvale. His Eastside Hoopers Coalition is in East Oakland too. Neither man's own address is on the block he built a program for. That doesn't make the programs any less the block's. It means the block has been handed things by people who go home somewhere else, and I think that deserves a plain look.

Aitken's words on the record are these: "I've been involved with the Mark Aitken Youth Baseball League since it started, and I've seen firsthand the positive impact it's had on East Oakland's youth. It's not just about teaching them baseball; it's about giving them a sense of community and discipline. As a player rep and community liaison, I've worked closely with the kids and their families, and it's rewarding to see the difference it's making in their lives."

He says *the kids and their families*, and I hold onto that. A baseball league is built on parents who show up, and I'd like to hear from one of them.

Whitfield's account of the Coalition runs the same direction: "I've seen firsthand how community programs like the Eastside Hoopers Coalition can bring people together and make a real difference in their lives. As the founder of the Coalition, I know how much work and dedication goes into making it happen. It's not just about the program itself, but about the people behind it and the impact it has on the community. The Eastside Hoopers Coalition has definitely changed the landscape for youth sports in East Oakland, providing a safe and supportive environment for kids to develop their skills and build relationships."

*The people behind it.* Both men reach for that phrase. I can't name those people, so I won't pretend to. The record gives me two founders and no volunteers.

The block's other numbers sit beside the programs. East Oakland this cycle counts 476 students and 1,535 adults, up two students and five adults from the last cycle. The hospital census shows 123 sick residents, 2 admissions, and 4 people in care at the close, with 4 beds occupied. None of the four is tracked, which means they are neighbors the record counts but does not name. The justice census shows 6 charges, 1 arrest, and 2 people in custody at the close. Those two are also not named, and they are also this block's.

Council Member Janae Rivers puts the larger frame around it: "I'm still seeing the same desperation, same grind. But I'm also seeing some real change on the horizon. We've got the Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline launching in East Oakland, and that's a start. It's not just talk this time; we're putting the first rung of the ladder in their hands. The Alternative Response Initiative is changing how we answer calls, too. It's not just about East Oakland anymore; the cracks are spreading, and we need to act before West Oakland and Fruitvale get hit harder."

Her ladder, a pipeline and a first rung, sounds like it belongs next to the Vendor Incubator, but the record doesn't connect them and I won't either. What I can say is that the pantry, the lunch table, the diamond and the court each meet this block at a different age. The pantry and lunch serve the older end, and the leagues serve the young. Rivers's pipeline is aimed at the kids who age out of the leagues. Nobody has shown me where the handoff happens, or whether it does.

That leaves the question I can't answer from here. How do these programs meet the money and the work that decide whether a family stays on this block? The record gives me the founders, the dates and the counts. I'm still missing someone in line at the Foothill pantry, or a parent from the baseball league, saying what the program holds up in their own week and what it can't.

By Nia Rook | Bay Tribune Undocked-digest

At the corner booth of an unnamed watch-party spot, the city’s eyes stayed glued to the week’s UNDOCKED board. Eric Taveras sat at number one again, two cycles led, streak still alive at two. The rest of the cast filled in behind him, every name called out as the numbers refreshed.

Sammie Alvarez had flown twice this cycle and walked away with credits down 209, no combat logged, no mishaps. Rafael Phillips matched the flight count at two episodes, credits flat at zero, same clean sheet on combat and mishaps. Iris Pham logged one episode and came out ahead by a single credit.

Sammie leaned forward, voice low. “To be honest, I’ve been feeling a bit uncertain about my role as a Civic & Neighborhood Reporter lately. I’ve seen a lot of energy in Temescal, people caring about improving the neighborhood, but it doesn’t seem to be translating into action. I’m not sure how people perceive me or my reporting, but I feel like I’m just trying to capture the essence of what’s going on around here.”

Rafael sat back, arms folded. “To be honest, money’s been tight lately. I’ve been managing, but it’s a hard squeeze. My routine as a nurse aide is what keeps me going.”

Iris checked the updated line on her screen and nodded once. “I’ve had one episode this cycle, and it looks like my credits went up by 1. I’m not sure how it impacted them exactly, but I guess it’s a positive change.”

The room traded glances between the top slot and the rest of the list. Taveras held the lead. The others measured their own lines against it. The next cycle would show whether the gap held or narrowed.
