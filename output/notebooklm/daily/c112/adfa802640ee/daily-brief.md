### **Uptown’s Two Speeds: The Block Moves Faster Than the Store**
*By Jordan Velez, Business Desk*

In Uptown, business ledgers show positive growth across both tech firms and storefronts, but a sharp divergence in pace has emerged between corporate offices and neighborhood merchants [1, 2]. On Telegraph Avenue, **Cedric Nakamura** works the counter at **Night Walk Records**, a six-person shop that brought in \$641,000 this cycle—a modest \$933 increase [1]. A few doors down, **Oakland Tech Collective**, a coworking hub led by principal **Priyanka Raman**, carries 62 employees and \$60.7 million in revenue after adding \$154,000 over the same stretch [1, 3]. 

The contrast highlights how foot traffic on the sidewalk translates differently depending on the door people enter [4]:

*   **Cedric Nakamura** (Night Walk Records) observed two distinct crowds moving through Uptown: *"The folks walking past on Telegraph are mostly new — younger, on their phones, heading to the tech offices or the pop-ups. The ones who actually come in are the regulars... The block's moving faster than the store is"* [4].
*   **Brady Lopez** (Dishwasher, Art Walk Cafe) sees the block's momentum from the dish pit of the 30-employee cafe-gallery: *"The lunch rush gets longer, and the coffee machine doesn't stop. But I'm in the back, elbow-deep in suds... It's a good job, steady. It pays the bills"* [2, 5].
*   **Derek Obi** (IT Support Specialist, Oakland Tech Collective) works alongside cybersecurity analyst **Dexter Patel**, writer **Michael Creighton**, and capacity analyst **Sarah Corliss** [3]. Obi reflected on the neighborhood rhythm: *"The tech places bring a certain energy, but the small spots — they're the ones that make the block feel like a neighborhood. You watch them, and you wonder if they feel the same push we do, or if they're just holding on"* [6].

While architecture firm **Ridgeline Studio** (led by principal **Naomi Castellanos**) grew 14.7 percent to \$32.6 million, smaller venues like **Gallery Night Lounge** and Art Walk Cafe saw gains measured in thousands rather than hundreds of thousands, raising questions about long-term merchant stability as local rents adjust [2, 3, 7].

### **Six Names for 142 Sick Residents**
*By Dr. Lila Mezran, Health Desk*

Chinatown carries 142 sick residents—representing 6.5 percent of the neighborhood's population and the highest illness count in Oakland [8]. Yet, the hospital census lists six beds occupied in Chinatown without a single local resident tracked by name on the public care record [9].

Reporting on the ground reveals how Chinatown families absorb illness behind closed doors [10]:

*   **Matra Woven** (Chinatown Custodial Staff) described how neighbors quietly step in when family members fall ill: *"You notice when Mrs. Chen's grandson is the one walking her dog, or when the usual guy at the corner store has his daughter running the register... family steps in, neighbors help out, and we don't always ask why"* [8, 10].
*   **Yu Zen** (Rockridge Commercial Insurance Broker) reflected from a hospital bed following a workplace accident on the systemic gap in visibility: *"The names on the forms aren't the same as the names on the beds. Chinatown's been hit so hard... It's just that nobody's writing their names down the same way. That gap isn't about who's sicker. It's about who gets seen"* [11, 12].
*   **Deacon Seymour** (Lake Merritt Manager, Oakland A's) shared a similar perspective from his own hospital stay: *"There are a lot more [beds] filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else... and that weighs on me"* [11, 13].
*   **Kenji Tanaka** (Senior Pastor, Chinatown) emphasized that official counts miss those who avoid asking for help: *"The city counts beds and cases. I count the people who are too proud, too scared, or too tired to be counted"* [14].

While external patients like **Maurice Franklin** (Rockridge) are named while recovering from severe seasonal flu, Chinatown's care trail remains largely unmapped by official rosters [11, 15].

### **At 19th Street, the Busiest Station Runs on the Slow Side of the Clock**
*By Trevor Shimizu, Transit Desk*

Handling 14,789 passengers this cycle, **19th St Oakland** stood as the busiest station across the transit line while recording an 83 percent on-time performance [16]. Station staff and drivers emphasize that high passenger volumes naturally create schedule friction [17, 18]:

*   **Eleanor Rivera** (Station Manager, 19th St) explained from the booth how heavy event crowds and game-day transfers lengthen station dwell times: *"You see the platform fill, and you know the trains are coming, but they're coming full... it's dwell time, sure — people squeezing on, people stepping back, doors cycling. But it's also crowd control... We're moving a lot of people, but moving a lot of people takes time"* [17].
*   **Evan Lewis** (Route 18 Bus Driver) noted from street level that high traffic is the inherent tradeoff of a central hub: *"When I pull up alongside that station, I see the crowds waiting and the trains crawling in late... that's the price of being the busiest... You can't move that many bodies and keep a perfect clock"* [18].
