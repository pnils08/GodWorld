Colin Soria moved through the end of his rounds the way someone does when the cold has stopped being news and started being company. Four days of fog following a head cold that dug in behind his eyes and refused to leave. By the time the overcast settled over Temescal on Cycle 109, the sky had flattened into something like white sheet metal, and the temperature — 49 degrees, still, no wind to speak of — felt less like weather and more like an argument the city had decided to stop having with itself.

He paused at the corner, hands shoved into the pockets of his work jacket, and watched a delivery truck idle long enough for the exhaust to pool against the pavement before the driver pulled away. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would make a person change their plans outright. Just the cumulative weight of gray stacking on gray.

“You know, it’s not the kind of weather that stops you in your tracks,” Soria said, “just that dull, gray blanket that makes everything feel a little slower. I’ve noticed it in the way people move, like we’re all carrying an extra weight we can’t quite name. Even my usual rounds feel heavier, like the cold’s seeped into my bones and decided to stay. It’s not bad, exactly, just… there. Makes you want to hunker down, keep to yourself a little more than usual.”

Down the block, in a storefront bakery where the windows had fogged from the inside, Melton Neilon was working dough that was taking its time. He’d noticed it too — the way the yeast behaved differently under a lid of cloud that had now held for the fourth straight cycle. Before the overcast, there had been fog: Cycle 106 at 52 degrees, then 44, then 46, each one a little deeper, a little more insistent. Neilon’s regulars had been arriving later, speaking less, lingering shorter.

“You know, when it’s this kind of flat gray for days, I notice my regulars come in quieter, like they’re carrying the weather inside with them,” he said. “Even the dough feels slower to rise — not bad, just… deliberate, like it’s thinking harder about whether it wants to wake up today.”

A few doors over, Elio Perez sat at a window table in a coffee shop, both hands wrapped around a mug he had been nursing for the better part of an hour. Outside, the street was occupied but unhurried — pedestrians in puffer coats, collars turned up, nobody stopping to chat. Perez wasn’t gloomy. That wasn’t the word. Neutral, he called it. The kind of day that made a person decide to keep things simple.

“The overcast days make everything feel slower, like the world is holding its breath,” he said. “It’s not gloomy, just… neutral, and it makes me want to stay inside, keep things simple, and not push too hard.”

No single event in the record forced these decisions. No storm, no advisory. Just a streak — four cycles of low sky, comfort barely registering, and a city making small, invisible choices: to go out later, to leave earlier, to not go out at all. The threshold between weather that’s not quite bad enough to keep a person inside and weather that’s not quite good enough to draw them out is posted on no sign, and it is not the same for everyone. What determines it, cycle after cycle like this one, is still an open question.
