# Chinatown's Count Slips to 135, and the Ledger Still Has No Name for It

Akua Adeyemi, a Chinatown plumber, has learned to carry her wrench like a sleeping person is in the next room. Often one is. She sets the tool down without ringing it on the tile. She runs the tap low and checks the pipes before she opens a valve. She does this because, in her words, a house tells you.

"I go into a lot of homes, and you can tell pretty quick when someone's down," Adeyemi said. "It's not the mess or the dishes — it's the quiet. The TV off in the middle of the day. A chair pulled up by the window where somebody's been sitting a long time. Sometimes the medicine gets moved to the kitchen counter so it's right there where someone'll remember it. And you learn to work quiet, not to bang pipes, because somebody's sleeping in the next room. I see more of that in Chinatown than anywhere else I work. People here take care of their own — you'll see a neighbor bring soup, or a cousin come by after their shift. It's not always the hospital that carries the load. It's the family that rearranges the whole house around one sick person."

The Neighborhood_Demographics sheet puts the number behind that observation at 135 sick residents in Chinatown for Cycle 115. The sheet counts 2,205 residents, which makes 6.1 percent, at or over the 6 percent watch bar. Chinatown carries more sick residents than any other neighborhood, as it has in each of this paper's recent reports. East Oakland is next at 117, then Ivy Hill at 115, Fruitvale at 109, and Laurel and San Antonio at 107 each. Across all 22 neighborhoods the sheet counts 1,771.

Chinatown's figure is down two from Cycle 114, when this paper reported 137. That in turn was down from the 140 and 142 the paper reported in Cycles 113 and 112. The count has eased by small steps for four cycles running. A slow decline is still 135 people, and the figure does not say who they are or how sick they are. It also does not say whether the watch bar will be cleared next cycle. Laurel is the only one of the eight neighborhoods listed that moved up this cycle, by one.

## What the care trail shows

The Care_Justice_Census hospital row for Chinatown reads in order. There are 135 sick residents and 3 admissions. At the close of the cycle, 6 were in care and 6 beds were occupied. None of the six is tracked by name. All six are other residents.

Citywide the same census shows 34 admissions this cycle, none of them tracked, and 69 in care at the close with 69 beds occupied. Thirty-five people left. The record does not say whether Chinatown's six are counted within the 135, and this report does not guess.

The named rows sit elsewhere. Yu Zen, of Rockridge, was admitted in Cycle 105 after a workplace accident and remains in care. So does Deacon Seymour, of Lake Merritt, admitted the same cycle for the same reason. Maurice Franklin, also of Rockridge, was admitted in Cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu. The cycle summary's one recovery entry is Howard Travis, on Piedmont Ave, who caught up on sleep and woke up feeling like himself again. None of the four lives in Chinatown, and nothing on the record says anyone there is sick because of a named cause.

Seymour has spoken to this paper before about the gap between names and beds. This cycle he put it more bluntly.

"I spent my own stretch in a hospital bed not long ago, and I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you," Seymour said. "But I also know that bed I was in—that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city, and if the ledger doesn't show them, that doesn't mean they're not there. It means nobody's counting. And when nobody's counting, people get missed. That's the part that keeps me up at night."

## What the record cannot yet say

The ledger does count Chinatown's sick, but it counts them as a number. It does not say how many of the 135 are being cared for at home. It does not say how many have seen a clinician this cycle, or what the neighborhood's six occupied beds are being used for. It does not say who covers the shift when a family member stays home, or whether the neighbor with the soup is the only visitor some households get. None of that is on the record, and none of it can be taken from the numbers.

Adeyemi's account is one plumber's view of the houses she works in. It is not a survey. But her description is of care happening in kitchens, and the hospital rows are built to count beds. For the 135, those two systems may not overlap much. Which office tracks the care that never reaches a bed, and whether any record of it exists, is the question this reporting could not answer. Until someone answers it, the next Chinatown figure will arrive as a number again, down or up by a handful, with no name attached.
