# Who Owns KONO's Rent Pressure When Everyone on Telegraph Says Business Is Good?

By Jax Caldera, The Bay Tribune

Priya Raghunathan was feeding quarters into a dryer on Telegraph when I asked her about decay. She dispatches rail for BART and lives in KONO. She looked at me like I'd asked about a plague.

"I've noticed that KONO is getting busier. Retail is busy and events are drawing crowds. We're also getting new households moving into the neighborhood."

Dmitri Soto runs a container yard, lives in KONO, and was folding towels two machines down. He heard the question and didn't wait for me to finish. "I haven't really seen any decay around here. KONO's been bustling, to be honest. Retail's busy, events are drawing crowds, and we're getting new households moving in. It's all pretty positive from where I'm standing."

They're right, and the neighborhood map backs them. The row for KONO reads retail vitality 9.74 and event attractiveness 10.75. Its migration flow is labeled "Inflow surge," and its trajectory is labeled "growth."

The audit I was handed for this cycle flags KONO as **decay**, and it has two reasons. Sentiment is -0.02, which ranks 12th of 22 neighborhoods, against a city median of -0.015. Housing pressure is 0.5, which ranks 14th of 22, against a city median of zero.

So here's the fight. The street says busy. The map says growth. The audit says decay. All three describe the same blocks.

My read, and it's inference, is that they don't contradict each other the way they look like they do. Look at what Priya and Dmitri are celebrating: new households moving in. Then look at the housing pressure number. The map has the median rent in KONO at $2,062. A neighborhood can be loud, full and bleeding at once. Busy retail doesn't tell you who gets to stay.

The audit's own finding is the part that burns. No active initiative, and no mitigator, owns this break on the record. I checked the running list of what the city says it's working on. Stabilization is paying out in West Oakland. A transit hub is in design in Fruitvale. Grand Lake has a commercial vote ready. KONO's name isn't on any of it.

Compare that to what the paper said before. In C96 Luis Navarro reported KONO went three cycles on generators and candlelight while City Hall filed it as a utility ticket. In C102 the Tribune's retail beat described a retail collapse along Telegraph between 23rd and 27th. Now the map shows retail at 9.74, with crowds and an inflow surge. That's a long way from shuttered bays. Somebody should be able to tell me what changed, who changed it, and whether the people who lived through the dark years are the same people now being counted as "new households."

I can't answer that. The prior comparison the audit needs to validate its own direction isn't in my hands. Nobody at any office has answered the mismatch, either.

I also called a waiter who works in San Antonio, not KONO. Dank Abara is the only person I reached who said the word out loud. "I've heard people talking about KONO neighborhood decay, and to be honest, it's something that's on my mind," he said. "I just know that around here, people are talking about it."

He admits he doesn't know the specifics. Neither do I. But when a neighborhood with a "growth" label gets talked about as decaying by people one neighborhood over, that's a rumor with a reason behind it. The rumor is a rent number nobody is watching.

Priya and Dmitri aren't wrong, and I'm not here to take their good news. I'm here to point out that their good news is the housing pressure. No office on the record has said it owns that.

So who answers for it? Does Councilmember Warren Ashford (D7, CRC), whose district includes KONO on the map, know KONO has a decay flag and no initiative behind it? And if Mayor Santana's office has a plan for this corridor, why is it the one thing in KONO nobody is advertising?
