Three clear days in a row, and you could hear the city exhale. Noah Tan walked the waterfront and found Elle Lilo back on the Jack London bridge, the cough gone, saying the harbor felt like it was breathing again and so was she. The hospital holds six of her neighbors. The rest are at home, with a daughter on the bus from Fruitvale to cook and a nephew late to work because he's picking up medicine. The city's records call the cluster low-severity. As Mezran notes, that means the record doesn't call it an emergency. It doesn't say the households are fine.

Then there is Baylight, where two records of the same project refuse to agree. Luis Navarro laid them side by side. Marcus Webb, who runs a program at OEWD and lives Downtown, feels the stall in the applications on his desk. "I want the council to finish the job," he told Navarro. I can't tell him which record is right. I can tell him that when the city's own books describe one project as both finished and frozen, someone owes residents an explanation, and so far Leonard Tran's statement that progress is "a crucial step forward" isn't one.

Housing runs under everything this week. Maria Keen's Fruitvale piece sets Lucia Polito's gratitude for the Postpartum Oakland Network beside Rick Walker's plain accounting. Walker teaches K-8, sees the tutoring posters at St. Esperanza and the loud mercado, and says the rent went up again. THE WEEK'S REPORTING

By Noah Tan | Bay Tribune Civic

Elle Lilo stepped back onto the bridge at Jack London, the first time in weeks her boots had met the metal grating without the weight of a lingering cough in her chest. The tugs moved below as they always did, horns cutting across the water, but the air felt different with the cranes sharp against the sky and gulls wheeling without the usual gray press.

"Three clear days in a row — that's a gift around here," she said. "When I finally shook that cold, the first morning I stepped back on the bridge, the sky was wide open, and I swear I could feel it in my chest. The work doesn't change, not really — the tugs still pull, the horns still sound — but the day feels lighter. You can see the whole bay, the cranes, the gulls, all of it. It's like the harbor is breathing again, and so am I."

A few miles north, Cee Lee moved along the Lake Merritt path at a steadier pace than the cold stretch had allowed. The water lay flat under the light, and the usual loop no longer pulled at her the way it had when the fog sat low.

"Three clear days helps, I won't lie," she said. "The cold stretch had me moving slower than I like—that kind of tired that sits in your bones. But 'lifted'? I'd say paused. Seasonal stuff like this, it lingers in the background. I'm out walking the lake path again, getting back to normal, but I'm not foolish enough to think it's gone for good. You learn to live with the ebb and flow of it."

In Rockridge, Johhny Necklar took the short walk to the diner without the extra layers that had slowed him through the earlier cycles. He still paused twice on the way, once on the corner and once near the bench, the sun on his back a small mercy after the weeks inside.

"The clear days help, I won't lie," he said. "I was laid up a good while this winter, and there's something about stepping out and not having to bundle up just to get the mail. But recovery isn't on a weather schedule. I take the walk to the diner now because the sun feels good on my back, but I still stop twice on the way. My body tells me when it's ready, not the sky."

Rafael Brannie stayed on Piedmont Ave, moving between jobs with the same measured steps he had kept through the cold. He noticed the light on the sidewalk but did not linger long enough to call anyone.

"Three clear days helps, sure," he said. "I noticed the light today walking to the job—it's different, makes you want to linger on the corner a moment longer. But the winter mood, that's a heavier coat than the weather. It kind of stays draped on you even when the sun's out. I haven't gone out of my way to see people yet. I'm still moving, working, that part doesn't stop. But sitting with someone, sharing a beer, that takes a little more than blue sky. The mood outlasts the weather, I think. The sun gets you halfway there, the rest is on you."

Kwame Fitzgerald and Omar Amici kept to the circles that had carried them through the colder cycles, their days shaped more by the familiar rooms than by the open sidewalk. The third clear morning brought the same question to each of them: what the light had actually changed, and what it had only set aside until the next turn of the weather.

# Three Programs, One Rent Bill: Fruitvale Counts What Holds

The Postpartum Oakland Network has been on Fruitvale's record since Cycle 101. Lucia Polito founded it, and it is one of three programs the block now has on the books. The other two came in the same cycle: the Fruitvale Summer Speed Series, which is youth sports, and Fruitvale Transit Corridor Mutual Aid. Three programs, one starting line, and twelve cycles of living on the block since.

I won't pretend to have stood in their rooms this cycle. What I have are the people who talk about them, and the numbers the block produced while they talked.

Polito speaks about the Network the way a founder would. "I've seen the impact that community programs like the Postpartum Oakland Network, which I founded, can have on people's lives," she said. "It's amazing to see the difference these programs make in Fruitvale. As someone who's dedicated to wellness and has been a part of this community for a while, I feel grateful to see the positive changes happening around us."

Gratitude is a real thing to hold, and it belongs on the record. A health program for new parents is one of the few things a neighborhood builds that is meant to be there at three in the morning.

Rick Walker teaches K-8 and used to work the line in a kitchen. He lives close to the other side of the same sentence. "To be honest, it's getting harder to feel that way," he said. "Fruitvale's still got its issues, but I've seen some new posters around, like the expanded tutoring program at St. Esperanza Parish. It's something, you know? The mercado's still buzzing, people are still grabbing lunch between train transfers. It's not like nothing's happening. But, yeah, the rent went up again, so it's still a struggle. I don't know if it's enough, though."

I keep turning that over. The posters are up and the mercado is loud. The people grabbing lunch between train transfers are a corridor of exactly the kind Transit Corridor Mutual Aid is named for. Then the rent goes up, and everything else has to be measured against it.

Gregory Mims is on the ledger as organized crime, and he does not oversell. "Fruitvale's still a ways off for a lot of folks around here, you know?" he said. "I mean, that kid's cousin getting placed with a plumbing crew is great and all, but it's not like that's the norm. I've seen too many programs come and go. You hear about success stories, but they're not always the ones that stick. I'm still a little skeptical."

Mims's skepticism has a long memory behind it, and that is why it's worth printing. Programs on this block have come and gone before. These three have lasted since Cycle 101, which is a start and not a verdict.

Ramon Vega has his own reasons for weighing in. "To be honest, that council fight still lingers in my mind," he said. "I understand why Christopher felt I was leaning too far into the developer's corner on the Fruitvale hub. At the time, I believed it was a necessary compromise to move the project forward, but looking back, I can see how it might have appeared otherwise. The Fruitvale community programs are crucial, and I still stand by the importance of supporting them. However, I acknowledge that the approach we took was contentious, and it's something I've reflected on since then."

That is a man saying the hub fight left a mark, and that the programs are what he says he still stands behind.

Here is what the block itself added up to this cycle. Fruitvale counts 423 students, 1,559 adults and 351 seniors. Against Cycle 112 that is two more students, seven more adults and one more senior. The block is barely moving, a few more bodies. Whoever is arriving is arriving into the same rent Rick Walker described.

The hospital census runs along the same slow line. It shows 114 sick residents, two admissions, and four people in care at the close, filling four beds. The judicial census shows six charges, one arrest, and three people in custody at the close. None of the seven are tracked citizens. They are residents of this block whose names the record doesn't carry.

I can't tell you whether any of them was at a Postpartum Oakland Network table, or on a Speed Series sideline, or leaning on the mutual aid that runs along the train corridor. The record doesn't connect those lines, and I won't draw one.

What I still need is the person on the other end. I want a parent who used the Network and can say what changed. I want a kid from the Speed Series. I want someone who took help from the corridor program the week the rent went up, and who can say whether the help covered it. Polito feels the difference and Walker doesn't know if it's enough. Only the people who took the help can say which of them is right.

# Morton's 285 At-Bats Are the Evidence the Outfield Math Has to Start From

Desmond Morton has 285 at-bats this season and is hitting .291, according to the feed line, with 7 home runs and 44 RBI. His TrueSource card adds the rest of the year: 80 games, 83 hits, 23 walks, 49 strikeouts, 28 stolen bases, a .348 on-base percentage and a .498 slugging percentage. It also works out one home run every 40.7 at-bats and one strikeout every 5.8. That is a left fielder with a full season behind him. Next season's outfield squeeze starts from his line, whichever player the club ends up favoring.

The squeeze is a rumor, and the feed prints it as an off-season item: five outfielders for three positions. It names Morton in left, Frank Reyna in right, Danny Horn in center, Vladimir Gonzalez in right and JR Rosado in left. The feed prints season lines for Morton, Reyna and Rosado only, so Horn and Gonzalez enter this piece as names without numbers. Nothing on the page supports a claim about their production or their health.

## The two lines that disagree

Reyna's feed line is 164 at-bats, .317, 14 home runs and 34 RBI. On the surface it beats Morton's in average and in power. His TrueSource card works out one home run every 11.7 at-bats against Morton's one every 40.7, and it prints a .683 slugging percentage against Morton's .498. If the question were who hit the ball harder in the innings each has played, Reyna would win it.

But the card also shows the denominators. Reyna's 164 at-bats came over 46 games; Morton's 285 came over 80. Reyna has 6 walks and 18 strikeouts, with a .339 on-base percentage. His card works out one strikeout every 9.1 at-bats, a better contact rate than Morton's 5.8. The line is clean, but it is thin, and a 14-homer spike in 164 at-bats is the kind of number that does not necessarily hold when the sample doubles. This is the standard small-sample caution: stable signals need volume, and Reyna has not yet supplied it. It is a statement about the sample, not about the player.

Morton's separation is different in kind. His 28 stolen bases are the one number in this group that neither Reyna (7) nor Rosado (2) comes close to on his card. His .348 on-base percentage is higher than either of theirs, and it comes with more at-bats behind it. The power is moderate. The profile is a corner outfielder who gets on base and moves, over a season-sized sample, with enough slug to keep pitchers honest. The feed does not print a pace or a projection for his home runs, so how much of that power carries over is a question the record does not answer.

## Rosado is the redundancy

JR Rosado's line is 184 at-bats, .298, 7 home runs and 27 RBI, with 57 hits, 12 walks and 23 strikeouts. His card works out one home run every 26.3 at-bats and one strikeout every 8.0. It is a good line, and it sits between the other two on nearly every rate. The feed lists him in left field, the same position as Morton.

That is the board problem. Two left fielders with nearly the same average, .298 against .291, and the same 7 home runs, but different volume and different games played. Rosado's 184 at-bats are fewer than Morton's, but he has 54 games to Reyna's 46. All three hit above .290. Nobody in this group is failing, and that is why the squeeze is hard. A roster built on three hitters at that level has no obvious weak starter to remove, so someone good ends up on the bench or on the trade market.

## What the card supports

The cleanest reading is narrow. Morton's line is the most reliable of the three, because the sample behind it is the largest and it covers the most ground: average, on-base percentage, slugging and speed. Reyna's line is the loudest, and the sample has not caught up to the power. Rosado's line is solid, and it overlaps with Morton's position.

None of that decides who plays right field in the spring. The feed does not say how the club views any of these lines, and it does not say what Horn or Gonzalez would bring to the same decision. What would test the claim is more volume on Reyna's side: whether the 14 home runs and the .317 survive a second hundred or so at-bats, and whether a 6-walk plate approach holds up as pitchers adjust. On Morton's side the test is whether 7 home runs in 285 at-bats is his ceiling or his floor. Until then, the question the front office faces is Mike Paulson's: does he trust the player who proved he can play every day, or the one who proved he can hit when the matchup favors him?

# Chinatown's 140 Sick Are Mostly at Home, and Home Is Doing the Work

Marisio Curazo keeps her Chinatown list in the order she calls the families, not alphabetically. A home health aide goes first, then a prescription delivery, then the households where a call is the whole job. Curazo is an elder care coordinator. This cycle the neighborhood's sick count is 140, and on her list that is 140 people with someone standing next to them.

"When I see a number like 140 sick in Chinatown, I don't see a number — I see the families I work with every day," she said. "Most of them aren't going to the hospital. They're staying home because that's what we do. The daughter takes the bus from Fruitvale to cook. The nephew calls in late to pick up prescriptions. I'm the one who helps figure out who needs a home health aide, who needs their meds delivered, who just needs someone to check in. The hospital has six of our folks right now — the ones who really couldn't stay home. The rest of us are doing what we've always done: taking care of each other, block by block, door by door."

**The count behind the calls**

The neighborhood sheet for this cycle puts Chinatown at 140 sick residents out of 2,201. That is 6.4%, at or over the 6% watch bar. It is the highest count of any neighborhood. The next highest are East Oakland at 121 and Ivy Hill at 120. Each of the eight highest neighborhoods fell by one or two from last cycle, and Chinatown fell by two. The week's change is small, and the weight on the block is not.

The hospital census traces the same neighborhood from illness to beds. Chinatown had 3 admissions this cycle and 6 residents in care at the close, occupying 6 beds. The census lists none of the six as tracked, so they are residents the city counts but does not follow by name. By comparison, Adams Point, Dimond, Downtown and East Oakland each have 4 in care. Citywide, the hospital took 35 admissions, held 71 in care at the close in 71 beds, and discharged 37.

The world record also carries a recorded health cluster for Chinatown, rated low-severity. In clinic language, "low-severity" means the record does not call it an emergency. It also does not say the households are fine.

**What the ride sees**

Omarion Farah drives a cab in Chinatown and hears about the sickness secondhand, in what people talk about and what they skip. "People talk in the cab, but not about hospitals much," he said. "They're more worried about who's going to cover a shift or pick up the kids. I see folks staying home, taking care of each other. The ones I hear about going to the hospital are the ones who really can't avoid it. It's a lot of home care, a lot of family stepping in."

The ledger does not report who covers the shifts. Farah's account is a driver's view from the front seat, not a count. The record cannot show whether the families he describes are among Curazo's 140.

**A bed on the other side of the arithmetic**

Yu Zen knows the six-bed side of that math from a different neighborhood. He is a commercial insurance broker who lives in Rockridge. The hospital record lists him as in care, active, admitted in Cycle 105 after a workplace accident. The Chinatown census shows no tracked resident among its six, so his bed is not counted in the neighborhood's six. He speaks about Chinatown as someone who has spent time in a hospital bed himself.

"When I was in that hospital bed after my accident, I kept thinking about the numbers I deal with every day as an insurance broker — the claims, the paperwork, the names on the forms," he said. "But lying there, I realized the names on the forms aren't the same as the names on the beds. Chinatown's been hit so hard, and I know it's not because those folks are any less careful or any less strong. It's that when illness clusters, it lands on households where someone's already stretched thin — caring for a parent, watching the grandkids, working two jobs. The six of us in hospital care, we've got nurses and monitors and someone bringing us meals. The other 134 — they've got a cousin or an auntie or a neighbor who's doing all that and still trying to keep the shop open. That's who's caring for them. And that's the part that keeps me up at night, because I know that kind of care doesn't show up on any ledger."

The 134 is his subtraction, 140 minus 6. The records do not say whether the six in Chinatown beds are inside the 140. Nothing in the record explains the cluster, and this report does not either. Zen's reading of why illness lands where it does is his opinion, not a finding.

# Baylight District: The Vote Is Recorded. So What Is Still Waiting?

Haul routes at the Baylight District have dust control running on them this cycle. A 48-hour construction hotline is live. Phase II remediation is proceeding with independent oversight. That is the Baylight line on the city's tracker for Cycle 113.

Marcus Webb, an OEWD Program Director who lives Downtown, describes the same project as stuck.

"Three cycles is a long time for something this big to sit in the same spot," Webb told me. "For me, the accountability part is that the Baylight District is supposed to be the project that ties the neighborhood together — jobs, housing, the works — and when it stalls, I feel it in the applications on my desk. People are waiting on decisions that ripple out from that one vote. So what I want? I want the council to finish the job. Not another study, not another workshop — a final vote. And I want to know that when it happens, the construction pace we've already started doesn't just keep spinning its wheels. That's what I'm watching for."

Two records on the same project don't line up. Here is where I looked and what each returned.

## What the record establishes

The engine's own audit flags Baylight District — Final Council Vote as stalled in construction-active. It also says the civic initiative carrying Downtown has not advanced in three cycles.

I pulled the Initiative_Tracker row for Baylight. It lists the $2.1 billion project as "passed." The vote was Cycle 83, 6–3. The yes votes were Denise Carter, Rose Delgado, Janae Rivers, Terrence Mobley, Ramon Vega and Leonard Tran. The no votes were Elliott Crane, Warren Ashford and Nina Chen. The mayor's action is recorded as signed.

The milestone notes run in sequence:

- C107: construction contracts executed.
- C109: Phase II remediation commenced.
- C110: soil test verification complete.
- C111 and C112: a construction milestone review is held for Cycle 115.
- C113: remediation on schedule, with the hotline, the dust control and a Jack London workforce coordination meeting set before that review.

So the row names no pending final vote. What it names as next is a construction milestone review in Cycle 115.

Leonard Tran, the council office holder on the record, put it this way in his Cycle 113 statement: "The progress in Baylight District remediation is a crucial step forward."

## What it does not establish

The "three cycles without advancing" reading fits the tracker's notes. Cycles 111, 112 and 113 all point at the same Cycle 115 review. The notes don't say what Webb means by "a final vote," and they don't say what kind of decision his applicants are waiting on.

My search of the paper's files for the Baylight final vote returned working drafts and staged packets. It returned no published piece that explains the gap, so I can't point you to one.

I have no record that connects Webb's pending applications to a specific Baylight decision. I have no record of who, if anyone, owes those applicants an answer. I have no request, response or deadline on file from any office.

The word "stalled" comes from the audit. The tracker says "on schedule." Both are on the record, and neither explains the other.

## The question for the people who can answer

Is there a decision still pending between a vote passed in Cycle 83 and a review set for Cycle 115? If there is, the tracker doesn't show it. If there isn't, then what Webb is watching for may be a different thing from what the council thinks it finished.

I'd ask the council office and OEWD to say, on the record, which decision sits ahead of those applications.

Until then, the work crews are on the haul routes and the hotline is live. Someone Downtown is looking at an application on a desk. If the vote was finished in Cycle 83, what is that person waiting for?
