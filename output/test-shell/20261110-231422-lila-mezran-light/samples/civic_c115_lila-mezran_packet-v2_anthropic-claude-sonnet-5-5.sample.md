# Chinatown Has the Most Sick Residents in the City. The Hospital Record Names None of Them.

Akua Adeyemi works quietly in Chinatown. She is a plumber and lives in the neighborhood. When a house has someone ill in it, she tries not to bang the pipes. "You learn to work quiet," she said, "because somebody's sleeping in the next room."

She is not reading a ledger when she says this. She is reading kitchens. "I go into a lot of homes, and you can tell pretty quick when someone's down," Adeyemi said. "It's not the mess or the dishes — it's the quiet. The TV off in the middle of the day. A chair pulled up by the window where somebody's been sitting a long time. Sometimes the medicine gets moved to the kitchen counter so it's right there where someone'll remember it."

Then she names the neighborhood. "I see more of that in Chinatown than anywhere else I work. People here take care of their own — you'll see a neighbor bring soup, or a cousin come by after their shift. It's not always the hospital that carries the load. It's the family that rearranges the whole house around one sick person."

## What the rows say

The neighborhood demographics sheet counts 135 sick residents in Chinatown at cycle 115. That is out of 2,205 residents: 314 students, 1,496 adults and 395 seniors. It works out to 6.1 percent, at or over the city's 6 percent watch bar. It is the highest count of any neighborhood. East Oakland follows at 117, Ivy Hill at 115, Fruitvale at 109, and Laurel and San Antonio at 107 each. The sheet totals 1,771 sick residents across 22 neighborhoods.

Chinatown's count is down 2 from cycle 114. East Oakland is down 2, Ivy Hill down 3, Fruitvale down 3. Laurel is the only one of the leading neighborhoods that rose, by 1. Chinatown is still first, and the small drop does not change that.

The hospital census gives the next step. Chinatown had 3 admissions this cycle. At the close, 6 Chinatown residents were in care and 6 beds were occupied. The census tracks none of the six by name. Of the 135, the record puts six in a bed.

That is not a count of how many of the 135 needed one. The record does not say, and this column will not guess. It also does not say where the other residents are or who is looking after them. Adeyemi's account is one plumber's view from inside the houses she works in. It is not a finding.

Other neighborhoods show the same shape on a smaller scale. Adams Point has 82 sick residents, 2 admissions and 4 beds occupied. Dimond has 90 sick, 2 admissions and 4 beds. East Oakland has 117 sick, 2 admissions and 4 beds. Eastlake has 86 sick, 2 admissions and 4 beds. In none of them does the census name a tracked resident. Citywide, there were 34 admissions this cycle, none of them tracked residents. 69 people were in care at the close, in 69 beds, and 35 left.

## The names on the record

Four residents are named on the hospital and cause records this cycle, and none lives in Chinatown. Yu Zen of Rockridge was admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident and is listed as in hospital care. Deacon Seymour of Lake Merritt was admitted the same cycle, also after a workplace accident, and is also in care. Maurice Franklin of Rockridge was admitted in cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu.

The city's recovery column has one entry. Howard Travis, on Piedmont Ave, caught up on sleep and woke up feeling like himself again.

Seymour knows what it means to be on the ledger. "I spent my own stretch in a hospital bed not long ago, and I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you," he said. "But I also know that bed I was in — that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city, and if the ledger doesn't show them, that doesn't mean they're not there. It means nobody's counting. And when nobody's counting, people get missed. That's the part that keeps me up at night."

## What the record cannot yet say

A named row is how a hospital system checks on someone. It is how a bed gets a name on the door, and how a follow-up gets scheduled. An unnamed row is a number that moves up or down by two or three each cycle.

Chinatown's number has led the city, over the watch bar, and still no one from the neighborhood appears by name on the hospital ledger. The record does not say what that means. It does not say whether the six residents in beds are being followed. It does not say what care the other 129 or so are getting, or from whom. It also does not say whether anything is planned beyond the family and neighbors Adeyemi describes.

So the question stays open, and it is for the city's health offices to answer. What would it take for Chinatown's sick to be seen, counted and cared for by name?
