# The Weather That Does Not Stop Anyone

Colin Soria kept making his usual rounds in Temescal while a head cold lingered through the week. The overcast did not give him a reason to stop; it changed the effort required to continue.

“You know, it's not the kind of weather that stops you in your tracks—just that dull, gray blanket that makes everything feel a little slower,” Soria said. “I've noticed it in the way people move, like we're all carrying an extra weight we can't quite name. Even my usual rounds feel heavier, like the cold's seeped into my bones and decided to stay. It's not bad, exactly, just... there. Makes you want to hunker down, keep to yourself a little more than usual.”

That is the useful distinction in this stretch of weather. The city is not being forced indoors by a named hazard. The pressure is quieter: a task still gets done, but the body negotiates with it first.

The pattern has held without becoming dramatic. Overcast gave way to fog before returning to overcast after fog. The sequence offers no clean dividing line between a day to go out and a day to stay in. It produces a series of small decisions.

Melton Neilon described those decisions through the people who return to him.

“You know, when it's this kind of flat gray for days, I notice my regulars come in quieter, like they're carrying the weather inside with them,” Neilon said. “Even the dough feels slower to rise—not bad, just... deliberate, like it's thinking harder about whether it wants to wake up today.”

The wording matters: not bad. Deliberate. A routine can continue and still feel altered. The weather works less like a barrier than an added layer between intention and motion.

Elio Perez put the same condition more plainly: “The overcast days make everything feel slower, like the world is holding its breath. It’s not gloomy, just... neutral, and it makes me want to stay inside, keep things simple, and not push too hard.”

For Soria, the question is not whether the day is severe enough to cancel a round. It is whether the accumulated gray has made the round heavier than it looked when he began. For Neilon, it is whether the regulars arrive carrying that same hesitation. For Perez, it is whether going out still feels necessary when staying in does not feel like a loss.

After the fog, the record calls the mood neutral. The people living it describe something more specific: a city continuing at a lower setting, with no obvious moment when a routine becomes too difficult to continue. Who decides where that line is?
