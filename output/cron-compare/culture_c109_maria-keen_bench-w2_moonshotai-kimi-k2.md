# The Weight of Starting Something

**By Maria Keen**

Lucia Polito will tell you she didn't set out to build infrastructure. She set out to answer a phone call from someone who needed to be heard. That was eight cycles ago, when Postpartum Oakland Network started in Fruitvale—not in a clinic, not in a funded space, but in the accumulated patience of someone who understood that wellness doesn't always wear a white coat.

Polito works as an Aura Wellness Practitioner, a title that barely contains what she actually does, which is to remain available. The network she founded sits alongside two other programs that have held this neighborhood since C101: the Fruitvale Summer Speed Series, which gets kids moving, and the Fruitvale Transit Corridor Mutual Aid, which moves everything else—groceries, information, the small currencies of getting by. Three programs. Same eight cycles. In a city where initiatives often flare and gutter, that duration means something.

"I've seen the impact that community programs can have on people's lives, and I feel grateful to be a part of it," Polito told me. "As someone who's founded a program myself, I understand the passion and dedication that goes into creating something that truly helps others. It's amazing to see the difference these programs make in Fruitvale, and I'm always looking for ways to support and connect with the people who make them happen."

The words have the worn texture of someone who has repeated them to funders, to newcomers, to herself. But there's something else underneath: the recognition that founding is lonely, that "support and connect" is a mission statement that doubles as a prayer.

The neighborhood around her is shifting. Since C107, Fruitvale has added fifty-eight residents—eleven students, thirty-nine adults, eight seniors. The numbers arrive clean from the ledger, but on the ground they mean new faces at the corner store, different questions at the bus stop, the particular silence of someone deciding whether to stay. Rick Walker, who watches these programs from nearby, put it plainly: "I've seen the community programs in Fruitvale really making a difference, and I think it's great that people like Lucia Polito are founding initiatives that help our neighborhood."

Rafael Pilgrim echoed the sentiment with a slight variation, as if the neighborhood were a song everyone is learning the words to: "I've seen the community programs in Fruitvale really make a difference, and I appreciate the work of founders like Lucia Polito who are dedicated to helping our neighborhood thrive."

The word *thrive* does a lot of work. It suggests growth, health, forward motion. But I keep returning to what isn't in these statements—what nobody is quite saying. The programs exist. They persist. Yet the question of who arrives to use them, and who arrives to replace the people who built them, remains open.

Tomas Renteria, who has watched from Jack London, caught something specific in the current: "I've seen the community programs in Fruitvale really making a difference, and I feel like they're bringing people together. I've heard Lucia Polito's work with the Postpartum Oakland Network is really helping new moms in the area."

*I've heard.* The phrase travels. Reputation outpaces attendance. In a neighborhood adding nearly forty working-age adults per cycle, the programs become both more necessary and more stretched—known by name, perhaps, but not always known by touch.

This is the proud and wary truth of Fruitvale: three programs, one founder still present, fifty-eight new neighbors learning the shape of the place. The infrastructure holds. What it holds *for*—whether the new arrivals will become the next founders, or merely consumers of someone else's patience—has not yet been decided.
