# One More Backpack in Rockridge

By Angela Reyes, The Bay Tribune

Gloria Liu has a way of listening in her own hallway. At Rockridge's top-ranked school, she hears the building before she sees it. Most days, she says, she can pick out a footstep she doesn't know.

This cycle the district table shows one more student in Rockridge than it did at C115. It is one child. West Oakland gained three over the same stretch, Downtown and East Oakland two each, and Piedmont Ave and Brooklyn one apiece. In a school this size, one child changes the sound of the place.

"When a new student comes in mid-cycle, the first thing I notice is the quiet," Liu says. "Not the building being quiet, but the way the hallways get a little more careful. Teachers glance up a beat longer when they hear footsteps they don't recognize. The office staff moves a little faster. And the child themselves — they're usually trying to take up as little space as possible, which is the opposite of what we want. Getting them caught up isn't about a test or a worksheet. It's about finding one adult in the building who they trust by the end of the first week. That's the whole job. The academics follow once that trust is there. We're lucky in Rockridge because we have the resources to do that well — small classes, aides who know the kids by name, a PTA that shows up. But I've seen it work in schools with a fraction of our funding, and it always comes down to the same thing: one person who says, 'I've got you.' That's what I look for first, and that's what I make sure happens before we even talk about grade-level standards."

I taught for years before I wrote about schools. I still keep my last gradebook in a drawer, and I know what she means. The roster is easy to change. The harder part is the first week, when a name on the list has to become a kid who knows where the pencils are.

I don't know who this student is. The table counts a head. It doesn't say whether the child is new to Oakland, new to the neighborhood, or new to this building, and I won't guess. Asking is the reporter's job. Not assuming is also the reporter's job.

## The After-School Hour

By the time the bell has gone and the pickup line has formed, the person who sees the next layer is Mei Wong, the after-school program aide. She has stood on the same steps for as long as this paper has been writing about her. The paper's C107 and C112 pieces found her at that door, learning the kids by how they walk through it.

"In the classroom, a kid can sit still and look like they're following along," Wong says. "In the after-school hours, you see the real picture — who's got energy left, who's running on fumes, who's carrying something from home that has nothing to do with math or reading. When a new kid shows up tired, they don't always say it. They find a corner, they put their head down, or they get a little short with the kids next to them. You learn to read it. You give them a snack and a quiet spot, and you don't push. The classroom has to move forward; we get to wait."

*We get to wait.* That is a stability policy in five words. A principal needs the child to find one adult by the end of week one. The aide has the afternoon, when no one is grading anyone.

## What the Table Holds

Rockridge's school quality index reads 8.4 on this cycle's district table, the highest of 22 neighborhoods. It sits a shade below last cycle, so the number didn't hold perfectly still. The paper's C114 piece noted a similar slight slip, and the top spot has stayed put. Graduation is 93.81%, college readiness 74.03%, teacher quality 9.12. The table lists 250 students and funding of 15,000. Baylight District is next at 8.3, then Piedmont Ave at 8.28, Brooklyn at 8.2, and West Oakland and Lake Merritt at 8.

Temescal is at the other end of the table. Its index is 6.25, graduation 83.8%, college readiness 53.5%, teacher quality 6.82. It lists 314 students and funding of 11,500. Liu says she has watched the first-week method work "in schools with a fraction of our funding." The table can't say how often that happens or where. It does show that Temescal's school serves more students than Rockridge's on less money.

The district behind both is Oakland City Schools, which the business ledger lists at 5,201 employees citywide, growth 7.37%. Per-hood funding on the table totals 286,000.

## The Unanswered Part

The Community Programs registry holds no row naming Rockridge. That doesn't mean the after-school room on those steps isn't there. Wong is in it, and I have stood in that line. It does mean the program isn't on the registry the city keeps. I can tell you who watches the door at 3:05. I can't tell you from the record what keeps that room funded, staffed or open next cycle.

So the question goes to the people who set the table. If one adult saying "I've got you" is the whole job, who answers for the schools where that adult isn't on the roster? Oakland City Schools' growth is on the books. Whether any of it reaches a hallway or an after-school room is not.

In West Oakland, three new children are finding their corners this cycle. In Rockridge, there is one. Someone has probably already handed that child a snack and a quiet spot.
