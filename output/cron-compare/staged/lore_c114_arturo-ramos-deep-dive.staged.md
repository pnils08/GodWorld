# The Five-Day Turn: Arturo Ramos and the Weight of Laurel

By Anthony Raines | *Bay Tribune* Special Feature

The fog off the San Leandro Channel rarely climbs past the MacArthur corridor with any real teeth, but by six in the morning in Laurel, it hangs just low enough to turn the lemon trees damp and silver. On the corner of 38th Avenue, a delivery truck idles in front of the bakery, diesel rattling against the plate glass. Down the street, the morning bell at St. Ansgar’s has not rung yet, but the kitchen lights are already on in the neat rows of 1930s craftsman bungalows that climb toward the ridge.

Arturo Ramos sits on his porch steps with a ceramic mug cradled between hands that are insured for eight figures. He is twenty-six years old. His right arm, loose inside an unbranded gray fleece, generated eleven strikeouts across eight innings in spring ball and anchored the Oakland Athletics through a fourteen-win campaign in Y2C48, carrying a 1.93 ERA into the postseason spotlight in Y3C4. On paper, under the cold arithmetic of the front office ledger, Ramos is an elite tier-one asset: an $8.1 million annual contract, entering his third full season, the designated successor to five-time Cy Young winner Benji Dillon.

In Laurel, however, the ledger has a way of flattening out into the pavement.

Laurel is not Rockridge, where Dillon retreats to an architect-renovated compound behind climbing jasmine, and it is not Downtown, where twenty-two-year-old phenom Travis Coles pays four-figure monthly rents to look down on Broadway from a high-rise tower. Laurel is the family belt that stayed reachable. It is the enclave where teachers, mechanics, city clerks, and small-business owners anchored themselves when the tech money crested through West Oakland and the estuary basin. 

"If you live down by the water, every conversation is about where the city is moving next cycle," Ramos says, his voice low, gravelly with the residue of sleep. "People look at you and they don't see nine innings; they see a property tax assessment or a ticket package. Here, nobody gives a damn about the luxury boxes at Baylight. If your lemon tree drops fruit on the sidewalk, you sweep it up."

### I. The Architecture of the Five-Day Turn

Every starting pitcher lives in a five-day loop that functions like an internal metronome. Day one is the start: adrenaline, rosin, crowd noise echoing off the concrete bowls of the Oakland Coliseum, the batter's box under sodium lights. Days two and three are the slow burn of lactic acid, the band work, the endless charting of hitters in darkened video rooms under the supervision of manager Deacon Seymour. Day four is the bullpen session—thirty-five pitches thrown with surgical intent under the watchful eye of general manager Mike Paulson. Day five is the silence before the rotation clicks over again.

In Y2C52, as the A’s chased pennant positioning, Ramos caught himself staring at the calendar on the kitchen wall, struck by how every square looked completely identical. 

"You can get lost in it," Ramos admits. "You wake up, you throw, you ice, you sleep. You win fourteen games and drop one, and people start laughing in the upper deck because it feels like a video game. But inside the game, it’s just the count. Two-and-one on the outside corner. One-and-two in the dirt. If you let yourself believe that sixty feet six inches is the whole world, your head rots."

That search for ballast is what keeps him rooted on this modest Laurel parcel. Born in Puerto Rico before being raised in Oakland’s public schools, Ramos never acquired the taste for insulation that usually accompanies sudden wealth. While teammates migrated toward gated enclaves or waterfront condominiums, Ramos bought a single-story house with a cracked driveway and a garage full of half-finished carpentry projects.

In Y3C7, during the quiet stretch before early-season workouts intensified, he started building an outdoor cedar potting bench. He had no garden to speak of, no immediate need for a bench, and no deadline. He simply bought two-by-fours from the lumber yard on San Leandro Street, laid out a square, and spent four afternoons cutting mortise-and-tenon joints with hand chisels. 

"My agent asked me why I didn’t just hire a crew to build a whole outdoor kitchen," Ramos smiles faintly. "I told him the point wasn't the kitchen. The point was sawing wood until your hands remember what wood feels like."

### II. Sidewalk Geometry

The reality of being the ace of an Oakland sports dynasty is that anonymity is a fragile illusion. In Y3C6, the week after his World Series opener, Ramos walked back up his front path at dusk to find that an anonymous neighbor had left a wooden crate on the porch containing six chilled bottles of craft cider and a note written on butcher paper: *Good sinker Tuesday. Keep your front shoulder closed.*

"You can't even get mad at that," Ramos chuckles. "It was good advice. I was flying open in the fifth against Philadelphia."

Yet being known in Laurel carries responsibilities distinct from the stadium tunnel. In Y3C7, walking down MacArthur to pick up morning pan dulce, Ramos passed the line wrapped around the local community health clinic. Standing in the chill were three people he had sat next to in high school homeroom a decade earlier. None of them mentioned the box score from the night before. They talked about dental coverage, city bus line delays, and the cost of groceries. Ramos covered a round of coffees, nodded, and listened. 

"You look at those lines, and then you see the flyers posted on the chain-link for the new Temescal Community Health Center, or you watch the city council argue over transit funding for Fruitvale," Ramos reflects. "You realize there are two Oaklands running on parallel tracks. There’s the Oakland that builds a two-billion-dollar waterfront showpiece at Baylight, and there’s the Oakland standing on the sidewalk at seven in the morning hoping the clinic doctor has an open slot. You don't get to pretend you don't see both just because you have an eight-million-dollar arm."

Even within the clubhouse, that tension hums quietly. His locker sits near John Ellis, the twenty-five-year-old rotation mate who also lives in Laurel, navigating the precarious early stretch of his career on a pre-arbitration $780,000 salary. Where Travis Coles arrived with the swagger of a high-school prodigy commanding superstar guarantees, and Benji Dillon carries the elder-statesman aura of a master whose jersey will inevitably hang from the Coliseum rafters, Ramos and Ellis inhabit the working core of the pitching staff. They are the arms that must absorb two hundred innings of high-stress leverage without flinching.

### III. The Wall on MacArthur

The defining moment of Ramos’s recent weeks did not occur beneath the floodlights of the diamond. It happened on a chilly Saturday morning in Y3C9, on the rear wall of the Laurel Recreation Center.

Overnight, a sprawling web of spray-painted tags had appeared across the cinderblocks facing the playground. By eight o’clock, a small cluster of neighborhood residents had gathered with five-gallon buckets of municipal gray paint, heavy-nap rollers, and scraping knives. Ramos, wearing an old paint-spattered sweatshirt and a knit beanie pulled down to his brows, showed up carrying two extra roller handles.

Beside him on the asphalt was Rilen Lend. 

Lend, forty years old, is the proprietor of the laundromat four blocks away—a father of four who has worked nineteen years in Laurel keeping commercial washers spinning and lint traps clear. Lend is an unapologetic, die-hard A's fan whose children can recite Ramos’s career strikeout-to-walk ratios from memory. But standing side-by-side dipping rollers into gray latex, baseball was not the topic.

"Rilen didn't ask me about my changeup grip," Ramos says. "He was talking about the bearings on his Maytag extractors, how parts are taking three weeks to ship through the Port, and whether the city's small-business utility rebate was ever going to actually disburse. We worked down the wall together for three hours. When you're rolling paint over someone else’s graffiti with five people you barely know, you find out real quick who actually cares about the block."

By noon, the cinderblock was a clean, uniform gray. The five neighbors shared lukewarm coffee from paper cups on the tailgate of Lend’s pickup truck. For Lend, who balances household finances to ensure his children’s school lunches are covered every week, Ramos wasn't a broadcast graphic on Bayline 6; he was a neighbor who understood how to lay down paint without leaving drips. By the time they shook hands and washed their rollers under the park spigot, a bond had formed that transcended the standard gap between professional athlete and season-ticket holder. 

"Rilen told me something that stuck with me," Ramos says quietly. "He said, 'The city will love you when you win twenty games, and they’ll forget you when your elbow blows out. But the people who live on your street will still have to look you in the eye at the corner store either way.' That’s why I’m here. That’s the whole truth."

### IV. The Next Horizon

As the season grinds forward, the conversations around Oakland baseball are dominated by transition. The historic East Oakland Coliseum, with its windswept concrete and cavernous foul territory, enters its final chapter as the Baylight District project takes shape on the estuary waterfront. Deacon Seymour manages with a pragmatism honed over decades, and Mike Paulson orchestrates transactions designed to keep the franchise afloat at the highest echelon of the American League.

Benji Dillon, now thirty-seven and navigating the inevitable negotiation between veteran cunning and diminished velocity, has already founded the Dillon Clinic up in Rockridge, preparing for life after the mound. The mantle of the rotation has passed, cleanly and irrevocably, to Arturo Ramos.

Yet Ramos does not seem burdened by the pedigree. Sitting on his porch steps as the Laurel morning traffic thickens on MacArthur, he watches an AC Transit bus rumble past, its brakes squealing softly against the curb. A teenager in an oversized green-and-gold jacket hops down from the rear doors, backpack slung over one shoulder, humming a tune as he heads toward high school.

Ramos finishes his coffee. In two hours, he will drive down to the Coliseum tunnel. He will walk into the clubhouse, check the rotation board, and throw fifty pitches off the mound with pinpoint command. But for now, he sits on the wooden porch of a house he owns outright, in a neighborhood that refuses to treat him like an untouchable icon. 

"I have five days between starts," Ramos says, setting the mug down on the weathered step. "That’s ninety-six hours of life where you’re just a guy who lives in Laurel. If you don’t know how to live those ninety-six hours, you won’t survive the two hours when the ball is in your hand."
