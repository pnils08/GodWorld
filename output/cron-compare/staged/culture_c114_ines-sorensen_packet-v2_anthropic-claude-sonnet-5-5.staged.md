# Uptown, Three Pages

"The A's winning season really brings Uptown together."

That is Priya Narang, who directs EMS and lives in Uptown. She gave the line to a reporter and kept going. "When they're doing well, the whole neighborhood feels it. The bars and galleries stay packed, and you can feel the energy. It's like we're all connected through the team."

Her page goes further, down to the kitchen counter. "I started a jar on my counter to save up for season tickets next year."

On the page she is answering the A's season and what it does to her block. The jar is still there.

"To be honest, the changes in Uptown have me feeling a bit uneasy."

That is Omar Amici, a construction laborer, also Uptown. He is answering the same season and the same streets. He didn't hurry to the next sentence, so I'll give you all of it. "The streets are busier than ever, and it seems like every other place is a for-rent sign these days. I've lived here long enough to see the neighborhood transform, but this feels different. It's like the neighborhood is shifting away from the people who've been here all along."

He has said versions of this on his page before. A week earlier he wrote: "I put on my good suit for Sunday service because some traditions still matter, but walking around, I see more strangers than familiar faces."

The question he carries is short: "Why does this change feel different from the others I've already watched happen here?"

Two people on the same blocks. One counts the bars and galleries that stay packed, and the other counts the signs in the windows. Neither one contradicts the other on the page.

Narang's own page from after work this cycle is a different kind of record. She wrote about a shift, a chart and a board on the wall. I'm leaving it for the desk that carried it and not folding it into this one.

The third page belongs to Ingmar Jordan, a spoken word poet, Uptown. Lives carried his wager this cycle. He lost on the UNDOCKED book, and a reporter asked whether that had colored what he sees outside his door.

"I've lost on the UNDOCKED book, yeah, but to be honest, it's not really on my mind when I'm thinking about the neighborhood changes. I've been noticing the rent signs popping up and then disappearing just as fast, and the bars and galleries staying packed. It's like the neighborhood is always in flux."

He had the answer, and then he put it down. What he kept, the line he carries, is the one the page marks as his question:

"I don't know if my losses on the book have affected my views on the neighborhood changes."
