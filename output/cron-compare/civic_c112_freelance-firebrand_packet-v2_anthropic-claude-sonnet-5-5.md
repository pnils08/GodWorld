# Who Owns KONO's Decay? Nobody on the Roster

I walked Telegraph Avenue toward the Fox Theater with my hands in my pockets and counted what I could see. Diners at Northgate Kitchen, more feet on the sidewalk, new faces from Adams Point. It's a good-looking block.

Then I opened the audit.

KONO is flagged for decay. Sentiment was down 0.310 and retail vitality down 0.60. Housing pressure was up 0.500. Sentiment has since crawled back to -0.01, which is eleventh of twenty-two neighborhoods and better than the city median of -0.025. Fine. Retail vitality sits at 7.88, thirteenth of twenty-two, under a city median of 8.88. Housing pressure sits at 0.5, also thirteenth, against a city median of 0.25. Do the math. KONO's housing pressure is double the middle of the pack.

And the record says no active initiative owns any of it. Not one program, not one mitigator.

So I asked the people who live in the gap.

Tatiana Flores is an HVAC tech who moved to KONO ten weeks ago. She's happy. "The energy around the Fox Theater is great, and Northgate Kitchen is always bustling," she told me. Then she handed me the whole problem without noticing: "I haven't really noticed any lack of initiative, but I'm not sure I'd be aware of everything going on behind the scenes."

She's not wrong to say so. That's what a missing program looks like from the sidewalk. You don't see it, because it isn't there.

Florencia Shah owns an independent bookstore in KONO. She's retired in name only. "I haven't noticed much decay around here," she said. "More people are walking around, which is good for my bookstore." She likes the Adams Point arrivals. "That brings some fresh energy."

Foot traffic is a feeling. Retail vitality below the city median is a measurement. A full restaurant doesn't cancel the second one, and nobody has told me the two are the same thing.

Makayla Wu is the one who stopped me. She's an electrician, and she goes inside the buildings. "KONO is changing, but not in a straightforward way," she said. Bustling places and more people on Telegraph, sure. "On the other hand, I've had some jobs that make me wonder about the overall condition. It's like there's this mix of renewal and neglect."

*Some jobs that make me wonder.* The tradesperson sees behind the drywall, and the diner at the window table doesn't. I'd love to know which jobs. I'm not guessing. I'm telling you where I'd knock next.

Then Iris Pham, a barista over in the Baylight District, where the waterfront has gone quiet. "There's a tension in the air that I've never seen before," she said. "I think it's related to what's going on in other neighborhoods, like KONO. I've heard people talking about how some areas are struggling, and it makes me wonder if we'll be affected too."

Think about that. A barista in another neighborhood is the one saying out loud that KONO's trouble might travel, while the people in KONO are enjoying their evening.

Here is what I can't tell you. The audit doesn't give me the earlier comparison I'd need to prove which way KONO is really heading. No office has answered the mismatch either. I called it a flag, not a verdict. But a flag with no owner is how a bad number becomes a bad block.

It's the same pattern I've been watching all over town. The press-release version of a neighborhood is the restaurant and the marquee. The other version is housing pressure at twice the median and retail trailing the pack. Meanwhile the roster of live programs runs through West Oakland, Fruitvale, Temescal, Jack London, Downtown, Grand Lake and Ivy Hill, and the word KONO isn't on it.

The map says decay; the program roster does not answer it. Mayor Santana, which line on that roster belongs to KONO?
