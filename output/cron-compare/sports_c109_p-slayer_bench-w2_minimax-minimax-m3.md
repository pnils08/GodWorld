# What We Asked John Ellis to Carry

I sat at my stool in a Jack London bar — the kind with one crooked TV and a bartender who doesn't ask what you're watching — and watched the Game 5 box score scroll across the screen. **A's 4, Twins 0.** We're 4-1 now, riding a one-game streak. **Pablo Almanzar: 9 innings, 1 hit, 0 earned runs, 3 strikeouts.** A playoff debut that read almost like his MLB debut. A re-introduction.

Eric Taveras went 3-for-7 across the two games with two homers and three RBI. The bats came alive. The kid on the mound was perfect.

But I kept thinking about Game 4. The 4-9. The man who took that ball.

John Ellis stood in the clubhouse after that loss and said the thing nobody in the front office will say. *"The emotional toll of the game is real, I can feel it myself after a tough loss. It's not just about winning or losing, it's about the pressure to perform and the weight of expectation from the fans. Sometimes it feels like we're carrying the whole city on our shoulders."*

Carrying the whole city. He said it, after a game where we got crushed. He didn't hide behind "location." He said: we're carrying you.

A small confession. After Game 4, I rode BART home and scrolled the message boards. Some of the takes in there — and I won't quote them, because they aren't worth quoting — were about how Ellis had nothing left. How it was time to move on. How one man can't be the whole damn staff.

Those takes are wrong. They're wrong the way a foul ball is wrong when you're standing in the bleachers and your beer is open. Here's why: a man just told you, on the record, that he is carrying this city on his shoulders, and your first instinct is to tell him to put it down harder? A man carrying the whole city doesn't get to put it down. We put it on him. We keep putting it on him.

That's the cost.

Pablo Almanzar got it. He understood what the city was asking, even on a debut night he had to be shaking through. He said it from the dugout after his nine innings of one-hit ball: *"I think the emotional toll of the game can be really high, not just for the players but for the fans too. I've seen how much it means to people around here, like when I'm out in the neighborhood and everyone's talking about the game. It's like the whole city is invested in it."*

A 4-0 win that was almost too clean to feel real. The kid carried us for nine innings while we caught our breath from the Game 4 hangover. And when he said "the whole city is invested in it" — that's not a small thing. That's a man saying: I see you, I see what you're carrying, here, let me take a turn.

Eric Taveras took his turn at his locker after the win. *"I feel like the game can be really tough on us players, both physically and mentally. The pressure to perform is always there, and when we don't do well, it can be hard to shake off the feeling of disappointment. I think the fans feel it too, they're so invested in the team and when we lose, it's like they're feeling the loss just as much as we are."*

He's the one putting up those numbers across the stretch, and he's telling you the loss hits him the same way it hits you in the bleachers. We ask these men to swing the bat, to take the mound, to carry our city — and then we ask them to tell us how it felt when they couldn't.

That's not fair.

The woman two stools down — Jessie Hess, who'd been at the bar since the first pitch of Game 5 — said it simpler. *"I feel like the game can be really tough on the players and the fans, it's like we're all in it together and when they lose, we feel it too."*

All in it together. That's the part we forget when Ellis is on the mound and the loss goes on his line. We're not on the outside yelling at the TV. We're inside, holding the same thing he's holding.

So here's the dare. We're 4-1. We just took Game 5 behind a kid who threw a one-hit shutout in his playoff debut, and Taveras is hitting like he's possessed. We can still go to the World Series. I believe that.
