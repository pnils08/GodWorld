### The Drop Nobody Claimed

By Luis Navarro | Bay Tribune Civic Affairs

---

Violent crime fell this cycle in Downtown, West Oakland, East Oakland, Lake Merritt and KONO — five neighborhoods, all at once, each drop large enough against that neighborhood's own recent run to register as something real, not the ordinary static of a cycle-to-cycle count. Not one of the five moved the other way. It is the sharpest simultaneous improvement in the number this city argues about more than any other that this cycle has posted. Nobody at City Hall has stood up to claim it.

Two institutions were positioned to. Neither was anywhere near where it happened.

Chief Montez ordered a visible shift of patrol this cycle — the kind residents notice, cars parked where people can see them. He sent it to Jack London, Chinatown, Brooklyn, San Antonio and Uptown. Those are, near to exactly, the neighborhoods the city's own review has flagged as declining with no program or initiative behind any of them. The chief deployed to where the city felt worse. The violence fell somewhere he didn't send anyone new. Downtown's Dillon Trevor, who watched an officer stop mid-chase to check that he wasn't hurt after a foot pursuit knocked dinner out of his hands, still won't sign off on "safer." "Cops are more visible lately, sure, but half the time they're just parked by the civic center scrolling on their phones," he said. On whether the neighborhood has actually changed, he landed where the numbers can't follow: "Things don't feel worse, but 'safer'? That's a stretch."

OARI's director, Tran-Muñoz, put out her own performance figure this cycle — a response-time number for District 7, which covers Temescal and Rockridge. Neither is among the five neighborhoods where violence actually fell at a level worth flagging. She reported strength from a district that didn't move.

That gap matters more than it looks, because of what this paper already has on the record about OARI. The program launched with one chartered goal: cut violent crime specifically in West Oakland, Fruitvale and East Oakland. It failed. Weeks of data came back showing the number in those three neighborhoods had risen, not fallen, and the metric was replaced. Mayor Santana explained the change herself, on the record: "We are shifting the rubric, and I'm going to be straight with you about that. The initial metric was based on an assumption about how violence reduction happens in communities with fresh access to non-police response. Six weeks of data said that assumption was incomplete." OARI has been scored since on non-police resolution rates and complaint volume — real measures, but not the one it was built to hit.

This cycle, violent crime fell sharply in two of those three original charter neighborhoods — West Oakland and East Oakland. The outcome the program was built for, failed publicly, and then stopped measuring, arrived in its own territory. Nobody connected the two. Whether OARI had anything to do with it is not something this record can answer, and this paper isn't going to pretend otherwise — that would be the same mistake the original charter made, reading a number as proof of a program rather than a coincidence worth investigating. What can be said is narrower and stranger: the agency chartered for this precise result now describes its own performance somewhere else entirely.

Nour Santos, a student in Chinatown, is the only person in this record who can describe what OARI actually delivers to a person on a sidewalk, whatever it adds up to citywide. A van pulled up near her walking home from school. "At first, I thought it was just another delivery truck or something," she said. "But then this lady got out and asked if I was okay. She had this badge, but she wasn't a cop. Just... calm, you know?" She told the responder she was fine. "She asked if I needed anything — food, or if I felt safe. I wasn't sure what to say because nothing was really wrong? But it didn't feel bad, either." A card with a number, and the van left. "I don't know if I needed it, but it didn't feel scary like when cops show up. That's something, I guess."

Temescal's Andre Lee, who has no institutional encounter to report either way, holds the piece's real thesis in his own words — locks double-checked after break-ins two streets over, and a block gathering the same stretch of days that ran past dark "laughing like old times": "So is it safer? Depends on the block, the—"

Set against that, the silence from City Hall reads differently than it might elsewhere. This paper has never handed institutions a crime decline. When West Oakland hit its lowest violence in cycles, this desk credited the people doing the work on the ground and used the moment to note an apprenticeship bill that had sat untouched for four cycles. When Fruitvale fell below its own norm, the credit went to festival crowds, not police. And Montez has his own record here worth remembering: on a Downtown uptick this desk called small, he answered it "without ceremony — something to monitor, not something to sound an alarm over," and this paper wrote at the time that a department with a rough relationship to its own credibility "has learned, slowly, that inflating routine variance into crisis is its own kind of damage." A chief who won't claim a drop he didn't produce is doing exactly what this paper has asked him to do.

So this isn't a story about officials hiding a win. It's a story about a public-safety event with no author. The city's sharpest improvement in violent crime this cycle happened while the department's visible attention and OARI's own reporting were both pointed somewhere else — and the one program actually chartered to produce this exact result, in these exact neighborhoods, stopped measuring for it. The drop is real. Nobody has explained it. And the agency built to explain it isn't looking.

### Standing in the Gaps

By Margaret Corliss | Editor-in-Chief

Winter makes this city honest. Every story in this edition is about a gap. The gap between a chart that hasn't moved and the clinic Temescal was promised. The gap between a drop in the crime numbers and anyone at City Hall willing to stand next to it. The gap between what Chinatown's ledger says and what its bartenders already know. Jax Caldera walked into a bar on 7th Street and came back with the question that threads this whole paper: when the record and the street disagree, who owes whom the explanation?

Some gaps closed this cycle. Mike Paulson, who once gave this paper exactly two words about the phone call that built the Oaks' front office, finally answered at length — no counterpunch, just an accounting he says he hasn't earned yet. And Elliot Abraham, the man that phone call brought west, gets his origins told properly in these pages.

The ones still open, we intend to stand in until somebody answers. That's the job. Light the candles, keep the receipts.

— MC

### The Decay Is On the Record. The Silence Is Too.

By Jax Caldera | Freelance

---

I walked into a bar on 7th Street in Chinatown on a cool winter evening, the kind of night where the overcast sky seems to match the mood of the neighborhood. The bartender, a woman who's been serving drinks in this part of town for years, looked up at me and said, "You know, it's been a quiet week around here, but that doesn't mean nothing's going on." She was referring to the decay that's been eating away at Chinatown, a problem that the city seems to be ignoring.

As I sat there, sipping my drink and listening to the bartender's concerns, I couldn't help but think about the numbers. The sentiment in Chinatown is at -0.310, retail vitality is down 1.06, and the crime index is up 2. These aren't just statistics; they're a reflection of the real-life struggles that people in this neighborhood are facing every day. And yet, despite these alarming trends, there's no civic initiative in place to address them.

I spoke to Selena Grant, a journalist who lives in the neighborhood, and she told me, "What the record shows does not line up with what I expected. What explains the gap in the record? I am going to keep watching this." Her words echoed the sentiments of many in the community, who are frustrated by the lack of action from the city.

Jose Colon, a catcher for the Oakland A's who lives in Chinatown, landed in the same place when I asked him: the record doesn't match what he sees on his own block, and he wants this paper to keep pressing for an answer. Hal Richmond, the senior historian who has watched this neighborhood longer than either of them, said the same — the gap between the ledger and the street is the story, and somebody at City Hall owes Chinatown an explanation for it.

As I left the bar and walked out into the cool winter air, I couldn't help but wonder: who is responsible for addressing the decay in Chinatown? The city council, which has been silent on the issue, owes the people of this neighborhood an answer. The fact that there's no active initiative in place to address the problem is a glaring omission, one that demands attention and accountability.

### The Chart Hasn't Moved

By Dr. Lila Mezran | Bay Tribune Health Desk

---

Two crews now work the lot at Temescal Avenue and 47th Street — one at dawn, one at dusk. As of last cycle's project record, excavation stood at roughly two-thirds complete and seven of twenty-four caissons were drilled. This cycle, the project director doubled the pace.

The cranes are up. The chart hasn't moved.

Andre Lee walks past the site most days. He is an electrician, and during the gap years he lined up at the faith health fairs when the fairs were the only care within walking distance. Asked this week not for his opinion but for his memory, he didn't talk about the project at all. He talked about the woman he used to share the line with.

"We'd always end up in line together, arguing about something stupid—whether the flu shot could really knock you out for a day, or if the free blood pressure checks were accurate. She'd roll her eyes, I'd laugh, and somehow that made the waiting feel shorter." She's gone now — moved away last year. "Every time I see that steel frame going up, I catch myself half-turning, like I'm about to say something to her. Old habits."

That half-turn is the whole story. The building is rising fast enough to turn toward — and there is still no one inside it. Construction is a fact; care is a promise. Lee is measuring the distance between them the only way a person can, against the people the waiting cost him.

The city's institutions, this cycle, started measuring the same distance from both ends.

From one end, speed. Bobby Chen-Ramirez, the project director, added a second excavation crew and extended drilling shifts, and framed the acceleration as a refusal to trade pace for safety: "We're running two crews now—one at dawn, one at dusk—but I won't sign off on concrete until every caisson meets OSHPD-3 specs. Temescal deserves a building that lasts."

From the other end, suspicion. Councilman Warren Ashford of District 7 — the district containing the site — demanded to know what the speed costs: "Before we celebrate the Temescal Health Center timeline acceleration, someone should ask what corners get cut and who pays for the change orders."

The district's own numbers then picked a side, and it was not their councilman's. Ashford's approval fell three points to 51 — the steepest drop on the council — in the same cycle the city recorded the health center as performing despite the opposition to it. The voters and their representative are looking at the same construction fence and rendering different opinions. That is not a scandal. It is a disagreement with a measurable cost, and it belongs on the chart.

And the chart itself says this: the city's own review of neighborhood conditions shows construction underway and Temescal's measured health outcomes not yet responding. Treatment in progress; response unconfirmed. Any nurse learns early not to declare from the doorway which way a flat monitor will go — but you also don't chart a recovery you can't see. Ashford is asking whether the treatment is being administered badly. The numbers are asking whether it is reaching the patient at all. Both answers live in the same building.

The timeline, for the record:

- **C80** — Council authorizes the $45 million center, 6-2. Vega and Tran cross to yes; Crane absent; Ashford and Chen vote no.
- **C85** — Mayor's priority designation, five months after the request, following an outside resubmission.
- **C92** — The Mayor attaches a public clock: architect by C93, site by C94, state filing by C95.
- **C95** — HCAI application filed on deadline. Site acquisition closes two cycles behind the original clock, at C96.
- **C100** — Groundbreaking. Calderon-Nishi mobilized.
- **C102** — Two crews, extended shifts, one audit demand. Twenty-two cycles from vote to this morning's first shift.

The context that gives the chart its weight: the city's winter illness rate sits at 9.9 percent — just under one in ten residents — the level Temescal's unexplained waves made infamous before they faded, never diagnosed. Interesting is doctor-speak for concerning, and that number is interesting. Whether it is seasonal burden or the ambient level of something nobody named is a question this desk has asked before and will keep asking.

Patricia Nolan, the retired teacher who told this paper there is a difference between patience and being ignored, put the neighborhood's terms plainly: "Quick doesn't equal right. … They cut corners when they left that lot vacant all those years. Now they'd better not cut them on what gets built inside. Temescal's waited long enough."

Lee's terms were plainer: "Now they're building that shiny health center down the block—good, about damn time—but tell me why it feels like we're still playing catch-up? … I'll believe it when I see the lights on and the doors open."

Both were asked directly, and answered as people living it. Note what neither of them said. Neither questioned whether the center should exist. The argument in Temescal is no longer whether — that ended at C80, and ended again every morning the lot sat fenced anyway. The argument now is whether the city can deliver the thing it finally agreed to, at the speed it just promised, without spending the trust the delay already burned.

**What to watch:** the first concrete pour — every caisson signed to OSHPD-3 spec, per the project director's own condition — and the neighborhood's health indicators in the cycles after the doors open. If the chart moves, the treatment reached the patient. If it doesn't, the audit the councilman demanded will have asked the right question for the wrong reason, and the city will owe Temescal a harder one.

Until then, the standing guidance holds: residents feeling unwell this winter should not wait for a ribbon-cutting. The faith health fairs continue screening and counseling, and Temescal Islamic Center has expanded its counseling services this cycle. Care within walking distance is still the community's own — as it was in the gap years, when the waiting was shared, and felt shorter.

### Before a Game Is Played, the Oaks Take a Hit — and the Man in Charge Finally Answers

By Anthony Raines | Bay Tribune Sports

---

The Oaks took Draymond Green in the expansion draft, then traded him to Portland for Jerami Grant days later — and this past cycle, on his own podcast, he said the organization is garbage, that players don't respect its head of basketball operations as a basketball man, and that the man in question, Mike Paulson, should stick to baseball.

Neither Paulson nor Elliot Abraham, the general manager Paulson brought over from his own staff in Chicago this past cycle, has said a word about it. That silence held through an arena announcement that added its own weight to the cycle: the new building in the Baylight District will not be ready until after the All-Star break, the Oaks will play their inaugural stretch at Oakland Arena in the meantime, and owner Elias Varek said the team is weighing naming rights among local businesses. That search has nothing to do with the naming rights already sold on the new Athletics stadium — Civis Systems, the urban-intelligence firm Varek founded, holds those. Two buildings, two deals, and the owner was careful this cycle to keep them apart even while little else about the operation stayed tidy.

Varek was not careful about defending his front office. Asked about Green's charge, he waved it off as noise — "some kid with a mic," not a threat to an operation that is, in his framing, still pouring concrete. "Front offices don't fight in the alley," he said. "They build the damn arena." But he did not stop there. Unprompted, he went further, drawing a line most owners don't draw in public: "If that exec can't take a punch? He shouldn't be in the room. But that's between him and me." Nobody asked him whether his own head of basketball operations could take a public hit like this one. He raised the question himself, and answered it by measuring whether the operation could survive a punch rather than closing ranks around it. That is daylight, and Varek put it there himself, in print, before his franchise has played a game that counts.

The daylight is the story, and it did not open this cycle. This paper has been tracing the gap since the tech-expo panel where Varek first floated hiring the Athletics' general manager to run his new team — "I will absolutely be making that phone call," he said then. Paulson's answer at the time was a flat denial: "I have not received that call." This paper kept asking. It was only later, cornered by the assembled press in a Minnesota clubhouse after an A's loss, that Paulson's account of the call changed — and shrank — to exactly two words: "we spoke." Whatever was actually said in that call has never been confirmed by either man since. It sat there for cycles as the one specific, unconfirmed thing at the center of an otherwise well-covered expansion story — a courtship nobody would put on the record, a hire nobody would describe. It was still sitting there when Baylight's own director, Keisha Ramos, took three cycles to formally name Varek's conflict of interest — Oaks ownership stacked on Civis equity stacked on Baylight equity stacked on the Athletics' stadium naming rights — and built a four-part recusal wall around it. It was still sitting there when Paulson, in his first offseason running the Oaks, traded the fifth pick and Herbert Jones and an unprotected first-round pick three years out to move up for AJ Dybantsa — the kind of move a front office makes when it is treating its inaugural draft class like Year Four of an established program, not Year One of a new one. And it was still sitting there when Abraham arrived from Chicago with nothing on the public record about how that year actually went for him beyond Paulson's own account of it. A two-city front office built on trust between two specific people works exactly as well as that trust holds. Green didn't invent any of that. He said it crudely, on a podcast, from Portland. This paper had already written most of it down.

What changed this cycle is that Paulson answered — not on the show, but to this paper, on the record, at length, for the first time since those two words in the clubhouse. "Look I appreciate Draymond is unhappy with how things may have played out but we were up front about the situation, he is in Portland now and is a future hall of famer." No counterpunch. The man who called his franchise garbage gets called a future Hall of Famer in return. On the respect question, Paulson didn't claim any: "Regarding the respect, that's something I try to earn each day and don't expect it to be given to me. Sustained success gets you respect." And on the charge itself, the only honest answer an expansion team that hasn't played a game yet can give: "Now The Oaks garbage? I guess we will see......" Set next to two words in a clubhouse and a phone call nobody would confirm, that is a man who has stopped declining to answer. It reads like restraint because it is restraint — not a clapback, its opposite.

Not everyone in Jack London Square is waiting on the front office to sort this out. Blair Patel, a ship repair foreman who has spent enough winters on the estuary to watch a "revitalization project" stretch like his grandmother's taffy, doesn't have a basketball opinion so much as a patience problem. "I don't care if they play in a damn parking lot — just win games, pay people fairly, and stop acting like these shiny new buildings are doing us favors. Half the folks who work the docks with me can't afford to step foot in those arenas anyway." Of Green: "Nothing Oakland loves more than somebody who talks reckless and backs it up. But if he's just flapping his—"

The Oaks have not played a game. They have already had their credibility tested in public, by a man they traded away days after they drafted him, and the answer their head of basketball operations gave was not denial. It was a daily accounting he says he hasn't earned yet, and a promise, unfinished on purpose, that the rest remains to be seen.

### Elliot Abraham: The Architecture of Quiet Leadership

By Hal Richmond | Bay Tribune Senior Historian

---

When you've spent forty years watching men attempt to build lasting things in Oakland, you learn to spot the difference between noise and structure. Noise is loud; it fills press conferences, drives sports radio commentary, and draws the cameras to the front of the room. Structure is quiet. It sits in the back of the house, reviewing balance sheets, checking travel manifests, and ensuring that when the floorboards are laid, they do not creak under the weight of an empire.

In Edition One Hundred One, when Head of Basketball Operations Mike Paulson announced the hiring of Elliot Abraham as the General Manager of the Oakland Oaks, most of the chatter focused on Paulson's two-city high-wire act. People wondered how one man could manage the final chapter of a three-title Athletics baseball dynasty while simultaneously building an NBA expansion franchise from scratch. But those who know how organizations actually function understood the real story: Paulson hadn't built a circus; he had summoned his bridge.

To understand Elliot Abraham, you have to look far beyond the front-office suites of Jack London Square or the court at the arena. You have to go back to Port-au-Prince, Haiti, where Abraham was born before being adopted at a young age by an American family. His childhood was shaped by two distinct, formidable minds: a father who was a novelist, weaving narratives out of blank pages, and a mother who was an educator, spending decades working directly in impoverished Haitian communities to construct schools where none existed. Throughout his youth, Abraham returned repeatedly to Haiti, witnessing firsthand the stark contrast between romantic ideas and the painstaking, unglamorous labor required to keep a community standing.

That dual inheritance—the narrative vision of a writer and the grounded execution of a community educator—defined his trajectory. When he entered the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, he pursued rigorous quantitative discipline while minoring in Business, learning to map complex human organizations like software systems.

His first major test in sports wasn't in the NBA or Major League Baseball, but in a space legacy executives routinely ignored. Abraham built the first successful professional pickleball league from the ground up, engineering its revenue model, venue logistics, and player contracts when conventional wisdom wrote the sport off as a backyard fad. He proved that value isn't inherited; it is constructed where others see empty space.

That structural brilliance caught the attention of corporate builder Elias Varek. Abraham joined Civis Systems, quickly rising to become one of Varek's most trusted organizational leaders. It was at Civis that Abraham mastered the mechanics of municipal infrastructure and complex operations—skills that made him indispensable when Mike Paulson took over as General Manager of the Chicago Bulls for a single season. In Chicago, while Paulson commanded the spotlight and fought high-visibility roster battles, Abraham operated in the shadows, keeping the day-to-day apparatus of the franchise from fracturing under the pressure of a single-season sprint.

Now, as the Oakland Oaks prepare for their inaugural Summer League slate in Cycle One Hundred Three, Abraham steps into an environment that demands every ounce of his background. Head coach Wilson Shepard is building his very first bench. Point guard Adash Stanley carries the weight of a seventy-five million dollar contract as a nineteen-year-old undrafted prodigy. And small forward AJ Dybantsa, the number one overall pick, carries the high-ceiling expectations of an entire city.

Paulson may be the architect who drew the blueprint, but Abraham is the builder holding the level.

Here is the long-view prediction for the next twenty cycles: Mike Paulson's dual-franchise experiment will not fail because of media scrutiny or roster math. It will succeed or break entirely on the strength of Elliot Abraham's quiet architecture. If the Oaks establish themselves as a stable, disciplined franchise in Northern California over the next decade, it will be because Abraham built an operational fortress in Oakland that allowed Paulson to dream without the foundation collapsing beneath him.

-- Hal Richmond
