# The Weight We Asked Him to Carry

I sat on the stoop outside the Laurel Tavern at midnight, still in my jacket from the bleachers, watching the last of the BART trains rattle past with its cargo of the stunned. The score was already dead history—Game 4, that 9-4 gutting—but I kept hearing John Ellis's voice from the post-game scrum, the way he stood there like a man who'd just remembered something terrible.

"The emotional toll of the game is real," he said. "I can feel it myself after a tough loss. It's not just about winning or losing, it's about the pressure to perform and the weight of expectation from the fans. Sometimes it feels like we're carrying the whole city on our shoulders."

I wanted to look away from him when he said that. I couldn't.

Some fans will tell you this is just October baseball, that every team feels this, that the pressure is the privilege. They're wrong—here's why. John Ellis didn't sign up to be Atlas. We made him Atlas. We spent the whole summer building our hopes around his arm, our October dreams, and when he couldn't lift the world that one night in Game 4, we felt the crush of it and so did he. The difference is he had to stand in front of microphones and explain why his body wouldn't do what we needed.

I watched Pablo Almanzar the next night and felt something worse than relief. Nine innings. One hit. Zero earned runs. The rookie pitched like he'd been handed the keys to a kingdom he didn't know was burning. "I think the emotional toll of the game can be really high, not just for the players but for the fans too," he said afterward, and I believed him because I'd seen him in the Jack London neighborhood before, the way people stopped him for photos like he already belonged to them. "It's like the whole city is invested in it."

The whole city. That's the thing we don't admit in the good times. Eric Taveras tried to tell us too—"the pressure to perform is always there"—and I thought about his two home runs in Game 5, the three RBIs, the way he made it look possible again. But I also thought about what he said about disappointment, how "when we lose, it's like they're feeling the loss just as much as we are."

Jessie Hess caught it simplest, standing near me in the lot after Game 4: "It's like we're all in it together and when they lose, we feel it too."

We're not all in it together. That's the lie we tell ourselves to make the wins sweeter. John Ellis carries something we don't carry. He carries our need for him to be the Superman we never asked permission to need. And when he couldn't be—when the Twins hung five runs on him before the third was over—he still had to carry our disappointment in him, our sudden memory of every other October when someone else's arm failed, when someone else's city moved on without us.

Anthony can tell you the ERA, the WHIP, the inherited runners stranded. I can only tell you what it felt like to watch John Ellis walk off that mound in Game 4, and to know that we did that to him—not the Twins, not the baseball gods, but us, with our weight, with our whole city on his shoulders.

The A's are up 3-2 now. Pablo Almanzar gave us that. But I keep thinking about what we asked John Ellis to be, and what it cost him to almost be it, and whether we'll have the decency to ask less of the next man or just find another pair of shoulders.

I don't know if we can bounce back from this. I don't know if we should.
