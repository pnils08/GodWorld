At OakHouse in Rockridge, dishwasher Matty Lipo arrives before the doors open and sets up the station, water already running hot.  

"A full shift at OakHouse starts before the doors open — you come in, you set up your station, you get the water hot. Then it's just a river. Plates, pans, the pastry crew's bowls, the ramekins that never end. Mimmie and Jesus and Kalyani keep me busy, that's for sure. What arrives is the mess of a busy kitchen, and what leaves is clean steel and dry racks, ready for the next course. It's honest work. My hands know it, my back knows it. And when that last rack is done and I shut off the sprayer, I walk out to the lake and I sit on that bench by the boat dock and I just breathe. That's the rhythm of it."  

Pastry chef Mimmie Carlisle works the same stations with Jesus Jorden and Kalyani Alvarez. "Pastry at OakHouse isn't a one-person show, that's for sure. With Jesus and Kalyani on the roster with me, we split the day into the early bake-off and the afternoon prep. Mornings are all about the laminated doughs and the croissants — you can't rush that, it's a rhythm. Then we pivot to plated desserts and the case stuff for dinner service. It's a lot of standing, a lot of timing, and a lot of checking that the walk-in is organized so nobody's hunting for a sheet pan mid-shift. We each have our stations, but we're always crossing paths, handing off a tray or a spatula. It's steady work, and when the rush hits, you're glad there's three of you who know the dance."  

At Claremont Table, server Elio Perez resets napkin rolls between services. "Between services, the floor at Claremont Table gets real quiet — the kind of quiet where you can hear the ice machine hum from the back. I don't mind it. I bus the last of the plates, reset the napkin rolls for the next rush, and if there's a lull longer than a minute, I step out back and just breathe. The neighborhood feels different then, slower, like even College Avenue is catching its breath. I try not to think about the next table or the side work stacking up — I just take the quiet while it's there. Some days I catch a few innings on the bar TV if someone left it on, but mostly it's just me, the stack of menus, and the sound of my own footsteps on that floor. It keeps me sane before the dinner crowd rolls in."  

Owner Camila Osman moves through the same floor. "People think owning a restaurant means I'm just in the office looking at numbers. But I'm on the floor most days, and I see the parts that don't show up on receipts—the way Elio remembers a regular's order before they even sit down, the quiet hustle when we're short-staffed, the little things that keep a place feeling like a neighborhood spot. That's the real work, and it's what makes Claremont Table feel like home to folks."
