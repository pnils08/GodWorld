# At Rockridge pickup, the strongest school record in the city slips by five hundredths and the hallways keep their hours

Mei Wong stands at the after-school door in Rockridge with the day's last snack count in her head. She is an after-school program aide, and her job at pickup is mostly questions. Parents ask whether their kid ate a real snack. They ask who sat with whom. She knows every answer, because the teachers here, she says, remember every child's name.

Ask her about the district's numbers and she says so plainly. "I can't speak to the enrollment numbers or the per-student funding figure — that's not something I see in my day-to-day," Wong said. "What I can tell you is what I see at pickup: parents asking if their kid ate a real snack, teachers who remember every child's name, and a community that shows up. The after-school space here has always felt tight, but it's the people who make it work. If the index slipped a little, I wouldn't be surprised if it's because we're all stretched thinner, not because the kids or the staff care any less."

The index did slip. Rockridge's school quality index reads 8.53 on the district table this cycle, down from 8.58 at C113. That is still the highest of the 22 neighborhoods. Baylight District follows at 8.24, then Piedmont Ave at 8.22. The table also puts Rockridge's graduation rate at 94.2%, college readiness at 75.03% and teacher quality at 9.1. It lists 248 students and funding of 15,000.

Enrollment moved the other way. Rockridge gained one student against C113. West Oakland gained three, Grand Lake and Chinatown two each, and Piedmont Ave and Brooklyn one each. Enrollment is the number that moves from cycle to cycle. The quality table mostly holds still, so a five-hundredths dip is the kind of movement that gets noticed.

Gloria Liu, the principal, did not treat it as an alarm. She reads it from inside the building. "A one-point drop on a scale like that — I'll be honest, it doesn't change what happens in the building on Monday morning," Liu said. "The kids still come in, the teachers still teach, and I still walk the halls. The district watches that number, sure, but I watch the kids. If anything, a dip like that makes me look closer at where we might be slipping — maybe it's a cohort thing, maybe it's a resource thing — but it doesn't mean I'm suddenly going to pull books off shelves or cut a program. We're still the strongest school in the city, and that's not because of a number. It's because of the people here."

She offered "cohort" and "resource" as possibilities, not findings. The table does not say what moved the number, and this story will not guess.

Maya Torres-Dillon teaches high school science in Rockridge, and she holds the strength and the slip in the same hand. "I read that 75% as both," she said. "It's a strength we should be proud of — that's a real number, and it means most of our kids are leaving ready for what's next. But if I'm being honest with you, as a teacher, I don't get to treat any number as a finish line. That 8.53 slipping, even a little, that's the kind of thing that gets my attention. It's not about panicking over a decimal point. It's about asking what that one student who just enrolled needs, and whether we're actually serving every kid in that building, not just the ones already on track. We've got the strongest record in the city, and that's something to protect — but the day we start thinking we've arrived is the day we stop pushing."

The youngest voice here may be the clearest. Rick Dillon, a grade schooler at Rockridge, waits out pickup the way kids do, shifting his backpack. "I don't really get the number stuff, but my dad says our school's still the best around," he said. "We got one new kid this year, and we're all still learning the same stuff. I think it just means we gotta keep trying hard, like in baseball when your batting average dips a little — you don't panic, you just take more swings."

The district table gives context. Oakland City Schools employs 5,201 people citywide, and the per-neighborhood funding on the table totals 286,000. Rockridge's 15,000 sits near the top of that spread. At the bottom of the quality ranking, Temescal reads 6.36, down from 6.43. It has 313 students, a graduation rate of 84.37%, college readiness of 54.5%, teacher quality of 6.86 and funding of 11,500. Temescal has more students than Rockridge and less money, and its index slipped by seven hundredths. The same table that calls Rockridge strongest records the same kind of dip in Temescal, in a school that starts further back.

I checked the program registry for Rockridge too. It holds no youth program row for the neighborhood. That does not mean the after-school space Wong describes is not there, only that the registry has not recorded it. The tight room and the snack count are, for now, a program that runs on people and not on paper.

Stability here is real. The doors open Monday, Liu walks the halls, and Wong knows the names. What's missing is the reason. The next record needed is what sits behind the five hundredths, whether that is one cohort, one resource line or noise. The other is whether that tight after-school room is counted anywhere. If the strongest school in the city cannot hold its index in place, what does that say about the ceiling for the other 21 neighborhoods, and who is willing to answer for it?
