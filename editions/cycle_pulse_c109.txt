The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

Daniel Cloak was wiping flour off his apron at a West Oakland bakery when he told Carmen Delaine what he thought of the Stabilization Fund. "It feels like something big, but I'm waiting to see it in action." That was the week in one sentence, and it wasn't only West Oakland talking.

The fund has passed and money is moving, but as Carmen reported, the people it was built to keep in their homes are still watching. Irene Fay, who pulls morning shifts at a coffee shop nearby, says people are hopeful and skeptical at once. Darius Clark knows exactly what the fund is for and hasn't seen a change on his block. Elena Patel heard about it at the community center last month and still hasn't noticed anything. I don't read that as ingratitude. I read it as people who have watched promises travel slower than they sound.

Now set that beside what Jordan Velez found a few blocks over. Oakmesh, Civis Systems and Coastline Construction are all growing along the same West Oakland lane, and the money there is not waiting for anyone. Enzo Walker, a plumber at Oakmesh, told Jordan he spends half his days fixing pipes in buildings that didn't exist last year. "The money's there, sure, but it's not sticking around here." His colleague Dom Will sees the same growth and calls it something real, not a bubble. Sam Fazio at Civis calls it a shared project. Maybe it is. But a neighborhood can have a crane on every corner and a fund in its disbursement phase and still be asking where the help is. Enzo has the quote of the week: "We're all just trying to keep the water running while the city builds over us." Private money arrived fast and visible this week. Public money meant to hold people in place has not yet shown itself. I'd like someone to tell me which of those is outrunning the other.

That question of who's responsible followed me east. Luis Navarro spent his week with the same empty lot in Fruitvale, and what struck him was how far the frustration traveled. Rick Walker, who teaches K-8 nearby, said it plainly: the same empty lots year after year and nobody explains why. But it was Melton Neilon, bagging sourdough at a Temescal farmers market table, and two Rockridge servers, Elio Perez and Depak Ying, who made Luis's point. People with no stake in Fruitvale know the transit hub is stalled, and none of them can say who is steering it. Luis found nothing on the books this cycle aimed at that gap. I don't have a name to give Rick either, and that bothers me more than the delay does.

What makes it sharper is Trevor Shimizu's reporting on the trains. Ridership jumped nearly 30 percent across the system, with 99,582 boardings. Fruitvale Station alone logged 10,414, up 3,756. Eleanor Rivera at 12th Street, which took in 23,848 riders, says her team "just adapts." Jerald Jensen, the janitor there, put it more honestly: trash cans overflowing by noon and no time to catch his breath between rushes. Joel Wren, the AC Transit mechanic, credits the crews keeping buses moving. Part of the surge was a game day and a pair of big events at Lake Merritt, so I wouldn't read it as a permanent shift. Still, people are pouring through Fruitvale Station while the hub beside it sits stuck. On-time performance improved, to 85 percent at 12th Street and 81 percent at Fruitvale. The workers are holding the system together. I'd like to see the planners do the same for the lot.

Then there's Temescal, which turned up in three different stories this week. Angela Reyes took us to the hallway outside Lo Thoop's creative writing classroom. Temescal's school index sits at 6.8, the lowest on the city table, with Rockridge at 8.87. Eighty-five percent of students graduate, but only 57 percent test college-ready. Lo said it best: "We're celebrating the finish line, but the race isn't over." Patricia Nolan, who taught there for decades, was blunter. "We celebrate the diploma, but we're not always honest about what it really means." The students had their own view. Jun Leu and Salma Navarro don't track the index. They do their work, go home, and say some teachers push while others let things slide. I won't scold teenagers for not carrying a statistic. The adults can carry it.

And Maria Keen's piece reminds us who is arriving. Temescal has 311 kids, eight more than last cycle. It has 2,106 working adults, fifty-one more, and 294 seniors, seven more. Nobody is leaving those numbers. Yet the one program of organized care on Maria's block is the Temescal Islamic Center Food Pantry, which has been standing since C83. Omar Lingio loves the fog that settles on his store. Marc Allie and Jackson Sift have both heard, secondhand, about a Temescal Holiday Market. Neither could say where or when. I'd like to know too, and I hope someone with the details calls us. A growing neighborhood with the lowest school index on the table and one pantry holding the line is carrying a lot on a thin frame.

The fog and gray were all over the week, by the way. Noah Tan's pieces have Colin Soria blowing on his hands outside the laundromat, and the people he talked to called the weather "neutral" and "deliberate." I'll take the dull gray days as a small mercy. They gave the city a week to slow down and notice things.

On the field, Simon Leary wrote the most careful column of the week about the A's. They are 1-0 and have won two straight, and there's a role change on the team that nobody has explained. I can't square 1-0 with two straight wins either, and I won't pretend to. Simon's restraint is right. A record is a beam, not a forecast. And over in Rockridge, Nia Rook found the neighborhood still lit up after Otis Redd's night on UNDOCKED, a 143-credit swing that has people arguing about who holds the edge and for how long. Rockridge, sitting at the top of the school table, can afford the entertainment. I won't begrudge anyone a good night.

Here is what I'm watching next week. First, whether the Stabilization Fund shows up where Daniel Cloak and Darius Clark can see it, in a rent kept in check or a household that stays. Second, whether anyone claims the Fruitvale hub, and whether the crowds at that station get an answer for the lot beside it. Third, whether Temescal gets a second program, a clearer school story, or at minimum a date and a street for that holiday market. And fourth, whether the A's can explain their own role change. I'll be listening for names this week. The city has plenty of complaints. What it's short on is somebody saying "that one's mine."

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

# West Oakland Stabilization Fund Enters Ground Phase—But the Money's Visibility Remains Unclear

Daniel Cloak stood behind the counter at the bakery in West Oakland, wiping flour dust from his apron. The morning rush had cleared, and he leaned against the register with the kind of patient skepticism that comes from living in a neighborhood long enough to watch promises move slower than they sound. "I've heard folks talking about the Stabilization Fund at the bakery, but honestly, I'm not sure yet how it's touching the ground here," he said. "It feels like something big, but I'm waiting to see it in action."

That wait—the gap between a fund's official disbursement phase and the moment residents feel its weight on their block—is the ledger's current problem. The West Oakland Stabilization Fund entered its disbursement-active phase this cycle. The Initiative passed. The money is moving through the city's machinery. Yet four weeks into implementation, the people it was built to hold in place remain in the posture of watching.

Irene Fay, a barista working the morning shift at a West Oakland coffee shop, had heard the same conversations Cloak tracked at his counter. "I've heard folks at the coffee shop talking about the Stabilization Fund, but honestly, it's hard to tell where that money's actually going. People seem hopeful but also kinda skeptical—like they're waiting to see if it'll really make a difference on our block."

The skepticism is not misplaced. Initiative_Tracker records show the fund's status as passed and active in disbursement phase as of this cycle. That designation means the decision is made and the allocation mechanism is live. It does not yet mean the money has landed in the hands of the people it aims to stabilize—the renters, the long-term residents, the households that have held ground in West Oakland as the city has shifted around them. The fund was designed to keep people in their homes. Darius Clark, another bakery regular in the neighborhood, had absorbed the purpose. "I heard about that fund at the bakery—couple regulars were talking how it's supposed to help keep folks in their homes. Haven't seen much change on my block yet, but I hope it works out."

Elena Patel, a resident, echoed the gap between announcement and arrival. "I heard about the Stabilization Fund at the community center last month, but honestly, I haven't noticed any changes on my block yet."

The civic record identifies the Initiative as active in disbursement. The people living under its intended reach report a wait. Neither observation is false. The fund is moving; the ground has not yet shifted. This is the moment where implementation clocks and lived time diverge—where a ledger entry says "phase disbursement-active" and a neighborhood watches for proof.

The question for the city offices managing this fund is not whether it was passed. It is whether the velocity between approval and ground-level arrival can close before the residents watching for it lose faith that it will arrive at all.

By Angela Reyes | Bay Tribune Civic

# The Finish Line That Isn't

Lo Thoop stands in the hallway outside the creative writing classroom in Temescal, watching students filter past at 3 p.m. on a Wednesday. The school quality index reads 6.8—the lowest on the city table. Across the street, Rockridge sits at 8.87. But Lo isn't thinking about rankings right now. Lo is thinking about the gap between what the diploma says and what comes after.

"You know, living here in Temescal, I've seen kids graduate—they walk across that stage, families cheering—but then there's this quiet worry afterward," Lo says. "Are they really ready for what's next? It's like we're celebrating the finish line, but the race isn't over. I teach creative writing, and I see it in their essays—bright ideas, but sometimes they're missing the tools to build on them. It's not just about getting them out the door; it's about making sure they're equipped to walk through the next one."

The numbers seem to tell one story. Eighty-five percent of Temescal students graduate. But only fifty-seven percent test college-ready. That gap—twenty-eight percentage points—sits in the silence between ceremony and consequence.

Patricia Nolan, who taught in Temescal for decades before retiring, speaks from the weight of that silence. "After teaching for so many years, I've seen kids walk across that graduation stage who can barely write a proper paragraph," Patricia says. "The numbers might say 85% graduate, but half of them aren't ready for what comes next. It's not just about passing classes—it's about whether these kids can actually think critically when they leave here. We celebrate the diploma, but we're not always honest about what it really means."

In the student zone, the ranking feels distant. Jun Leu, sitting on a bench near the gym, doesn't track the index. "I don't really think about graduation rates or college stuff yet. At school, we just do our work and go home. Some kids talk about college, but most just wanna play basketball or hang out after class," Jun says. "My cousin Ira says high school is harder than middle school though."

Salma Navarro nods along the same current. "I don't really think about the numbers. At school, everyone's just trying to get through the day. Some teachers really push us, but others just let things slide. My brother Hector says high school was like that too—you could graduate easy, but actually being ready for what's next? That's different."

The Oakland Unified School District employs 5,201 people citywide and is growing. Temescal's school receives $11,500 in per-student funding—less than Rockridge's $15,000. The city's teacher quality score here sits at 6.96. The question that hangs in the hallway, in the essays, in the families' quiet worry: *Is this a resource problem, a culture problem, or something harder to name and fix?*

By Luis Navarro | Bay Tribune Civic

## The Same Empty Lot, Four Times

Rick Walker stands at the chain-link fence at the corner lot two blocks from his school, waiting for the crossing guard to wave the line of kids through. It's the same lot it was last year. He teaches K-8 in Fruitvale, cooked line before that, and he's got maybe four minutes before the bell before he has to be back inside.

"Honestly, I hear teachers and parents at school talking about how the city keeps promising things for Fruitvale but nothing ever moves forward," he tells me, one eye on a kid untying his shoe mid-crosswalk. "We see the same empty lots year after year, and nobody ever explains why."

I don't have an explanation for him. There isn't one on the record.

Three miles north, Melton Neilon is bagging sourdough loaves at the Temescal farmers market table, flour on his forearms, half-listening to the vendor next to him complain about parking. He's not from Fruitvale. He doesn't need to be to have heard about it.

"I've heard folks at the farmers market talking about the Fruitvale Transit Hub delays," Neilon says, "but honestly, I'm not sure who's supposed to be steering it forward. It feels like everyone's waiting for someone else to take charge."

That's the sentence that stuck with me. Not the complaint — the shrug inside it. Nobody in this cycle's record names who's steering.

Over in Rockridge, servers Elio Perez and Depak Ying are working the same lunch rush at the same diner, six tables apart, and somehow talking about the same lot in Fruitvale neither of them has a stake in.

"Honestly, I hear folks at the diner grumbling about how nothing ever moves forward with the Fruitvale plans," Perez says between orders, "but nobody seems to know who's actually holding the reins or why it's stuck."

Ying, wiping down a table Perez just cleared, doesn't disagree. "I've heard people talking about the Fruitvale Transit Hub delays, and it feels like no one's stepping up to take responsibility — it's frustrating because we need that progress."

Four people. Three neighborhoods. Zero names.

Here's what the record gives me and what it doesn't. What it gives me: the city signal this cycle flagged a civic gap — accountability, or the absence of it, tied to Fruitvale. And it gives me this: no civic initiative on the books this cycle addresses that signal. Not a placeholder, not a stalled line item. Nothing pointed at it at all.

What it doesn't give me is a name. Not an office, not a person, not a duty assigned to anyone who could pick up a phone and explain the lot. I don't have a request on file. I don't have a response, or a refusal, or a date stamped on either. There isn't one to report — which is different from saying nobody's ever going to answer. It just means nobody's on record answering yet.

So I'm left with what four residents in three different neighborhoods noticed without prompting each other: a stalled thing with no fingerprints on it. That's not proof of wrongdoing. It's proof that the story of who's responsible hasn't been told to the people standing at the fence.

Walker's bell rings. He heads back in without finishing the thought. Neither did I.

What's still unfinished, for the people living next to that lot, isn't the transit hub. It's the answer to Neilon's question — who's supposed to be steering it forward — and right now, that answer doesn't exist anywhere I can find it.

By Trevor Shimizu | Bay Tribune Civic

### Ridership Surge at 12th St Oakland City Center Tests Transit Systems  

At 12th St Oakland City Center, the fares click louder and more often. Eleanor Rivera, a BART station manager, has felt the surge in her bones—23,848 riders this cycle, up 5,472 from the last. “It’s not just numbers,” Rivera says. “It’s people adjusting their routines, and us adjusting right along with them. The team just... adapts. No big announcements, just everyone doing their part to keep things moving smoothly.”  

The station isn’t alone. Across Oakland, ridership has jumped nearly 30% this cycle, with 99,582 boardings logged across eight stations and ten corridors. Coliseum Station saw 9,724 riders, a spike of 4,859 from the last cycle, while Fruitvale Station recorded 10,414 boardings, up 3,756. Even MacArthur Station, quieter by comparison, saw a 24% increase, with 12,030 riders passing through its gates.  

The reasons aren’t hard to spot. Downtown hosted a game day, while Lake Merritt saw two major events. The Baylight District’s ongoing construction planning has also redirected traffic, funneling commuters toward 12th St. Joel Wren, an AC Transit mechanic who works near the station, has noticed the uptick firsthand. “You know, I’ve been seeing more folks hopping on the buses lately—especially down at 12th Street,” Wren says. “It’s good to see people out and about, trusting the system again. But what really gets me is the crew keeping those buses running. Those mechanics and drivers? They’re the ones putting in the long hours to make sure everyone gets where they need to go, especially with all these extra riders.”  

Jerald Jensen, a janitor at the station, sees the surge in the wear and tear. “More coffee spills to mop up, more trash cans overflowing by noon,” Jensen says. “Used to be you could catch your breath between rushes, but now? It’s constant. Folks pouring in for work, for games, for whatever’s happening downtown.”  

Coen Cartier, a transit operator, echoes the sentiment. “It’s been busy, but it feels good to know we’re helping people get where they need to go,” Cartier says. “The team’s been working hard to keep things running smoothly, and it’s paying off.”  

On-time performance has improved at most stations, with 12th St hitting an 85% on-time rate, up four points from the last cycle. Fruitvale Station, despite its own surge, managed an 81% rate, up three points. Even Coliseum Station, often a laggard, saw its rate climb to 83%, up five points.  

The question now is how long this surge will last—and whether the system can sustain it.

By Jordan Velez | Bay Tribune Business

# West Oakland’s new business money is moving faster than the block can absorb

Enzo Walker spends his working days at Oakmesh Systems keeping water moving through buildings that, in his telling, keep appearing around him. He is a plumber at the West Oakland civic-tech company, working alongside Elijah Roberts, Dom Will and Jace Robinson.

Oakmesh, Civis Systems and Coastline Construction are all expanding along the same West Oakland business lane. Those are strong signs. They are also not the same thing as money settling into the neighborhood.

“Honestly? Those tech firms like Oakmesh and Civis—they're changing the block faster than we can keep up. I work at Oakmesh, and half the time I'm fixing pipes in buildings that didn't exist last year. The money's there, sure, but it's not sticking around here. Guys like Elias at Civis talk about 'systems' and 'efficiency,' but ask the folks at the bus stop if they feel any of that. We're all just trying to keep the water running while the city builds over us.” Walker said.

The contrast is visible in the roster itself. Oakmesh’s named jobs range from construction labor and plumbing to machine learning. Civis lists Sam Fazio and Catherine Santos as digital twin architects, Elias Varek as founder and Maurice Franklin as drone fleet coordinator. Coastline’s managing partner is Desmond Achebe. Mariner Construction and Northgate Construction are also part of the construction economy around the lane.

Dom Will, a machine learning engineer at Oakmesh, sees the same expansion from inside the company but reads its purpose differently. “Oakmesh feels like we're building something real here - not just another tech bubble. Marcus keeps us focused on civic impact, not just profit. Civis down the street? Elias runs a tight ship, all that urban intelligence work. You see their drone fleet buzzing around sometimes. Coastline's got cranes up everywhere - hard to miss them. But it's the small crews, like our construction guys, that make the neighborhood hum.”

At Civis, Fazio describes the growth as a shared project rather than a balance-sheet story. “Working at Civis Systems, I’ve seen how West Oakland businesses like ours, Oakmesh Systems, and Coastline Construction are really shaping the neighborhood. It’s not just about the jobs—it’s about the community we’re building together. People here are passionate about what they do, whether it’s urban systems intelligence or construction. There’s a sense of pride in contributing to something bigger.”

That pride is real in the people carrying the work. So is Walker’s harder question: if the companies are expanding, who gets to feel the benefit beyond the payroll? The next big move for West Oakland’s businesses is not simply whether Oakmesh, Civis Systems or Coastline Construction grows again. It is whether that growth becomes something the block can keep, and who answers for the gap if it does not.

## Culture — Temescal

The sign on Telegraph still says Temescal Islamic Center Food Pantry, same as it did back in C83, when whoever started it first propped a folding table against the wall and called it a program. Twenty-six cycles later it's still the one thing on this block's record that counts as organized care — one program, the ledger says, and for a block this size, carrying this many new faces, that's a thin single line to hang a neighborhood on.

The faces keep coming. Three hundred eleven kids now walk these sidewalks to school, eight more than last cycle. Twenty-one hundred six adults are clocking in somewhere, fifty-one more than before. Two hundred ninety-four seniors are still here too, seven added since C108 — nobody leaving that count, just more joining it. Nobody on the ledger says why they picked this particular stretch of blacktop over another, but ask around Temescal Alley and you get answers anyway, just not the kind that make it into a program file.

Omar Lingio will tell you it's the fog. "It's moments like those that remind me why I love Temescal so much," he said. "I was just standing there, watching the fog roll in, and it felt like the whole neighborhood was wrapped in this quiet, peaceful blanket. The rest of the city might have been sunny and bustling, but here, it was like time stood still. I felt grateful to be a part of this community, to have my store here and be able to experience days like that." He's not wrong that the weather plays favorites with this block. It settles here when the rest of the city stays clear, like it's got an address to keep.

Down the street, word of a holiday market had made its way to Marc Allie before he'd gotten the full shape of it. "I was just walking by and heard some folks talking about the Temescal Holiday Market," he said, "sounded like it's going to be a nice event, you know, something to bring the community together. I didn't get all the details, but it seemed like it was going to be on a quiet main street over in Temescal." Jackson Sift had heard the same thing secondhand the night before. "I was talking to some folks last night and they mentioned the Temescal Holiday Market," he said, "it sounds like a great way for the community to come together. I'm looking forward to checking it out and seeing what it's all about." Two men, two separate conversations, same half-formed rumor traveling the block like it already belonged here.

And at the stoplight by Temescal Alley, Yammie Perez heard something else holding the neighborhood together — not an event, just a sound. "I love how Temescal always has this energy, you know?" she said. "Like, I was stopped at a light near Temescal Alley and I could hear the bass thumping from a few cars away. It was infectious, I found myself nodding along. It's moments like those that remind me why I love living here — there's always something going on, some rhythm to the neighborhood that just makes you want to move."

Fog, bassline, a market nobody's confirmed yet — that's the texture holding down a block that's absorbing fifty-plus new adults a cycle on the strength of one listed program. The pantry on Telegraph has been here since C83. Nobody on the ledger can say who started it, who's still running the folding table, or what they'd tell the 311 kids and 2,106 adults now calling this stretch home. That's the name still missing from this block's record.

# The Structure of a Second Win

*By Simon Leary*

Jack London is a useful place to consider what a record can hold—and what it cannot.

The A’s are 1-0. They have won 2 straight. Between those two facts sits a role change, a small alteration in the shape of the team that is visible without being fully explained. The available present does not say why it happened, what came before it, or what anyone intends to make of it. It only gives the change and the record beside it.

That is enough for an interpretation, but not for a verdict.

A team’s continuity is not always the absence of change. Sometimes continuity is the structure that allows a change to occur without making the whole shape unrecognizable. The A’s current record gives that idea its narrow footing: one lossless mark, two consecutive wins, and a role inside the team that is no longer quite where it was. The record is the beam. The role change is the adjustment in the load.

Nothing more should be built on it yet. The 1-0 record does not establish a plan. Two straight wins do not establish a philosophy. The role change does not, by itself, reveal an intention to preserve either number. Those are questions that belong to a later account, if the facts arrive.

For now, the A’s have something more modest and more durable than a story about destiny. They have a present arrangement that has held through the record available to them. In that arrangement, change and continuity are not opposites. One can be seen without pretending to know the whole design.
