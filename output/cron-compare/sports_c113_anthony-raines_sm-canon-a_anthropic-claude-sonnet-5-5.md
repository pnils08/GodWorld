# Morton's 285 At-Bats Hold More Weight Than Reyna's .317 in the Left Field Question

Desmond Morton's season line reads 285 at-bats, a .291 average, 7 home runs and 44 RBI. It is the longest sample among the three men the feed lists for one open outfield spot. His TrueSource card fills in the rest: 80 games, 83 hits, 23 walks, 49 strikeouts, 28 stolen bases, a .348 on-base percentage and a .498 slugging percentage. Those 285 at-bats are the main fact in a rumor-class item about next season's outfield, which the feed frames as five outfielders for three positions.

The arithmetic of the squeeze is on the feed. Danny Horn is the center fielder, and the feed calls him a returning MVP. Vladimir Gonzalez is entering his peak and had the best numbers of his career last season, according to the same entry. That leaves one left-field opening and three candidates: Morton, Frank Reyna and JR Rosado. Reyna and Gonzalez are both listed in right field, which makes Reyna's path harder. The roster does not need another right fielder, so the question is who plays left.

## What the average says

The batting averages barely separate the three. Reyna's feed line is 164 at-bats, a .317 average, 14 home runs and 34 RBI. Rosado's is 184 at-bats, .298, 7 home runs and 27 RBI. Morton is .291. Taken alone, the average would hand the job to Reyna. Rosado would sit between the other two, and Morton would sit last.

The cards do not support that order.

Reyna's TrueSource card shows 46 games, 52 hits, 6 walks and 18 strikeouts. His slugging percentage is .683 and his on-base percentage is .339. Morton's on-base percentage is .348, higher than Reyna's even though Morton's average is lower. The difference is the walks: Morton has 23 and Reyna has 6. Six walks in 164 at-bats is a thin base for calling the average repeatable. If the average slips, as an average built on a short sample often does, there is little else in the on-base line to keep Reyna's number up.

The power is the real difference. Reyna's card works out to one home run every 11.7 at-bats. Morton's works out to one every 40.7. Rosado's is one every 26.3, and his card shows 12 walks and 23 strikeouts. A rate like Reyna's is the kind of number a club notices.

## Is it real

The test here is the one a PANDAS-style read applies: weigh the stable signals above the noisy ones. The stable signals are the large sample, the strikeout and walk columns, and consistency across seasons. The noisy ones are a short run of home runs and a high average over a few weeks. On that test, 14 home runs in 164 at-bats is exciting but unproven. Reyna's card holds one season, and 46 games is not a season's worth of evidence about how pitchers will adjust. The feed tags him as a power hitter "with a mass of potential." That is a description of what he might become, not a measurement of what he has done.

Morton's line is quieter, but it has been tested longer. An earlier feed entry, from cycle 102, had him at 249 at-bats with a .305 average and 7 home runs. By the current line he is at 285 at-bats and .291, still with 7 home runs. The average drifted down and the power count did not move. That is the profile of a hitter whose value is spread across contact, walks and 28 stolen bases rather than concentrated in one tool. It is also the part of his line that does not yet answer the harder question about corner power. His card shows a .498 slugging percentage, which is respectable but not Reyna's .683. The card prints no projection that would say whether 7 home runs in 285 at-bats grows or stays put.

The feed calls Morton the highest-rated prospect in the group, with five tools, and says he has already shown success at the major-league level. The earlier cycle-111 entry said Morton and Reyna were competing for the one everyday outfield spot, with the other likely to become the fourth outfielder. That framing still holds. Rosado is described as a good-contact player who "may trend as a role player." His .298 supports the contact part. His 7 home runs in 184 at-bats and 2 stolen bases do not make a case for more than that.

## The claim

The case for Reyna rests on a .317 average and a home-run rate that outruns everyone's, both from 164 at-bats and 6 walks. The case for Morton rests on 285 at-bats, a better on-base percentage, 23 walks and 28 steals, with a lower ceiling in the power column. The numbers do not make Morton the better hitter. They make his line the more reliable one. If the left-field decision is made on evidence rather than on the headline average, Morton has the stronger claim to an everyday role, and Reyna has the stronger claim to a closer look.

What would change that is more Reyna. A longer run of at-bats with the walk total moving off 6 would show whether the .317 and the home-run pace survive being pitched around. More Morton would show the same for his power. Neither card carries a second season, and the feed does not say which way the organization leans, so the question of whether to trust the man who has played every day or the man who has hit when the matchup favored him can't be answered from either line yet.
