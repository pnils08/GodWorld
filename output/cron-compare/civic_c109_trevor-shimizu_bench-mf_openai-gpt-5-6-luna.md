# The System Gets Busier Before It Gets Better

At 12th St Oakland City Center, Jerald Jensen is already reading the station in its residue. He works for AC Transit as a janitor, and his measure is not the display board. It is what collects on the floor and around the bins.

“You wanna know about 12th Street? I see it in the wear and tear—more coffee spills to mop up, more trash cans overflowing by noon. Used to be you could catch your breath between rushes, but now? It's constant. Folks pouring in for work, for games, for whatever's happening downtown,” Jensen said.

This cycle, 12th St Oakland City Center carried more riders than before. Its on-time performance also improved. The station moved more people and, on the supplied measure, did so more reliably. The less flattering part of that improvement is that reliability is being maintained inside a busier physical system.

Eleanor Rivera, a BART station manager with BART Oakland Division, described the change without dressing it up as a program.

“At 12th Street, we've definitely felt the uptick in ridership. It's not just numbers - it's people adjusting their routines, and us adjusting right along with them. The team just... adapts. No big announcements, just everyone doing their part to keep things moving smoothly,” Rivera said.

That is how infrastructure usually reveals its actual operating model. The public sees a train arriving on time. The system also includes the floor afterward, the bins before noon, the mechanics keeping vehicles available and the operators absorbing the extra movement. None of those tasks announce themselves until one of them cannot be absorbed.

Joel Wren, an AC Transit mechanic, put the labor behind the increase in plain terms.

“You know, I've been seeing more folks hopping on the buses lately—especially down at 12th Street. It's good to see people out and about, trusting the system again. But what really gets me is the crew keeping those buses running. Those mechanics and drivers? They're the ones putting in the long hours to make sure everyone gets where they need to go, especially with all these extra riders. Makes you appreciate the work that goes on behind the scenes,” Wren said.

The pattern is not confined to one platform. Recorded ridership increased across the network. The service picture is not uniform, with some stations improving more than others. That is not a system failure by itself. It is a reminder that a network increase can conceal different tolerances at individual stations.

Coen Cartier, an AC Transit transit operator, sees the same shift from the vehicle side.

“I’ve seen more folks hopping on the buses lately, especially around 12th Street. It’s been busy, but it feels good to know we’re helping people get where they need to go. The team’s been working hard to keep things running smoothly, and it’s paying off,” Cartier said.

The record places the largest increases alongside game-day activity and events in Downtown, Lake Merritt and other named areas. It also places Baylight District activity in the same operating environment. That does not establish which activity produced which trip. It does establish the maintenance question: whether a system that can absorb a surge today is being measured closely enough to absorb the next one without treating janitorial work, vehicle work and platform work as invisible capacity.

The unanswered question is what this surge means for transit’s future as civic events and infrastructure projects continue shaping Oakland. The workers describe the adjustment. The next failure, if one comes, may first look like a full bin, a longer repair queue or a punctuality measure that stops improving.
