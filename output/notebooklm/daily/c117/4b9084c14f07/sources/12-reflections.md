# Rockridge, in their own words

## Avery Santana — Mayor of Oakland

> Jax — you know I don’t duck questions, so let me give it to you straight. You’re right to push. And I’m not going to sit here and tell you that every street in this city gets the same attention at the same time. That would be a lie, and you’d smell it from a mile away. But here’s what I’ll say: the noise isn’t what moves me. It’s the data, and it’s the people who show up with receipts. Fruitvale — I was down there last month with the OARI team, not for a press conference, just to watch them work a corner. East Oakland — I know exactly where the potholes are on MacArthur because I drive them. Grand Lake — that’s not sliding, that’s creeping, and it’s just as unacceptable. The problem isn’t that we only respond to noise. The problem is that the system is built to respond to noise — and I’ve spent the last two years trying to rewire it so that we respond to *need*. That means community boards that report to me directly, not through twelve layers of city hall. It means funding formulas that don’t reward the loudest ZIP code. So no, I won’t pretend every neighborhood has gotten its fair shake. But I’ll also tell you this: the day I stop showing up in Fruitvale without a camera crew is the day you can call me out. Until then, keep asking. That’s your job. Mine is to make sure you don’t have to ask twice.

## Jett Walker — Plumber

> *(I let out a long breath, the day's grime still under my nails. I don't even have to think about it—my feet already know the way.)* The Temple. Not during a service, not when it's busy. Late, like now, when the weekend market's just packing up and the only light left is the one over the side door. I'll sit on the low wall out front, the one with the chipped paint, and just watch the street for a while. There's something about that spot. The smell of the incense still hanging in the air, the sound of a vendor folding up his tarps, the way the streetlights buzz a little off-key. It's quiet in a way that doesn't ask anything of me. No pipes to fix, no customer waiting, no phone buzzing about a job. I wasn't always a temple guy. That was my wife's thing—she'd drag me to the evening services, and I'd stand in the back, hands in my pockets, feeling out of place. But after she passed, I found myself coming back.
