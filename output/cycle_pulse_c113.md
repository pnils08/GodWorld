Three clear days in a row, and you could hear the city exhale. Noah Tan walked the waterfront and found Elle Lilo back on the Jack London bridge, the cough gone, saying the harbor felt like it was breathing again and so was she. Cee Lee is back on the Lake Merritt path, though she wouldn't call the cold "lifted," only paused. Johhny Necklar, in Rockridge, put it best: "Recovery isn't on a weather schedule." He still stops twice on the way to the diner. I trust the people who say the sun helps and then refuse to promise it lasts.

The sun doesn't reach every block equally. Dr. Lila Mezran spent time with Marisio Curazo, an elder care coordinator in Chinatown, where the sick count is the highest in the city and sits over the watch line, though it fell slightly this week. Curazo doesn't see a figure. She sees a list ordered by who gets called first: the home health aide, the prescription delivery, the household where a phone call is the whole job. The hospital holds six of her neighbors. The rest are at home, with a daughter on the bus from Fruitvale to cook and a nephew late to work because he's picking up medicine. The city's records call the cluster low-severity. As Mezran notes, that means the record doesn't call it an emergency. It doesn't say the households are fine.

Then there is Baylight, where two records of the same project refuse to agree. Luis Navarro laid them side by side. The engine's audit says the final council vote is stalled and Downtown's civic initiative hasn't moved in three cycles. The tracker says the vote already happened, six to three, signed by the mayor long ago, and that the next real event is a construction milestone review in Cycle 115. Marcus Webb, who runs a program at OEWD and lives Downtown, feels the stall in the applications on his desk. "I want the council to finish the job," he told Navarro. I can't tell him which record is right. I can tell him that when the city's own books describe one project as both finished and frozen, someone owes residents an explanation, and so far Leonard Tran's statement that progress is "a crucial step forward" isn't one.

Housing runs under everything this week. Maria Keen's Fruitvale piece sets Lucia Polito's gratitude for the Postpartum Oakland Network beside Rick Walker's plain accounting. Walker teaches K-8, sees the tutoring posters at St. Esperanza and the loud mercado, and says the rent went up again. Gregory Mims has watched too many programs come and go to be impressed by one cousin landing on a plumbing crew. Meanwhile Tamsin Pruitt's Page of Lives reads like a city of people signing leases. Brady Lopez cleared the last debt and rented on Piedmont. Isla Sike, widowed and directing a shelter in West Oakland, called her new place "a relief to have some stability." Orion Kerkering took a rental in Lake Merritt, and a janitor who'd seen it said it isn't always easy for young folks. Brady, though, can't tell whether the sameness of the days is peace. I wouldn't hurry to answer that for them.

Now the people who cross our desks twice, because that is where the city turns into one city.

Kwame Fitzgerald is the clearest case. Pruitt has him renting in Uptown, a craft brewery owner who a cycle ago waved at the regulars, "all present and accounted for." This cycle the log has him drinking free cold brew in the company kitchen, feeling "completely disconnected from the neighborhood outside," his social life limited by what the record calls winter mood. He also appears in Noah Tan's civic reporting. I'd read it against that week of clear skies and lifting colds. A man can move into a new place, hold a business, have the sun come out, and still not feel the block. The Tribune last named him in the Baylight Phase II story in Cycle 99, back when the project was stepping into the light. Now Baylight is stuck between a tracker and an audit and Kwame is stuck between boxes, and neither has said what's inside them.

Ramon Vega's name sits on the Baylight yes votes in Cycle 83 and turns up again in Maria Keen's Fruitvale reporting. I'll be careful: I haven't got his words in front of me this week, only the two places he stands. In one he voted to build. In the other the people near him are paying more rent for the same block. A council member's vote doesn't reach a tenant's bill by itself, but residents will keep drawing that line, and I think officials should be ready to be asked about it.

Omar Amici is also a civic name who crosses into Pruitt's pages. I'd rather tell you honestly that I see two doorways into the same man than invent a room I haven't entered. The pattern is the same: the person the city counts and the person the city lives with are rarely the same page.

Then Desmond Morton, whom Anthony Raines and Tanya Cruz cover and whom Pruitt also lists. Raines has the evidence on the field: a left fielder with a full season, 28 stolen bases nobody else in the group comes near, and an on-base mark that holds up over a real sample. He cautions against crowning Frank Reyna on a hot 164 at-bats, a caution about the sample, not the man. Cruz is in the clubhouse where five outfielders are fighting for three spots and Vladimir Gonzalez says the mood is lighter than you'd think. Morton and Reyna stretch side by side, the whiteboard lists names and circles none. A ballplayer waiting on a manager's glance is also a person on the Page of Lives. Whatever Paulson decides will move one man's season and his address, and that's worth remembering when we write "the squeeze."
