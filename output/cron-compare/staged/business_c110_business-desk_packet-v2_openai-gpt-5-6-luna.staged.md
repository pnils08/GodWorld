# Downtown’s Growth Has Different Clocks

Priya Nair works at EBMUD on a problem that does not fit neatly into a growth story: how to make water systems withstand climate change while Downtown expands around them.

“My work at EBMUD focuses on ensuring our water systems can handle the pressures of climate change, especially as the city grows,” Nair said. “It’s about balancing the need for reliable infrastructure with the realities of a changing environment. Downtown’s growth adds complexity, but it’s also a chance to innovate and build resilience into the system.”

That is the cleanest version of the Downtown business question this cycle: growth creates work, but not every dollar moving through the neighborhood arrives with the same weight.

EBMUD is the largest operation in this slice of Downtown’s economy. Its scale matters because Nair’s work is not an abstract office exercise. Infrastructure has to absorb the demands of a growing neighborhood before the people using it have any reason to think about the pipes, treatment systems or planning behind the tap.

At Atlas Bay Architects, the operation is smaller but still substantial. Tobias Jurko is listed there as a landscape architect. Pacific Standard Architecture also reports growth. The architecture firms show a Downtown economy that can post gains without giving much away about who receives the benefit. Payroll, project assignments and the daily commute sit behind those totals, but the ledger does not say how the gains are distributed.

The smaller businesses make the contrast harder to ignore. Oakland Entertainment Talent Ageny has Nina Rines, an actress, on its roster. Miso Metro has Andre Turner working there as a line cook.

Those are positive movements, but they are not interchangeable. An increase at a utility and an increase at a restaurant describe different kinds of breathing room. One can register as institutional capacity; the other may be closer to a shift’s worth of payroll pressure, supplies or the next bill. The figures do not establish who is secure. They do show why growth is too blunt a phrase for the people clocking in.

The storefront scene is quieter than the totals. There is the counter, the work surface, the place where a person turns a business number into a task for the day. At Miso Metro, Turner’s title is line cook, not owner or executive. At Oakland Entertainment Talent Ageny, Rines is an actress inside a small operation. The roster puts workers next to businesses, but it does not promise that growth feels the same from either side of that relationship.

Nair’s description offers the most useful standard. Downtown’s expansion is “a chance to innovate and build resilience into the system,” she said. The chance is real. So is the balancing act. EBMUD’s scale can make resilience look like a permanent institutional function, while an increase at Miso Metro leaves the same question at street level: what does growth buy the person carrying it?

This cycle’s figures answer who expanded on paper. They do not answer who gets the durable benefit, who carries the risk, or whether the people working inside Downtown’s businesses have enough room to call this steady.
