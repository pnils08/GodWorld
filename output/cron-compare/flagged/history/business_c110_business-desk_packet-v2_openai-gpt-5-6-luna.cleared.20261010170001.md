# The Work Behind Downtown’s Growth

At EBMUD, Priya Nair’s job is to think past the next billing cycle. As a Climate Adaptation Engineer, she works on whether Downtown’s expanding demand can be met by water systems built to withstand a changing climate.

“My work at EBMUD focuses on ensuring our water systems can handle the pressures of climate change, especially as the city grows. It’s about balancing the need for reliable infrastructure with the realities of a changing environment. Downtown’s growth adds complexity, but it’s also a chance to innovate and build resilience into the system.”

That is the less visible side of a growing Downtown: not a ribbon-cutting or a leasing announcement, but the technical work required to keep a larger neighborhood functioning. EBMUD has 1,900 employees and reported $656.6 million in revenue this cycle, up $1.5 million from the previous cycle. Its recorded growth is 11.7 percent.

For Nair, the pressure is not abstract. Growth turns water service into a question of capacity, maintenance and long-term planning. The work may happen away from the storefront, but it carries the same economic concern as any small counter or office: whether the demands placed on a system outrun the resources available to maintain it.

The rest of the Downtown business ledger shows a more uneven scale of expansion. Atlas Bay Architects has 86 employees and $42.3 million in revenue, up $78,000 from the previous cycle. Pacific Standard Architecture has 96 employees and $48.3 million in revenue, with a $69,000 increase. Those are substantial firms, but their cycle-to-cycle gains are not interchangeable with the conditions at a two-person restaurant.

At Miso Metro, where Andre Turner works as a line cook, revenue is recorded at $250,000, up $376. Oakland Entertainment Talent Ageny, with Nina Rines on its roster as an actress, has 10 employees and $1.3 million in revenue, up $1,000. The figures describe growth, but not necessarily comfort. They do not tell a line cook whether a stronger month changes his hours, or whether a small increase gives a restaurant room to absorb its next expense.

That is the contradiction in Downtown’s current business picture. The larger employers are carrying the weight of expansion in headcount and dollars, while smaller operations can show movement without gaining much room to maneuver. Growth is present in the numbers; its benefit is less evenly legible from the counter.

Nair’s work offers the clearest measure of what the neighborhood is asking for. If Downtown is going to add pressure to its infrastructure, who pays for the resilience that keeps that growth possible—and who gets to share in the value created by it?
