# The Room After the Lights Go Down

On a weeknight in Grand Lake, the sanctuary at Or Hashalom Renewal Community is a room before it is anything else. Folding chairs lean against one wall. The lights are set low, and the space still smells of the building, not of anything being done in it. Someone has left a jacket on the back of a chair. The congregation of about 400 has a founding date 58 years back, and the community is led today by Rabbi Miriam Goldstein. The record describes it as progressive, with social justice at its center.

Those are the facts the record holds. What the room holds on a weeknight is quieter, and the record does not reach it.

Across the city this week, the record shows several congregations doing the same kind of thing in different rooms. At Bay Pure Land Buddhist Temple in Chinatown, in C109, the congregation opened its doors for community support, with 41 people in attendance. At Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship Downtown, the same kind of response came in C107, with 72 in attendance. Temple Beth Or in Rockridge opened its doors in C104 with 104 in attendance. Lakeshore Unitarian Universalist Fellowship on Lake Merritt activated an emergency assistance fund in C104, with 53 in attendance.

The holy-day entries run alongside these. Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship celebrated Epiphany in C109 with 237 in attendance. Cathedral of the Living Word on Lake Merritt observed traditional Epiphany rituals in C108 with 1,077 in attendance, and celebrated Epiphany again in C109 with 1,265. St. Esperanza Parish in Fruitvale celebrated Epiphany in C108 with 374 in attendance.

Those numbers are attendance, and they say who came through a door on a given night. They do not say what anyone felt there, what was asked, or what was given. A count of 41 in a Chinatown temple and a count of 1,265 at a cathedral on Lake Merritt are different sizes of gathering, and the record does not say they are the same kind of act. What the two share is a building that opened its doors when the record says it did.

Back in Grand Lake, the difference between a building that is opened and a building that is used is the question that matters most to a community like this one. An opened door is a fact the record can hold. A used door depends on who walks through it, what they came for, and whether they came back. The record holds the first. The second belongs to the people who stayed late to put the chairs back.

The ethical weight of this week's record falls on the institutions that carry these rooms. Congregations have set their own rules for who may enter, who leads, and who answers when something goes wrong. The record shows congregations responding to crisis, but it does not show who, inside each building, is responsible for what happens after the doors open. That is the accountability question no entry on the record can answer, and it is one the congregations themselves are placed to answer.

Or Hashalom's social justice orientation puts it in an unusual position on the record. The question its leadership and members can answer is how the community's social justice work could serve as a model for other faith communities. The record does not yet show that model. It shows a congregation of 400, founded 58 years ago, in Grand Lake, with a rabbi and a stated orientation, and a sanctuary that empties after dark.

The chairs are still against the wall. Whether the next person who walks in finds a community or only a room is not yet on the record.
