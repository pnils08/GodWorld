# Seven Miles of Growth, Measured From the Wrong Side of the Fence

*By Jordan Velez, Business Reporter*

Tre Mingo works at the Port of Oakland, and every day he walks past a fence. On the far side, things are going up. On his side are a port job, three kids, and a rent that keeps climbing.

"I walk past that fence every day," Mingo said. "The development's not for me — it's happening around me. I work at the port, I've got three kids, and I'm watching rents climb while the people I work with talk about leaving. The businesses are growing, the numbers look good, but the folks on the ledger aren't the ones who get to stay. I don't feel like I'm part of it. I feel like I'm watching it from the other side of the fence."

He is right about the numbers. This cycle the business ledger lists 18 named workers at five named businesses in Jack London, and every one of those businesses grew. The Port of Oakland still carries 1,501 people. Its revenue is $430.2 million, up $1.1 million since last cycle. When this desk wrote about the Port in C111, the figure was $425.1 million. The dock keeps getting louder and the money keeps coming in.

Anchor Build, the construction outfit, has 181 people on its payroll, and the ledger puts its growth at 13.7 percent. Revenue is $148.4 million, up $389,000 in a single cycle, and the average salary on its row is $80,000. Marguerite Salcedo, the managing partner, does not hide from those numbers. She also doesn't stop there.

"Look, I run the numbers every day," Salcedo said. "Anchor Build is growing — 13.7%, 181 people on the payroll. That's real. But I'd be lying if I said I don't see the squeeze. My crews — the electricians, the laborers, the folks swinging hammers — they're the ones building these new units, and half of them can't afford to live in the buildings they're putting up. That's not a talking point, that's what I hear at the job site. I've got guys driving in from Antioch, from Stockton, because Jack London rent went up again. We're not pricing out strangers — we're pricing out the people who pour the concrete. And that keeps me up at night, because I've been in this business long enough to know you can't build a neighborhood on the backs of people who can't stay in it."

The ledger names three people on her crews: Travis Golin, a construction laborer; Briar Soto, an electrician; and Kaila Brown, a construction worker. Salcedo describes a commute from Stockton before first light. The ledger has no column for that. It doesn't record gas money, the hours on the road, or the hours a kid spends without a parent at home. A payroll can grow while the people on it move farther away from the job, and both things can show up as success in the same set of books.

Down the waterfront, the money is smaller and moves more slowly. Green & Gold Tavern employs 30 people. Its revenue is $3.7 million, up $9,000 this cycle. The roster names Hector Leech in the dish pit, Trenton Nawan on the floor, Guadalupe Lupe behind the bar and Sora Hint at the espresso machine. Harborline Grill has seven people, including line cooks Quynh Le, Jalen Hill and Bruce Wright, plus Tomas Renteria, who splits his time between a podcast and the line. Its revenue rose $2,000, to $869,000, and the average salary on its row is $61,000. Blue Lantern Bar, where Yuki Ji and Guadalupe Lee tend bar, added $11,000 and now has 31 people.

Put those side by side. In one cycle the Port added $1.1 million and Green & Gold added $9,000. The kitchens that feed the dock crews and the bars where those crews drink after a shift grow at about the same rate as the Port on paper. In dollars, though, they grow at a fraction of its size. The people working those kitchens and bars live under the same rent as the port workers.

Elle Lilo has spent thirty-nine years on the water as a harbor tugboat captain at the Port. Over that time she has watched people quietly do the math on whether they can stay.

"I've been on that water for thirty-nine years, and I've watched this neighborhood grow up around the port," Lilo said. "The numbers going up—the cranes, the new buildings—that's real. But I'll tell you what I see from the wheelhouse: the same faces showing up at the union hall, the same guys on the dock at 5 a.m. Tre Mingo's one of them. The work's steady, but the cost of living here keeps climbing, and I watch good people do the math on whether they can stay. I moved myself, just a few miles, to make it work. That's the part nobody puts on a spreadsheet—the quiet decisions people make every day to keep doing the job they love in the place they grew up."

In C111 she told this desk that the Port's growth hadn't changed her day-to-day much. Five cycles later, her answer is that it changed where she sleeps.

Not every ledger on the waterfront is rising. Over in the Baylight District, Marine Systems shed three workers this cycle and Oakland Casino Town shed two. That is a separate neighborhood and a separate problem. It does show that "growth" is not a word that covers the whole shoreline.

My mother ran a laundromat on 7th Street until the building sold. On paper the block did well after that. She was gone from it all the same.

The ledger says Jack London is growing. Salcedo, Lilo and Mingo all agree with that. What the ledger can't answer is who gets that extra $1.1 million at the Port and that extra $389,000 at Anchor Build, and whether any of it gets back over the fence to a port worker with three kids who walks past it every day. The businesses have reported their numbers. Nobody has yet reported what happens to the workers.
