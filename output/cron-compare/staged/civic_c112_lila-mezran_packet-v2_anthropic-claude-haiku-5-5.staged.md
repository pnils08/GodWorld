# Six Names for 142 Sick Residents

Matra Woven works the early shift in Chinatown, and she knows the hallways by the sound they make. On the morning this cycle's record was closing, she mopped the same stretch twice because a neighbor stopped her at the door to ask about a grandson's dog. The dog was fine. The grandson was walking it because his grandmother could not. Matra leaned on the mop handle and said it plainly, the way people here talk about each other:

"I see the same faces every day, and lately some of them aren't there. It's not like I keep a list, but you notice when Mrs. Chen's grandson is the one walking her dog, or when the usual guy at the corner store has his daughter running the register. I don't know who's in the hospital beds, but I know who's covering for someone at home. That's just how it goes around here — family steps in, neighbors help out, and we don't always ask why."

Her account matches the count. Chinatown carries the most sick residents of any neighborhood in the record this cycle: 142, two fewer than last cycle, from a population the neighborhood demographics layer puts at 2,193. That works out to 6.5 percent of residents, above the 6 percent watch line. The next-highest neighborhoods run close behind. East Oakland has 123, Ivy Hill 121, Fruitvale 115, San Antonio 111, Laurel 109, Grand Lake 106, and Rockridge 105. Across all 22 neighborhoods in the record, 1,874 residents are counted as sick. Chinatown's figure is the largest of them, and it is not the figure that moved most.

Then the count narrows, and the room gets smaller.

The hospital census for Chinatown shows three admissions this cycle and six residents in care at the close, occupying six beds. None of those six is tracked by name in the record. The census gives the neighborhood a number and nothing more. The citywide hospital census, which the cycle summary reports alongside a city illness rate of 4.3 percent, shows 36 admissions and 73 residents in care at the close. Chinatown's six are a small part of that total. They are also the part of the total the record cannot attach to a person.

The names that do appear are not Chinatown names. Yu Zen is listed in Rockridge and was admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. Deacon Seymour is listed in Lake Merritt and was admitted in the same cycle after a workplace accident. Maurice Franklin, of Rockridge, was admitted in cycle 106 with severe seasonal flu. All three remain in hospital care as active cases on the ledger. Between them they account for three of the six residents the cycle record names on its hospital and cause entries. The other three named residents in the record are Jonas Patel, listed in Chinatown with a seasonal health concern, and two Jack London residents, Elle Lilo and Elena Leon, who the cycle summary says cooked healthier meals all week and felt the difference. The summary does not say more about either of them.

Yu Zen wrote out the problem from a hospital bed in Rockridge, where he works as a commercial insurance broker. He spends his days with claims and forms, and he said the forms and the beds do not carry the same names:

"When I was in that hospital bed, I kept thinking about the numbers I deal with every day as an insurance broker — the claims, the paperwork, the names on the forms. But lying there, I realized the names on the forms aren't the same as the names on the beds. Chinatown's been hit so hard, and I know it's not because those folks matter less. It's just that nobody's writing their names down the same way. I'm lucky — I've got people who can advocate for me, who know how to work the system. Not everyone has that. That gap isn't about who's sicker. It's about who gets seen."

Deacon Seymour, the Lake Merritt manager of the Oakland A's, has been in the same position. He was admitted in the same cycle as Yu Zen and speaks from his own hospital stretch. What he saw from the bed was the difference between being named and being counted:

"I spent my own stretch in a hospital bed not long ago, and I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you. But I also know that bed I was in—that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city right now, and I can't help but think about all those people who are just a number on a chart, not a face anyone's asking about. That gap, it's not about who's sicker—it's about who gets seen. And that weighs on me."

Kenji Tanaka, senior pastor in Chinatown, sees the people the census does not. He knows the grandmother who still folds flyers and the man who waves from his doorway, and he has learned that the count and the street disagree about who is ill. He said the people who are hardest to count are the ones who are least likely to ask for help:

"The numbers tell us how many are sick, but they don't tell you about the quiet ones. I see the faces of people who don't want to be a burden — the grandmother who still shows up to help fold flyers even though she's been coughing for weeks, the man who waves from his doorway but won't come to the community meal because he doesn't want to answer questions about his health. The city counts beds and cases. I count the people who are too proud, too scared, or too tired to be counted."

The record does not say whether any of the people Tanaka describes are among the 142, or among the six in care. It does not say why the admissions are as low as they are, or whether the hospital census reflects everyone who has been admitted. Those are questions for the people who keep the ledgers and run the census. For now, the cycle record holds a neighborhood with the most illness in the city, three admissions, six beds, and no named resident in the Chinatown hospital count.

Matra Woven finished the hallway and went back to the stairs. She did not know the names of the people in the beds. She knew who was at home.

What does it mean for a neighborhood to carry the most illness but have no one named in the hospital record?
