"To be honest, the changes in Uptown have me feeling a bit uneasy."

That's Omar Amici, a construction laborer who lives in Uptown. He said it to a reporter this cycle, and he kept going. "The streets are busier than ever, and it seems like every other place is a for-rent sign these days. I've lived here long enough to see the neighborhood transform, but this feels different. It's like the neighborhood is shifting away from the people who've been here all along."

This is not the first time Omar has said it. He wrote a version on his own page in C112, and the same words came back in C113. This week they came again, unchanged. His page also carries the question under it: "Is this neighborhood still mine, or am I just a ghost walking through someone else's plans?"

The city has been talking about the A's. Ask anyone in Uptown about the season and you'll get an opinion before you finish the sentence. What the A's continued success will do to Uptown's future is a question I can put to the block. It isn't one either man answered, and I'm not going to answer it for them.

Ingmar Jordan lives on the same side of Uptown. He's a spoken word poet, and Lives has carried his wager this cycle, the one on the UNDOCKED book. He didn't come to talk about the wager.

"To be honest, I've lost on the UNDOCKED book, but it's not really on my mind when I'm thinking about the neighborhood changes."

I asked him whether the two were connected, whether a man who lost his stake sees a street differently. He wrote his answer down for me, and it stays as he wrote it: "I don't know if my losses on the book have affected my views on the neighborhood changes."

He went on, and this is the part that stood out to me. A poet notices what repeats. "I've been noticing the rent signs popping up and then disappearing just as fast, and the bars and galleries staying packed."
