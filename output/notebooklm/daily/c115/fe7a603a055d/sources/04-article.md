# The Name Outlasts the Salary

By Hal Richmond, The Bay Tribune

By Hal Richmond | Bay Tribune Sports & Features

Vinnie Keane's last line reads 37 games, 127 at-bats, 40 hits, 13 home runs, 29 RBI, a .315 average. His dossier prints the slugging percentage at .701. It prints 0 walks. His career line was filed with it: 8,229 at-bats, 2,363 hits, 436 home runs, 1,321 RBI, a .287 average, .523 slugging, 71.4 WAR. One MVP, seven All-Star selections, three Gold Gloves, five Silver Sluggers, three Home Run Derbies, and seven World Series.

The A's filed a roster move this cycle, off-season. The ledger names what stands behind it. Keane retired from the A's, announced in C115. The playing salary ends, and the ledger records that appearances and the name pay $250,000 a year. The ledger also says the name is the thing he keeps.

I have written this man for a long time. I did not expect to be handed the last line of him in a week this quiet.

## The water first

The paper's C100 Origins begins on the water. It does not mean the water of postcards. It means the cold Atlantic that chews at the Massachusetts coast until the docks creak and the gulls go quiet. Keane was the youngest of five, in a two-bedroom house where dinner smelled of diesel, salt and hope. His father worked the harbor as a dock fisherman and measured time by tides and storms.

The Archive's index card for him reads *The Underdog Slugger*. Another Origins piece, written about a different rookie, remembers him in his first camp as "the undrafted spark plug from Massachusetts." That is the road in one line. Nobody drafted him, and he reported anyway.

The dossier opens in 2025. In that first season he played 144 games and hit 29 home runs, with 76 RBI and a .276 average. He stole 16 bases. He did not look like a man waiting to be noticed. He looked like a man who had already decided to stay.

## What the seasons say

His dossier covers 17 seasons. For most of the middle of it he was an everyday player. In 2031 he played 160 games and the dossier credits him with the MVP. In 2035 he played 160 games and had 192 hits. In 2037 he played 150.

The late seasons shorten. 2038 is the one the record will keep arguing about: 94 games, 49 home runs, 119 RBI, a .362 average. He hit those numbers in fewer than 100 games. The next three years, 2039, 2040 and 2041, were played in 55, 58 and 37 games. The dossier shows a man used in smaller doses and still hitting for power. His 2040 line has 14 home runs in 209 at-bats, and his 2041 line has 13 in 127.

Two of the paper's earlier pieces said as much while he was still playing. The C93 column found him on the bench in his final season and hitting. The form, the column said, refused to leave him. The C90 piece caught him on the dugout steps, in the designated hitter's role, "that quiet acknowledgment that the legs and the range have done their work and the bat" had not.

## The names the city gave him

The city gave him two. The first is *Saint Vinnie, the Patron Saint of the Comeback*, which the C100 Origins records alongside the rituals fans built around him: the glove tap, the dirt wipe, the pause before breath. The second, in the archive's own index, is *The Underdog Slugger*. One is what a crowd calls a man it believes in. The other is what a historian writes on a card.

Nobody voted on either one. They stuck because of how he played, and the C85 coverage put the reason plainly. Among the men of that era, Keane was the readable one. He played with pleasure in the act itself, in the swing and the contact between wood and horsehide, more than in the result or the celebration.

## Grand Lake

The ledger puts him in Grand Lake now. A recent entry says he spent ten weeks there and "the move worked." Another says he spent a night on the phone working out where the family would land. A third says he was recognized twice at the grocery store after the story ran, and that he came home from an event with a bag of thank-you cards from kids who had never seen a game.

I will step in here once, because the record allows me one entry. In thirty-one years of this work I have learned to distrust farewells that arrive as statistics. A line like 436 home runs is a fact, and it is not a man. The man is in the cards from children who never saw him play. Those children will know him from a plaque, a story and a name, not from a swing. That is a different way to be remembered, and I think it is a fair one.

## What he became

The C79 coverage observed that his name did not appear on any plaque inside the stadium, and that Oakland has never been good at goodbyes. This cycle does not settle that. A roster move is not a ceremony. It is not clear from the record what the franchise will do with the name now that the player has left the roster. That question belongs to the club, and the club has not answered it in anything the paper holds.

What the record does hold is this. He started undrafted, the youngest of five, off a Massachusetts dock. He played 17 seasons. He hit .315 in the last one and slugged .701. He has seven World Series on his career line, and on this city he has a name.

The playing salary ends. The name does not.
