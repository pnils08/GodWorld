**West Oakland Stabilization Fund Moves $81,188 This Cycle as $23.4 Million Remains on Ledger**

*By Carmen Delaine*

The build-out of the flats in West Oakland has not paused for the stabilization grants promised to the remnant left behind. On blocks where new framing rises over lots cleared in cycles past, long-tenure residents still pass construction fencing that went up and never came down. Plywood barriers announce future units where older housing once stood, and the only city money visibly at work is a patch of fresh asphalt and a posted notice for a disbursement program residents have learned to track by rumor rather than mailbox. The funds meant to soften displacement arrive in tranches large enough to read as commitment on the ledger and small enough to vanish against the scale of the rents rising around them.

The West Oakland Stabilization Fund, Initiative INIT-001, passed the council in Cycle 78 on a 6-to-3 vote. Ramon Vega voted yes; Leonard Tran voted no; Elliott Crane logged a yes by remote. Mayor Avery Santana, who proposed the measure, signed it that same cycle, locking in a $28 million promise. But promises on the tracker do not pay rents on the street. By Cycle 108, the scope had been recorded as chartered: not blanket relief for the neighborhood, but stabilization grants aimed specifically at long-tenure residents priced out as the flats built out. That narrowing redefined the fund from a general seawall into a targeted valve. Under Deputy Mayor Okoro, 47 checks totaling roughly $4.2 million had gone out by then—a disbursement that suggests either deep grants to a few or modest ones to many, a distinction the public record does not yet resolve. Either reading leaves the bulk of the $28 million pool untapped.

Cycle 109 brought the month-eight release. The Initiative Tracker logs a $400,000 tranche formally released into West Oakland. Against that, it records exactly two household grants, combining for $81,188. The tracker does not list where the remaining $318,812 of the tranche rests, nor does it detail the criteria that filtered 47 prior recipients down to two current ones. There is no line item for administrative hold, pending application, or encumbrance. What the ledger does show is the balance line: $23.4 million remains citywide, meaning roughly $4.28 million has been deployed since the fund’s inception, a rate that would stretch full exhaustion well past the pace of the build-out it was designed to slow.

The arithmetic raises a question the record cannot yet answer. If the charter’s narrow scope was intended to sharpen the fund’s impact, the record so far shows sharpening without striking. A program holding nearly twenty-four million dollars in stabilization money—explicitly earmarked for the residents most exposed to this build-out—moved only $81,188 to households this cycle while the physical transformation of the flats continued apace. The gap between appropriation and delivery is not new in city initiatives, but here it is measurable in actual checks against the pace of actual permits.

The next hard beat on the clock is fixed. A month-nine disbursement review is scheduled for Cycle 110. That review will face a ledger in which the bulk of the original appropriation sits untouched. It will need to explain not only who qualifies under the remnant criteria, but why a program built to move at the speed of displacement has spent less than a sixth of its balance through month eight of active disbursement. The build-out will not wait for the accounting. Whether the remaining $23.4 million reaches the street before the street finishes changing is the gap Cycle 110 must close.
