# The Lives of C115

Vinnie Keane came home from the event with a bag of thank-you cards from kids who had never seen a game. That was this cycle, the one in which the A's announced his retirement. The record calls him a Former Designated Hitter and Oakland A's Legend, and puts him in Grand Lake. It says the playing salary ends and that appearances and the name now pay $250,000 a year. It does not say what came next.

On the same cycle the grocery store recognized him twice, after the story ran. The record also holds a C114 Tribune wire desk piece that named him in a hard light, and "When a Name Fades, Who Still Says Hello?" ran in Culture the same cycle. The two lines don't agree, and the record doesn't settle which one he lived on. In Y3C10 he had been in Grand Lake ten weeks, and the ledger said the move had worked. In C115 he spent a night on the phone working out where the family would land. What the calls decided is not on the record.

He did speak for himself. "After retiring from the A's, I've been keeping busy with appearances and staying involved with the community," Keane said. "I still get recognized around Grand Lake, which is nice. My wife Amara and I are enjoying our time together, and I'm looking forward to seeing what's next for the A's, especially with the catcher situation being a topic of discussion."

Elle Lilo retired in C115 as a Harbor Tugboat Captain out of Jack London. The ledger says she stepped back from professional life. The Tribune had named her three times in civic pieces, C112 and C113, and this page wrote her new household in C114.

She had a full week behind the retirement. She took coffee from the cart outside the hospital before the morning rush hit. She stayed thirty minutes after a service, catching up in the parking lot. She left the playoff watch party hoarse, with sticky shoes.

Asked about Keane, she talked about herself first. "I know how hard it is to adjust to not being on the job every day," Lilo said. "I retired recently too, and it's been...interesting. The alarm still goes off at 4:30, old habit. But now I get to enjoy that first cup of coffee without the rush. I think Vinnie's probably figuring out his own new routine. We don't talk about it much, but I can relate."

Marguerite Salcedo was promoted at Anchor Build in C115, after nine years as Managing Partner. The ledger still lists her as Managing Partner, Anchor Build, Piedmont Ave. The new title is not on the record.

The same cycle she drove the church van on the senior trip and listened to three hours of unfiltered neighborhood gossip. She realized she was becoming more like an older relative, and didn't mind. During a choir solo she closed her eyes and felt the tension leave her shoulders. Her last Tribune mention was C111: "The Port Got Louder. Down the Block, the Ledger Barely Moved."

"I've seen Vinnie Keane around Grand Lake a few times since he retired," Salcedo said. "He seems to be enjoying his post-baseball life, taking it easy, and spending time with family. I've noticed he's still got that same energy, just not on the field anymore."

Richie Ramiro, First Baseman for the Midland RockHounds (AA), moved into a place of his own in Uptown in C115. He is renting. The record has him in the C114 cycle painting over graffiti on the recreation center wall beside five neighbors he barely knew. That same cycle he spent too long in a bookstore aisle he'd never stopped at. In C115 he grabbed coffee from the cart outside the hospital, near a late-night counter.

"I don't know him personally," Ramiro said of Keane, "but you can tell he's still a baseball guy at heart. I've heard he's been to some A's games, which isn't surprising. He's a legend, after all."

Tobert Lewis, a Civil Rights Attorney, moved into a place of his own Downtown in C115, renting. In C113 the ledger recorded that his last debt had cleared and that it finally read clean. In C114 he badged into the Baylight office early, trying not to think about the protestors from last month. He also read a flyer for the Oakland Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline and wondered who was actually going to get those spots. In C115 he finally fixed the thing that had been broken for months. The record doesn't say what it was.

Two households crossed into West Oakland this cycle. Laila Cortez and Cedric Nakamura, a household of two, moved from Uptown. Cortez stood in a line at the Uptown corner store that turned into a small crowd with the same complaint. Nakamura bought a new A's jersey and counted what was going right for once. He also attended a community feedback session for the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II and felt heard. Xavier Ramington moved from Temescal. Through an open window he heard the block erupt when the final play landed. He also saw an Oakland Alternative Response Initiative team de-escalate a situation quietly on a corner.

On the UNDOCKED book, Farah Lindqvist staked 529 Downtown, and the book kept it. In C114 she said no to something and felt the shape of who she is in it. In C115 she recognized the OARI van idling outside the corner store and knew better than to ask who it was waiting for. Darius Scoot, in Rockridge, staked 478 and was paid 875. The same cycle he traded produce over a fence and came out ahead. He waved at the regulars on his usual route, all present and accounted for.

Dalton Rushing, in Chinatown, loved the A's on principle and still cleared the night for UNDOCKED. He took a call about a future partnership opportunity. Illy Jile, also in Chinatown, watched the bar go silent for UNDOCKED the way it used to for a late A's lead. Another morning she read the whole paper front to back near the market street, with her coffee going cold beside it.

Camila Osman, in Chinatown, read the flyer about the Fruitvale Transit Hub delays and shook her head at the timeline. Darrin Davis, on Piedmont Ave, saw his name in the sports pages again. He stayed an extra hour at the community center because nobody wanted to leave. Rosa Foster, in Rockridge, thought about an old friend on the walk home. The record does not say which friend.
