# Three Programs on a Block That Keeps Counting Itself

The Postpartum Oakland Network sits in Fruitvale, and Lucia Polito founded it. It is one of three programs the record holds for the neighborhood. The other two are the Fruitvale Summer Speed Series, a youth-sports outfit, and Fruitvale Transit Corridor Mutual Aid. All three have been on the books since cycle 101. That is not long, and a block can tell the difference between a program and a program that has stayed.

Polito is listed as an Aura Wellness Practitioner. When I asked what the network has meant, she did not give me a pitch. "I've seen the impact that community programs like the Postpartum Oakland Network, which I founded, can have on people's lives," she said. "It's amazing to see the difference these programs make in Fruitvale. As someone who's dedicated to wellness and has been a part of this community for a while, I feel grateful to see the positive changes happening around us."

I believe the gratitude. I also notice that she is the one holding it. A founder sees the room from the front, where the work is.

Rick Walker sees it from somewhere else. He teaches K-8 and used to work the line in a kitchen, so he knows what it is to be on your feet for other people's comfort. Asked whether the changes were landing, he took longer to answer.

"To be honest, it's getting harder to feel that way," Walker said. I don't know if it's enough, though."

That is the block in one breath. There is a poster on a wall, a mercado with its lunch rush, and people changing trains. Under all of it sits a rent number that does not care how many posters went up. Walker also said the teachers elsewhere work just as hard as the ones in Fruitvale, "but they're doing it with less." He never says who holds the less. He doesn't have to.

Gregory Mims is blunter about how long good news lasts. "Fruitvale's still a ways off for a lot of folks around here, you know?" he said. He says he stands by the programs, and he admits the road there was contentious. I'd like to hear what supporting them looks like once the reflecting is done.

Now the counting. This cycle Fruitvale holds 423 students, 1,559 adults and 351 seniors. Against cycle 112 that is two more students, seven more adults and one more senior. Ten people is barely a shift. It is the kind of movement a block feels as one new name on a mailbox, or one gone. The hospital census for Fruitvale shows 114 sick residents, two admissions, and four people in care at the close, in four occupied beds. The judicial census shows six charges, one arrest, and three people in custody. None of those people are tracked individually, so I can't tell you who they are. I can tell you the block has them. Someone on this block has a bed they aren't in and a chair they aren't sitting in.

Here is where the three programs have to prove their worth. A postpartum network, a youth speed series and a mutual-aid group working the transit corridor have nothing to do with a ledger. They are about who comes by, who carries the groceries and who keeps a kid running. Whether they reach the four beds or the three in custody, I do not know. Whether they reach the person paying the new rent, I know even less.

# Rockridge: The Reporter Who Won't Call It Remarkable

She keeps coming back to the aisles. Tara Ellison, a journalist covering Rockridge, talks about them the way you talk about a room you know by sound: the carts knocking, the shoppers doubling back for the one thing they forgot, the same faces on the same errands. "I've been noticing the usual things in Rockridge, like the busy aisles at Canopy Market," she says. "I don't feel like I've been working on anything particularly remarkable or influential lately."

It is a strange thing to hear from someone whose name is climbing. The record has Tara Ellison as a journalist in Rockridge whose fame is rising fast, surging rather than drifting, and she is describing herself as ordinary, a reporter doing the work in front of her. Both things can be true at once. Most of what makes a local name is ordinary work done on a schedule, repeated until the repetition becomes a habit people notice.

She does not hide the friction. "Recently, a manager at Bay Tribune asked me to redo some work that had already been approved, which was a bit frustrating." She says it flatly, the way someone reports weather they did not choose. A piece that was signed off and then sent back is a small, specific thing. It is also the kind of thing a rising reporter learns about first: that the approval is not the end, that the story keeps going after the byline is set, and that other people are still deciding what it says.

Think about what a rise looks like from the street. It is not a premiere or a crowd at a door. It is the same byline turning up on a phone at a checkout counter, a neighbor who says *I read that one*, a question at the market about a council item from last week. Fame in a neighborhood like this is built out of local news, and local news is built out of showing up to the same meetings and the same aisles until the people who shop there start to recognize the reporter as part of the block.

That is the interpretation, and it is only that. The record shows the climb. It does not show why the climb is happening, and it does not show what Ellison thinks the climb means. Her own account points away from any single breakthrough and toward routine, which may be the most honest reading available. A reporter who keeps covering the market, the meetings, and the daily events of Rockridge is building a kind of trust that the ledger cannot count.

So the question that stays open is the one the city will have to answer over time. Is Tara Ellison's rise a product of the stories she files, the people who read them, or simply the number of times she has walked the same aisles and asked the same questions? And when a reporter says she is not doing anything remarkable, is that modesty, or a fair description of the work that has made her hard to ignore?

Evening settles over the market. Somebody at the register holds up a phone to show a friend a headline, and the friend nods without looking up from the bags. Nobody in the aisle is thinking about fame. They are thinking about dinner. Whatever Rockridge is becoming, it is becoming there, in the ordinary traffic of people who have started to know whose name is on the page.

# The Names That Stay on the Wall

On Piedmont Ave, Lena Cross is talking about what happens when a neighborhood’s memory keeps an older version of someone alive.

“To be honest, I've noticed Marisol Rivera's name popping up less often in conversations around Piedmont Ave, and when it does, it's usually in the context of looking back at her past work,” Cross says. “I've seen her flyers and posters around, but they seem a bit outdated. People still speak highly of her community work, though. I think she's done some great things, but it feels like she's not as actively involved as she used to be.”

There is a particular tenderness in that account. It does not erase Rivera. It separates recognition from immediacy: the good work is remembered, while the person’s present-tense place in neighborhood conversation has become harder to locate.

Rivera is based in Rockridge and works in civic life. Her standing is regional, and her name is cooling. Cross, an actor whose own name is also fading from the current conversation on Piedmont Ave, recognizes the pattern from close enough to describe it without turning it into a verdict. A flyer can remain visible after the shared habit of discussing the person has thinned out. A poster can preserve a reputation while leaving open the question of what connection looks like now.

That distinction matters because fame is not one thing in a city. Some names become larger than their neighborhoods. Marin Tao, in Chinatown, is currently an iconic arts name, while Rico Valez holds the same citywide force from Fruitvale. Their names travel. Rivera’s, by contrast, seems to be carried more locally, through remembered civic work and the physical traces Cross describes.

Rowan Pierce, a journalist on Piedmont Ave, is also in the fading file. The pattern is not limited to one profession or one kind of public life. But Rivera’s case asks a more intimate question: when a community advocate becomes less present in everyday talk, does the community feel the absence, or does the memory of past work become its own form of continuing relationship?

I would not call a quieter name a finished story. Cross’s words leave room for admiration, uncertainty and the possibility that public attention is a poor measure of civic value. The outdated flyer is not proof that Rivera’s work has ended; it is evidence only of a gap between what the neighborhood remembers and what it can presently see.

The harder account is still needed from the people who live with that gap: how does Marisol Rivera’s fading visibility affect the way her community understands, recalls or continues her advocacy?
