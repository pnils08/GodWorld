### **The Sea-Level Desk Is Down to Three**
*By Jordan Velez, Business Desk*

At **Marine Systems** on the edge of the Baylight District, sea level monitoring technician **Mina Linas** is now one of only three workers left on staff after the company shed two positions this cycle [1]. The reduction represents a 40 percent staff cut, eliminating roughly \$360,000 in annual payroll for the specialized office [1]. Linas noted that the departure happened abruptly without explanation, leaving her as the sole technician tracking shoreline water levels while the rest of the team vanished [1, 2]. 

Directly surrounding the contracting environmental office, corporate technology and development outfits continue rapid expansion [3]:
*   **Anthropic** increased revenue to \$649.8 million (adding \$335,000 this cycle) across 86 employees, including AI safety researchers **Kelvon Ochoa**, **Iggy Davis**, and **Linda Chow**, and security guard **Broderick Mitchell** [3].
*   **DigitalOcean** expanded to 800 employees and \$521.4 million in revenue, up \$525,000 in a single cycle [3].
*   **Baylight Construction Authority** employs 451 workers under Director **Keisha Ramos**, generating \$31.3 million with trades including historic preservation carpenter **Brianna Lee**, repair technician **Tariq Jello**, and solar installation lead **Talmon Lint** [4]. Lee observed the cuts from the scaffolding, while Ramos emphasized that the loss of two jobs directly affects working families and removes vital scientific monitoring [5, 6].
*   **Oakland Casino Town** edged down to 99 employees under manager **Philly Rodriguez**, bookmaker **Manfred Owens**, and **Gregory Mims** [7].

Across town in Ivy Hill, neighborhood storefronts experienced parallel contractions, with **Ivy Hill Cafe**, **Ivy Cleaners**, **Ivy Hill Family Dental**, and **Hillcrest Kitchen** each shedding two workers [8]. Velez reflected that specialized outfits like flood monitoring and neighborhood laundromats are often the first to disappear, because their essential public value—providing warnings before the bay water rises—is rarely captured on a commercial balance sheet [9, 10].

### **Chinatown's Sick Are Counted by the Hundred and Named by No One**
*By Dr. Lila Mezran, Health & Civic Desk*

Chinatown carries **135 sick residents**, remaining the highest illness count of any neighborhood in Oakland at 6.1 percent of its population, ahead of East Oakland (117), Ivy Hill (115), and Fruitvale (109) [11]. While the count has slowly receded from 142 in Cycle 112, 140 in Cycle 113, and 137 in Cycle 114, Chinatown has remained at the top of the city's health logs throughout every reading [11]. 

Despite that concentration of illness, the neighborhood hospital census recorded only three admissions this cycle, with six residents occupying hospital beds at the close [12]. The remaining 129 sick residents are cared for inside private homes [13, 14]:
*   **Akua Adeyemi**, a Chinatown plumber, described the quiet adjustments families make across the neighborhood, keeping her tools low so as not to disturb sleeping households [15]:
    > *"I go into a lot of homes, and you can tell pretty quick when someone's down... It's not the mess or the dishes — it's the quiet. The TV off in the middle of the day. A chair pulled up by the window where somebody's been sitting a long time. Sometimes the medicine gets moved to the kitchen counter so it's right there where someone'll remember it... People here take care of their own — you'll see a neighbor bring soup, or a cousin come by after their shift. It's not always the hospital that carries the load. It's the family that rearranges the whole house around one sick person."* [13, 15]
*   **Jax Caldera**, a freelance accountability writer living in Chinatown, emphasized that neighbors and herbal shopkeepers track who is sick long before any public health office takes notice [16].
*   **Deacon Seymour**, manager of the Oakland Athletics who remains hospitalized following an earlier workplace accident alongside **Yu Zen** and **Maurice Franklin**, warned about the danger of uncounted illness [17, 18]:
    > *"I spent my own stretch in a hospital bed not long ago, and I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you. But I also know that bed I was in—that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city, and if the ledger doesn't show them, that doesn't mean they're not there. It means nobody's counting. And when nobody's counting, people get missed."* [18]

### **The Mechanics of Release: John Ellis and the Arithmetic of Laurel**
*By Anthony Raines, Special Feature*

On the rear patio of a rented craftsman bungalow on 39th Avenue in Laurel, Athletics starting pitcher **John Ellis** (25) sits rolling a scuffed regulation baseball between his palms [19, 20]. An elite cornerstone who threw 185 innings across 32 starts in 2040 and earned 20 wins in 2041, Ellis pitches on a \$780,000 pre-arbitration deal and pays \$2,150 a month to a landlord who hasn't fixed the sticky back door [20-22]:
> *"People read the sports pages and assume every guy who threw six innings in the World Series drives a six-figure coupe. Then I walk down MacArthur to buy milk and the guy behind the counter asks me if I’ve got enough quarters for the coin laundry. I tell him I’m good. It keeps your feet on the concrete."* [23]

Ellis's daily life is anchored in the physical fabric of Laurel [24]:
*   **Sidewalk Friction:** Confronting municipal liaison **Gordon Tate** on a downtown sidewalk, Ellis pressed why public funding flows smoothly toward downtown stadium amenities while neighborhood facilities like the **Laurel Recreation Center** suffer from rotted dugout roofs and cracked asphalt [25, 26].
*   **Community Grounding:** Ellis channeled that frustration into neighborhood action alongside **Rosalind Young**, owner of the MacArthur Boulevard smoke shop [27, 28]. The two spent hours together rolling gray primer over graffiti on the 42nd Avenue perimeter wall, talking about bee navigation and tobacco tax stamps, while AC Transit driver **Evan Lewis** steered his bus past in quiet recognition [28-30].
*   **The Decision to Stay:** While looking at listings in Rockridge where his trajectory would allow him to buy an estate, Ellis chose to remain in his Laurel rental, drawing perspective from quiet mornings on an east-shore bench at Lake Merritt [31]:
    > *"Out here in Laurel, nobody cares about your WHIP. They care if you painted the wall, if you waved at the bus driver, and if you sweep your own sidewalk. That's a good way to live. The rest is just sixty feet, six inches."* [32]

### **Nineteenth Street Filled Up, and the Rows Do Not Say Why**
*By Trevor Shimizu, Transit Desk*

Weekday station boardings at **19th St Oakland** surged to **16,076 riders**—an increase of **5,324 passengers** over the prior cycle—while maintaining an 81 percent on-time rate [33]. Across all eight tracked stations, total ridership rose 58 percent, carrying 73,938 passengers compared to 46,839 previously [34, 35]:
*   Platforms swelled across the network: **12th St City Center** reached 14,526 riders (up 4,515; 86% on-time), **West Oakland** climbed to 9,519 (up 3,491), **Fruitvale** hit 8,943 (up 3,880; 87% on-time), **Coliseum** rose to 7,776 (up 2,980), and **Lake Merritt** reached 6,780 [34].
*   Highway corridors mirrored the transit surge, with traffic ratings climbing sharply on I-580 East, I-880 North, I-880 South, and I-980 [35].
*   AC Transit operator **Coen Cartier** observed that the afternoon crowd at 19th Street was unusually dense, with riders funneling heavily toward Lake Merritt and Downtown [33].
*   BART PR representative **Ximena Mehta** noted that multiple neighborhood draws, sporting events, and downtown gatherings converged to fill platforms simultaneously [35]. 
*   However, Fruitvale Transit Hub planning lead **Elena Soria Dominguez** cautioned that design-phase planning cannot account for the surge, noting that transit workers on their breaks under the overpass witness crowds accumulating without clear logistical explanations [36].

### **Voices: What the A's Left Them**
*By Ines Sorensen, Culture Desk*

Ines Sorensen gathered reflections on how the Athletics' championship season reverberates through everyday life [37]:
*   **Alicia Hernandez (Retired Coffee Roaster, Uptown):** Experiencing her first mornings of retirement after decades of commercial shift work [37]:
    > *"The alarm didn't go off, and for a second I forgot and reached for it anyway. Then I just lay there, listening to the house. It's strange, the quiet. Forty-some years of waking to the smell of beans before I even had my coffee, and now I wake to the smell of my own sleep... I made myself a proper breakfast this morning, eggs and the good bread, and I ate it slow. I think that's the change—I'm learning to be a person instead of a schedule."* [37, 38]
*   **Pilar Ramirez (Paramedic, Lake Merritt):** Walking along the lake path with her husband Vincent, expressing fatigue with neighborhood workshops that produce little tangible change [38, 39]:
    > *"It's easy to put up a sign, harder to actually change things. And Paul's got his A's opinions, sure. But you know how that goes—everyone's a manager until opening day comes... Seen too many of them come and go over the years. It's always the same folks at the podium and the same folks in the chairs, and nothing really moves."* [38, 39]
*   **Mariana Kapoor (Student, Chinatown):** Recalling a neighborhood parade with local children while adults at the cultural center discuss rising rents and job security [40, 41]:
    > *"I keep thinking about how the grown-ups at the cultural center talk about things like rent and jobs, and I don't get all of it, but I feel it in my stomach. And then I think about that parade I made with the kids, and how for one second everyone was just laughing, and it felt like we were bigger than this block."* [41]
