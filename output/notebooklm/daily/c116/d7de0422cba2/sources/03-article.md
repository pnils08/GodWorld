# culture_c116_ines-sorensen_packet-v2_anthropic-claude-sonnet-5-5.staged.md

By Ines Sorensen, The Bay Tribune

"I walk past that fence every day. The development's not for me — it's happening around me."

Tre Mingo said that first, before the rest of it. He is a port worker who lives in Uptown. He put it on his page this cycle, to a reporter, and the next sentences came at the same pace, with no breath between them.

"I work at the port, I've got three kids, and I'm watching rents climb while the people I work with talk about leaving."

I have been taking down people's words for a long time. The line that matters usually comes at the end of the sentence, after the part they planned. Mingo's sentence had no planned part. It went from the fence to the port to the three kids to the rent without a pause, so I can't mark where the true thing starts. I've left it whole.

The paper has nothing earlier on him. No Tribune piece from before C116 came back when I searched his name, and the fence he walks past turned up nothing either. This is the first time he's in print. I won't guess what the fence is or who put it up. He didn't say, and the page doesn't.

He kept going, and this is the part I'd put beside the first.

"The businesses are growing, the numbers look good, but the folks on the ledger aren't the ones who get to stay. I don't feel like I'm part of it. I feel like I'm watching it from the other side of the fence."

Two other people on the record this cycle named him or stood where he stands.

Guadalupe Lupe tends bar in Jack London. She carries a question on her page, and she used his name in it: "How much longer can people like Tre Mingo afford to stay here before rent pushes them out?" She doesn't say she knows him, and I won't say she does. She read the same worry and put a name on it.

In the paper's C115 pages, Caroline Cabrera, who runs an after-school program in Uptown, asked it from the other side of the neighborhood: "How do the numbers capture what families in Uptown are really going through as rents keep climbing?" Mingo uses the word "numbers" too, and he gives them a place: they look good, and they sit on a ledger he isn't on.

Mingo's three kids are on his page and so is the port. Nothing on it says who has stopped talking and started leaving. "The people I work with talk about leaving" is all we have, and it is his sentence. I can't tell you how many of them, or when. I can only tell you he walks past the fence every day and says what he hears.

His page carries one more line, and it is a question. It is not an answer or a complaint.

"Will any of us who work here still be able to stay, or is this development happening without us?"
