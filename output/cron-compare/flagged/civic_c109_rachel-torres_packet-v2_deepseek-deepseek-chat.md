# Chinatown's Incident Lead Raises Questions About Clearance Rates

The steam from Jonas Patel's cart rose through the cold morning air as he rearranged jars of preserved plums behind the counter. His stall on Chinatown's Franklin Avenue sits in a neighborhood tied with four others for the most incidents this cycle—a pattern that has drawn both attention and concern.

"Working in Chinatown, I've noticed the streets feel busier lately with more incidents," Patel said, adjusting his apron. "But it's hard to say if that's just my perception or if it's really worse than other neighborhoods. The clearance rates seem low, which makes people nervous."

At the Lucky Dragon restaurant three blocks away, kitchen worker Ronald Williams prepped vegetables while reflecting on the neighborhood's activity. "I hear Chinatown's got more incidents than other spots lately, but honestly, at the restaurant we just keep our heads down and do our work. The numbers don't really change how we move through the day."

Laundromat attendant Jacquez Tearn stacked folded shirts near 9th Street. "Man, I hear Chinatown's got the most trouble right now, but I keep my head down, work my shift," he said. "Folks say the cops take their time clearing things up, but what can you do? Just keep moving, stay sharp."

The pattern raises questions about whether Chinatown's higher visibility—with its dense commercial corridors and street life—leads to more reports compared to other neighborhoods, or if there's a genuine gap in investigative resources. With no neighborhoods showing rising incident counts this cycle—and KONO seeing eased pressure—public safety analysts will track whether Chinatown's numbers reflect better reporting or need deeper attention. Who assesses that difference remains the open question when street-level perceptions and clearance rates don't align.
