# What They Say After the Part They Planned

Four people in Uptown talked to the Tribune this cycle about rent. Each of them said the planned part first. The sentence they meant came last, so I've left each answer whole.

**Tre Mingo** works at the Port and lives in Uptown. He has said the big thing before, in the weeks I've been listening to him. This time he said something smaller and about someone else.

"To be honest, I've seen some familiar faces moving out of Uptown. My coworker, Jamal, just told me he's looking for a new place because his rent just went up."

He didn't build a speech around it. The man is named, and the reason is one line long. Jamal's rent went up, so he's looking.

**Theo Banks** is a rapper. His neighborhood is his material, and he told me the rent talk reached him secondhand, over a fence.

"I heard it from my neighbor, she was talking about how she can't afford the new rent prices, and some people are already looking for places in other neighborhoods; after that, you hear the frustration, people talking about how they're gonna have to leave their community behind."

It's one sentence, and it moves from a neighbor to a number to a word he doesn't dress up. The word is "behind." On his page this cycle he began with how alive the streets are. The streets he described are the ones people are planning to leave.

**Caroline Cabrera** directs an after-school program in Uptown. She has told the Tribune before that numbers don't tell the whole story, so she gave me a person instead.

"When I see the kids and families at our after-school program, I see resilience and community, not just numbers on a page. This week, I saw a mom who had to take on a second job to make ends meet, but she still made it to our parent-teacher conference, beaming with pride over her kid's progress. It's the little moments like those that remind me of what's at stake."

The planned part is "resilience and community." What she actually saw was a woman with a second job who showed up anyway, beaming. She doesn't say whether the second job is how that family stays in Uptown or what it costs them to stay. I won't answer that for her.

**Elliott Crane** is the council member for District 6, which includes Uptown. His answer is the most carefully built of the four, and he says the sentence plainly.

"I'm always looking for ways to keep Uptown vibrant without pricing out long-time residents; it's a delicate balance between commercial vitality and community needs."

He has also written down the question he carries. It is shorter, and it doesn't claim a balance has been struck: "Can we keep Uptown residents from being priced out while our corridors stay vital?"

Put the two beside each other. The statement says he is looking for ways. The question admits he hasn't yet found one that holds.

---

Daniela Kapoor is a teacher who lives in Glenview, not Uptown. She wasn't asked about rent. I include her because of what she said about the neighborhood next door, late at night, with the TV on low. She said the block feels quieter than it should, and that the corner store has paper over its windows.

"It's like the neighborhood is holding its breath."

She was talking about her own street. I'm not claiming she was describing Uptown, and I'm not claiming she wasn't. It's the same kind of sentence the four above gave me, and it ended up in the same place.
