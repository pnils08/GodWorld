THE CYCLE PULSE — Y3C10
The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

Walk the week with me from the Coliseum platform to a wine bar on Grand Avenue, because the same handful of people kept turning up on the way.

Start with Evan Lewis, who drives a bus past the stadium every shift. Celeste Tran caught him on his route telling a passenger he hasn't seen much of the A's in West Oakland. He knows Arturo Ramos and John Ellis from around Laurel and says they're nice guys, but that's about it. Meanwhile Trevor Shimizu reported that the Coliseum station lost more riders than any other this cycle, on a weekend with no event, no construction, and no cause on the row to explain it. Jerald Jensen, the AC Transit janitor, reads it in the trash cans: fewer cups, fewer people lingering. Lewis and Jensen are describing the same emptiness from two sides of a bus door. The ledger can tell you how many people stopped showing up. Only the people who work the platform say what they stopped showing up for.

John Ellis is the other half of that picture. Anthony Raines laid out the extension question for him, and the case is more honest than the record. Ellis's win-loss line is the loudest thing on his card and the least reliable. Underneath it is a solid starter who improved, worth a team-friendly deal but not an ace's. That is a fair description of the whole neighborhood relationship too. Ellis is a name people know and a face a bus driver can place, but the loud number and the lived presence are different things. Both deserve pricing separately. Raines's pick of the four is Travis Coles, the one whose rates back up the record. Coles also turns up in Kai Marston's culture pages, which are about a block that treats its ballplayers as furniture. The pitcher whose walks fell and strikeouts rose is the one case where the street's affection and the spreadsheet agree. That's rare, and I'd keep an eye on it.

Austin Reaves is where the week's contradiction sits most plainly. P Slayer had him after the road loss, down twenty and fighting back, saying it felt like they'd left it on the road, proud of the response but not letting it go. That's a man fully inside his season. Then Maria Keen asked him about the Reaves Line Transit Fund, which he founded and which has been active in Temescal for some time, and he told her he isn't sure what it's doing there. He's been focused on the team and family, he lives in West Oakland, and he doesn't see its effects firsthand. I agree with Keen that it isn't an indictment. It's the truest sentence she collected. But a transit fund is a promise to people who ride, and the same week the Coliseum platform emptied, its founder couldn't say whether his fund was reaching anyone. Somebody should find out, whether it's him, his staff, or us. I'll be asking.

Then there's Vinnie Keane, who is quietly the week's connective tissue. Tran has his mentions climbing and two recognitions at the Grand Lake corner store. Tamsin Pruitt's ledger of lives has Tyler Filet, a biotech lab director a few weeks into a Lake Merritt rental, awake at night with the wind rattling the window frame, wondering about old decisions. Filet's one steadying thought was seeing Vinnie Keane at the Grand Avenue Wine Bar: "It's nice to have some continuity." The city is adding people by the truckload, and what keeps newcomers from feeling like strangers is a familiar face at a bar. The same man is a name the city can't stop saying and a fixture one lonely renter leans on. The attention is the loud part. The continuity is the part that matters.

Helena Voss-Adeyemi crosses us from the other side of the same growth. Jordan Velez has her running Northgate Construction, one of three West Oakland building outfits all up this cycle. His headline voice, though, is Enzo Walker, a plumber at the biggest of them, who sees the growth on the trailer board and says it doesn't match the street. He fixes pipes in places that didn't exist last year, for the tech firms moving in, while the people who used to live on those blocks are gone. In Keen's Temescal piece, Voss-Adeyemi is on the street too, walking north across the neighborhood line and watching the storefronts. The piece cut off before I could tell you what she concluded, so I won't. But I find it fitting: she's a person who signs the checks on the buildings Walker describes, and she's out looking at what the blocks are becoming. Whether she's looking the way Walker fears nobody does is the question I'd put to her directly. Keen also reported Temescal's adult population up by only a handful, a few leases and a couple of moving vans. Growth in this city usually isn't a wave. It's a steady drip that rearranges whose block it is.

That's what ties this week together for me. Brie Harris, the florist, has her own place in Temescal and is "still getting used to it." Derek Obi sold a house downtown and now rents in Rockridge, and the record calls it moving up while its own words say he's renting until buying is in reach. Graciela Foster moved to East Oakland for a better fit between income and housing costs. Every one of these is a person making their arithmetic work in a city that keeps changing the arithmetic. Even Nia Rook's UNDOCKED board has the same flavor: an electrician at the top, a janitor with a busy week, a daycare director who finally closed a positive night. Ordinary jobs, strange routes, and nobody quite sure what carried them.

What nobody has answered: why Coliseum emptied when no cause was listed, and whether it's settling or sliding. What the Reaves Line fund has actually done on its block. Whether the A's and Oaks will get out into West Oakland and Fruitvale the way Daniel Cloak says the Fruitvale Transit Hub session at least listened. And who is at the table when the buildings go up.

Next week I'm watching the Coliseum row first, and whether the platform comes back or settles lower. I'm watching the Ellis and Coles extension talk, to see whether the club prices what the rates support or what the record shouts. I'm chasing Reaves on his fund, and I'm waiting to hear what Voss-Adeyemi makes of Walker's complaint. And I'm watching whether the Oaks, after a loss they carried home, find the finish. Oakland doesn't need the fight alone. It needs what comes after it.

THE WEEK'S REPORTING

By Celeste Tran | Bay Tribune Wire

West Oakland added nineteen residents this cycle while Vinnie Keane’s mentions climbed to six. Henry Rivas, the starting pitcher who calls the neighborhood home, stood on the sidewalk outside his building watching a moving van unload another set of boxes. “The A's are a big part of my life, and I plan my days around the games,” he said to the new neighbor. “When I'm not pitching, I'm thinking about the next start or recovering from the last one. I've lived in West Oakland for a while now, and I see the neighborhood changing. More people are moving in, and it's getting livelier. I think the A's bring a sense of community and pride to the area. I'm proud to be a part of it, and I hope we can keep being a positive presence here.”

A few blocks over, the same pattern held. People who had never lived near the Coliseum before were now walking past it on their way to work. The mentions for Kai Tanaka, the Fruitvale artist, ticked up by one as well, though he kept his distance from the conversation. “To be honest, I haven't really thought much about The A's and their community engagement in West Oakland,” he told a friend over coffee. “I live in Fruitvale, and while I'm aware of the team, it's not something that's directly impacted my daily life. I mean, I know they're based in Oakland, but my connection to them is pretty casual. I do notice when there's a lot of activity around the city, and sometimes I hear conversations about the team, but it's not something I engage with regularly.”

Evan Lewis, the bus driver who passes the stadium every shift, was more blunt when the topic came up on his route. “To be honest, I haven't really seen much of The A's around West Oakland lately,” he said over his shoulder to a passenger. “I drive past the Coliseum all the time, and you can tell when there's a game or event going on, but it's not like they're out in the community that much. I mean, I've seen Arturo Ramos and John Ellis around Laurel, they're nice guys, but that's about it. I'm not sure how much they're really engaging with the community in West Oakland.”

Vinnie Keane, back in Grand Lake, had already fielded two recognitions at the corner store that week. The city’s attention keeps drifting toward the same handful of names even as entire blocks fill with new faces. The pattern leaves one open question hanging over the influx: how will the A's continue to shape the community in West Oakland as more people move in?

By Kai Marston | Bay Tribune Culture

# The Pole on Rockridge Corner Is Starting to Say His Name

The flyer on the pole outside the Tuesday Room has no face on it. It has a torn corner, a strip of staple, and a rain-stained edge that has held on longer than anyone meant it to. Two doors down, a man in a faded sweatshirt holds a paper cup in one hand and a boy's sleeve in the other. The boy keeps glancing back at the pole as if it owes him something.

Nobody on this stretch of Rockridge has to look for Benji Dillon. He pitches for the Oakland A's, and he has lived here long enough that the block treats him as part of its furniture. The culture ledger has him rising this cycle, the way it has 31 other figures rising, but on the sidewalk the trajectory looks less like a chart and more like a man who can't get a coffee without someone saying his name.

I asked him, in the only way the record gives me, what he makes of it. His answer, as it stands on the record:

"I'm not sure what's behind my growing presence in Rockridge. I live here, I've been part of the community for a while, and I'm just doing my thing. I've been around long enough that people know me, I guess. I'm just trying to be a good neighbor and a good dad to Rick."

He doesn't sound like a man performing modesty. He sounds like someone who has noticed the crowd has started to lean in, and who would rather talk about the kid than the crowd. The phrase *good dad to Rick* does more work in that sentence than the word *fame* ever could. It turns the question back toward the street, where a family walks past a bakery window and nobody stops to ask for a signature.

The rest of the A's are watching from a distance. Darrin Davis, a left fielder who lives on Piedmont Ave, has noticed the same thing from the dugout side:

"I've seen Benji Dillon's name popping up more often around the A's, and it's clear he's gaining traction. To be honest, I'm not super close to him, but you can tell when someone's rising through the ranks. I've been around long enough to recognize the buzz. It's like when I was breaking in – everyone's curious, and you're getting more attention. I think it's great for the team to have fresh talent making waves."

Davis hears the buzz as a thing that happened to him once, and he's gentle about it. Danny Horn, a center fielder from Temescal, is less interested in the chatter. He says he's focused on the club making a deep run, and then adds a line that matters for this beat:

"To be honest, I've not really thought much about Benji Dillon's rising fame. I've seen him around the clubhouse, he's a talented kid, but I don't know him well enough to say much about his personal story or what's behind his recent performances."

Travis Coles, a starting pitcher from Downtown, keeps it even shorter. "I know he's a teammate, but I don't really know him personally. I've seen his name in the papers, though."

Four teammates, four distances. One of them is his neighbor's kid's favorite. Another has been around long enough to read the crowd. Two know him only from the clubhouse and the papers. None of them is telling a story about how Rockridge itself is changing. They are telling stories about how close they stand to the man.

That gap is the real news here. Fame in this city does not arrive as a building or a bar opening. It arrives as a flyer that doesn't come down, a teammate who hasn't had time to ask, a father who would rather talk about his son than about what the sidewalk is doing. I have no record that his name has moved a single business on this block, and I won't pretend otherwise. What I can see is a neighborhood that has begun to notice a neighbor, and a man trying to stay a neighbor while it does.

The boy finally tugs his sleeve. They walk off toward the bakery. The pole keeps its torn flyer, and the corner keeps its quiet.

So who answers for what a rising name does to the people who share its street? The team talks about talent. The player talks about Rick. The sidewalk just keeps saying his name. How is Benji Dillon's rising fame affecting the Rockridge community, and who gets to say when it has?

# The Pantry Has Been Here Since C83. The Fund's Founder Lives Across the Bridge of Himself.

The Temescal Islamic Center Food Pantry has been on the record since C83. It is one of two active programs the city has on file for Temescal, and it is the older one by a long stretch. I can't tell you what was on the shelves this cycle, or who carried which bag out. The record doesn't say, and I won't paint a line of people I didn't stand beside. What the record does say is that it is still there, and on a block, that is most of what a pantry has to prove.

The second program is newer. The Reaves Line Transit Fund has been in Temescal since C109, and its founder is Austin Reaves, who plays shooting guard and lives in West Oakland. I asked him what the fund has done for the block, and his answer was the most honest sentence I collected this cycle:

"To be honest, I'm not really sure about The Reaves Line Transit Fund's impact on Temescal. I founded it, but I've been focused on the A's and family stuff lately. I know it's been around since C109, and it's still active, but I haven't been keeping up with its day-to-day. I live in West Oakland, so I don't really see its effects firsthand."

I don't hear that as an indictment. A man put his name and money behind a thing on a street he doesn't walk, and now he's telling the truth about the distance. It does leave a question the block deserves answered, and I'll come back to it.

Here is who the block is this cycle: 313 students, 2,131 adults, 298 seniors. Compared with C113, the adults are up by six. Six is not a wave. It is a couple of U-Hauls and a few people who signed a lease.

One of the people the block has gained, or kept, is Brie Harris, who owns a flower shop. She has a place of her own in Temescal now, and she said what that does for a person who spends her day on her feet: "Having my own place in Temescal is still a game-changer for me. It's been nice to have a spot to unwind after being on my feet all day at the flower shop. I feel like I'm still getting used to it, but it's made me appreciate the neighborhood even more."

*Still getting used to it.* I keep turning that over. Appreciation and unfamiliarity can live in one apartment, and probably a whole block lives that way.

Helena Voss-Adeyemi described the edge of that feeling from the street. "I walked north across the Temescal line and watched the storefronts change," she said. "It's always interesting to see the different vibes on either side. Temescal's got a unique energy, lots of new businesses popping up."

Then there is the picture Theo Park can't shake, from a line at Temescal Brewing. "To be honest, that moment at Temescal Brewing really stuck with me," he said. "Seeing the janitor from the Islamic Center chatting with Danny Horn like they were old friends... it felt like a small but real sign of how the neighborhood is evolving. It's not just about new faces moving in; it's about how we all connect. I still see Temescal as a place where you can bump into anyone, and that's something I appreciate."

I can't give you that scene firsthand. I can tell you why it lands. The pantry's people and the brewery's people are the same neighbors, and the program does not stop at its own door.

The block has its hard numbers, too, and I'll set them down plainly. The justice census shows eight charges in Temescal this cycle, two arrests, and three residents in custody at the close. The hospital census shows 86 sick residents, two admissions, and four people in care at the close, with four beds occupied. None of those people are tracked citizens, so I can't give you a name, a porch, or a story. They are other residents, which means they are somebody's neighbors, and I know nothing more about them than that.

I was asked to wonder whether the programs explain the growth or the quiet in those counts. I'm not going to say they do. Nothing I hold ties a pantry shelf to a hospital bed or a transit fund to six new adults. Claiming it would be me inventing a good ending for a block that hasn't offered me one.

What I'd rather know is smaller, and it's the kind of thing only a person on the block can answer. Who on these streets has ever used the fund that carries Reaves's name, and did it get them somewhere they needed to be? Has anyone who carries a bag home from the pantry also stood in that brewery line? Until someone who lives here tells me, the two programs are a founder's name and an old promise, and I'd like to hear the rest from the block.

# The Board in the Trailer Says 9.5 Percent. Enzo Walker Is Counting Something Else.

*By Jordan Velez, Business Reporter*

Enzo Walker is a plumber at Coastline Construction in West Oakland. His working day takes him past the board in the job-site trailer, where the company's numbers are posted where the crew can see them. This cycle the numbers are good. The Business Ledger lists Coastline at 221 employees, a 9.5 percent growth rate and $187.1 million in annual revenue, up $341,000 from last cycle. By any spreadsheet, the firm Walker works for is winning.

"Look, I see the numbers on the board in the trailer — 9.5% growth, revenue up, all that. And sure, we're busy. I'm not complaining about work," Walker said. "But the numbers don't tell you what it's like on the ground. The buildings going up, they're not for us. They're for the tech folks coming in, the ones from Oakmesh and Civis. I fix pipes in places that didn't exist last year, and the people who used to live on those blocks? They're gone. The work's changing, alright — but it's changing in a way where I'm not sure the folks signing the checks even notice who's not at the table anymore. The ledger says growth. The street says something else."

The ledger lists 19 named workers at five West Oakland businesses this cycle, and every one of those five firms is up. Three are construction outfits. Coastline is the largest. Northgate Construction, run by managing partner Helena Voss-Adeyemi, is listed at 95 employees, 8.8 percent growth and $78.8 million in revenue, up $133,000. Mariner Construction is listed at 165 employees and 7.2 percent growth. Those three crews include plumbers Hazel Reed, Kenton Pearl, Maeve Pillai and Yadira Rao, electricians Elijah Brown and Jude Hess, laborer Elijah Roberts and carpenter apprentice Minh Raimon.

The other two firms are the reason the sites are busy. Oakmesh Systems, founded by Marcus Tan, is listed at 46 employees and $9.5 million in revenue. Its growth rate is 34.1 percent, more than three times Coastline's. Civis Systems, Elias Varek's urban-systems intelligence firm, is listed at 41 employees and $60.2 million in revenue. Its roster includes digital twin architects Sam Fazio and Catherine Santos and drone fleet coordinator Maurice Franklin.

The pay gap shows up in the ledger rows themselves. Coastline's average salary is listed at $85,000. Northgate's is $76,000. Oakmesh's is $135,000. Walker installs the plumbing, Northgate's electricians pull the wire, and the engineers who move in at the higher number are the ones bidding on the apartments nearby. Desmond Achebe, Coastline's managing partner, sees the problem from his own payroll.

"What keeps me up at night isn't the growth on the ledger — I can read a spreadsheet. It's the quiet stuff the numbers don't show," Achebe said. "I walk a different street home sometimes, just to see what's actually happening on the block, and I see the new folks settling in from Chinatown, the Stabilization Fund money finally moving. That's good. But I also see the workers — my plumbers, the electricians, the carpenters at the other shops — and I know a 9% growth number doesn't tell you whether a guy can afford to stay in the neighborhood he's helping rebuild. The contraction isn't loud. It's the guy who's been on the crew for twelve years quietly looking at rents across the bridge. That's what I'm watching. The growth is real, but if it's not growing for the people doing the work, it's just a taller building on the same shaky ground."

That is an unusual thing for an owner to say. In my experience, a managing partner with a strong quarter usually talks about backlog. Achebe talked about retention, and what he described isn't on any ledger yet. Losing a twelve-year journeyman doesn't change a revenue line. It changes who is still at the site gate at 6 a.m. a year from now.

Tan, whose company is the fastest-growing on the list, sounded like a man who knows people are watching his door.

"I can't speak for the whole neighborhood, but I can tell you what I see from my own door. Oakmesh is growing, and that growth means we hire more people, we pay more taxes, we take up more space. That's real," he said. "But the neighborhood isn't a ledger. It's the people who've been here through the ups and downs. What do we owe? We owe it to not forget that. We owe it to hire locally when we can, to be a good neighbor, to not let our success push anyone out. And what does the neighborhood owe us? I think it owes us a fair shake — the same chance to grow that it gives everyone else. It's a two-way street, and we're all still figuring out how to walk it together."

"When we can" is the phrase I would ask him to define. It is a promise with no number attached.

Not every block in the city is growing. The ledger's contraction list this cycle is concentrated in Ivy Hill. Ivy Hill Market shed three workers and is at negative 4.5 percent growth. Ivy Hill Cafe, Ivy Cleaners and Ivy Hill Family Dental each shed one. Down in the Baylight District, Marine Systems lost one worker. Nothing in the record ties those losses to West Oakland's gains. Still, it is the same city in the same spring, and the ledger shows it moving in two directions. The growth is in construction and civic tech. The losses are at a grocery, a cafe, a cleaner and a dentist, the businesses people use every day.

My mother's laundromat on 7th Street would have shown up on that second list, if it had lasted long enough to be counted.

That leaves the question Walker raised in the trailer. Coastline's 9.5 percent is a real number, and the revenue is too. But the board doesn't show what that growth is worth at the end of a plumber's shift, measured against the rent on the blocks he's plumbing, or who gets to count it as theirs.

By Trevor Shimizu | Bay Tribune Civic

# Coliseum Lost 799 Riders on a Weekend With No Event on Its Row

Jerald Jensen reads the Coliseum platform by what the trash cans hold. He is a janitor with AC Transit. "When the Coliseum empties out like that on a weekend, I see it in the trash cans — fewer cups, fewer wrappers, fewer folks lingering after the last train," he said. "The platform records don't catch the quiet. They don't catch the feeling that people aren't just missing a train, they're missing a reason to be there."

The ridership row agrees with him on the count. Coliseum logged 4,796 riders in cycle 114, down 799 from the 5,595 on the cycle 113 row. That was the largest drop of any station on the record this cycle.

**What the two rows say**

The cycle 113 row listed events in the Baylight District ×2 among its causes. The cycle 114 row lists no event and no construction. Its entries are mild weather, weekend, the ledger base, and crowded platforms. Crowded platforms is on every station row this cycle, so it does not set Coliseum apart. The weekend rule applies citywide, so it does not either. The station moved more than the weekend rule alone would explain, and the row gives no reason of its own.

Earlier rows show how much this station swings with its causes. Cycle 112 carried "events in East Oakland ×3" and logged 9,062 riders. Cycle 113 logged 5,595. Cycle 114 logged 4,796. The row cannot say whether the station is settling toward a baseline or sliding past one.

Coliseum's loss also cancelled the system's gains. Four stations rose: 19th St Oakland by 740, 12th St Oakland City Center by 670, Fruitvale by 510 and MacArthur by 156. Four fell: Coliseum by 799, Lake Merritt by 729, Rockridge by 219 and West Oakland by 10. Total ridership across the eight stations was 46,839, up 319 from 46,520. That is roughly what the gains left over after Coliseum's loss. The stations that rose each carry listed causes: First Friday, game day, events, a hub in design.

**The on-time column**

Coliseum's on-time performance was 81%, down one point. Every other station fell by seven to thirteen points: West Oakland by 13, Lake Merritt by 11, 19th St Oakland and Rockridge by 10, Fruitvale and MacArthur by 9, 12th St Oakland City Center by 7. The platform that lost the most riders held its schedule best. That fits a station with room on it. It does not explain why the room appeared.

Evan Lewis drives the line past the Coliseum for AC Transit and does not treat the pattern as routine. "On a weekend, yeah, the whole city thins out a bit — that's just how it goes. But when you pull up to that station and it's quieter than it ought to be, even for a Saturday, you start wondering."

He did not offer a cause. He offered a gap. "They don't tell you about the family that used to catch that train to a game and decided it wasn't worth the hassle this month," he said. "But nobody's asking the folks who aren't there anymore why they stopped. That's the part the numbers can't say."

The sports feed this cycle logged games in Indiana and Toronto. It also logged a sold-out NBA Cup opener at KONO, where the Oaks beat the Oklahoma City Thunder 101-95. The Coliseum row carries no game-day entry for any of them. The feed notes the rider lift is real only where a station row says game day, and this one does not.

**What the platform managers can and can't say**

Eleanor Rivera, a BART station manager in the Oakland Division, was asked about the drop. She described her own platform first. "At 7:40 on a weekend morning at 12th Street, the shift is quiet in a different way than a weekday. The crowds aren't there, so you hear the announcements bounce off the tile more." She declined to speak for Coliseum. "I wasn't there, and I don't have that platform's view."

She did offer a rule of thumb. "When a station drops more than the usual weekend pattern, it's rarely one thing. It's usually a rhythm stacking up — something at the station, something on the line, something in that neighborhood. But I'd be guessing if I told you where those riders went."

Her three layers are the places to look. The Packet has no entry for the station, none for the line, and none for the neighborhood. The three BART incidents on the record this cycle are a medical hold at Fruitvale, a signal failure in West Oakland and a stalled train in Rockridge, all from last week. None involves Coliseum. No chaos entry touched BART or AC Transit this cycle.

Elena Soria Dominguez leads planning for the Fruitvale Transit Hub Authority. Her description of Phase II applies in reverse here. "The documents show sightlines and path widths," she said. "They don't show the woman who sets up her cart at the panadería corner every morning." Fruitvale gained 510 riders with a hub in design and three events in the neighborhood. Coliseum has neither a hub project nor an event line. The row has nothing human attached to it except Jensen's trash cans and Lewis's empty stretch of platform.

**What stays open**

The record holds 14 transit workers on its roster and 8 citizens who have lived a transit line. None of them has been asked about Coliseum's cycle 114 numbers except through these four voices. Whether the 799 riders went to another station, another mode or nowhere at all is a question no row answers. The next Coliseum row will show whether the drop was a one-weekend gap or the start of a lower floor. If the next row also names no cause, Coliseum has a rider count and no reason, and that is a harder condition to maintain around than a broken signal.

# The Lives of C114
