# Faith and Quiet Work: The Week When Doors Opened

Manjit Singh stood in the Gurdwara Singh Sabha Fruitvale kitchen at dusk, a room that had been running the same langar service since 1982—the same year the congregation was founded. The space holds a steadiness that does not announce itself. Four hundred and fifty people come through the neighborhood from Fruitvale itself, and from the edges of Lake Merritt and the transit corridor. This cycle, the Gurdwara was not in acute crisis response the way the Bay Pure Land Buddhist Temple in Chinatown opened its doors with forty-one people seeking shelter this week, or the way Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship in Downtown moved into emergency mode in C107 with seventy-two. The Gurdwara's work had not changed shape. But the question that Bhai Manjit Singh did not need to articulate—how can faith-based organizations in Fruitvale work together to address the most pressing needs—was moving in the room anyway.

Across the city, eight named congregations and faith spaces had reported some form of crisis response or holy-day gathering in the past six cycles. The Cathedral of the Living Word, in Lake Merritt, had held 1,265 people for Epiphany this cycle and 1,077 the cycle before. St. Esperanza Parish in Fruitvale had marked the holy day with 374 attendees one cycle back. Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship downtown had brought 237 into the space for Epiphany this cycle alone. The scale of gathering and the scale of need were not the same thing, and the Gurdwara—with its 350 congregants, its langar kitchen, its neighborhood reach—had been absorbing both in the same steady breath.

Lucia Polito had founded the Postpartum Oakland Network out of Fruitvale. The Fruitvale Summer Speed Series was in its ninth cycle. The Fruitvale Transit Corridor Mutual Aid was live. These were not faith initiatives, but they lived in the same zip code, held the same people, answered to the same geography. The Gurdwara's langar kitchen did not compete with them; it was an older instrument, already in the room.

Eunice Marston, a faith leader in Downtown, had never held direct involvement with the Gurdwara. But she had seen the shape of what was happening. "I haven't had any direct involvement with the Gurdwara Singh Sabha Fruitvale," she said, "but as a faith leader in Downtown, I appreciate the various initiatives and organizations that bring people together and foster a sense of community."

The words were careful and true, and they did not resolve the question. What infrastructure—faith-based or secular, kitchen or fund, gathering or quiet standing—could hold Fruitvale's people steadily through both crisis and ordinary time? The Gurdwara had been asking that for forty-two cycles. No one had yet said whether it was asking alone.
