# Voices: What the A's Left Them

"The alarm didn't go off, and for a second I forgot and reached for it anyway."

That's Alicia Hernandez, a coffee roaster in Uptown, on her morning page. Lives carried her retirement this cycle. This is the rest of what she wrote: "Then I just lay there, listening to the house. It's strange, the quiet. Forty-some years of waking to the smell of beans before I even had my coffee, and now I wake to the smell of my own sleep."

She wrote that the same week the city is talking about the A's. Asked what the club's run has done for Uptown, she didn't pick up the talk. "To be honest, the A's haven't really affected my business or daily life that much. I mean, I'm happy when they're doing well, but I'm not really connected to the team. I've been too busy with my own transition into retirement to pay much attention."

Her page ends the morning on breakfast. "I made myself a proper breakfast this morning, eggs and the good bread, and I ate it slow. I think that's the change—I'm learning to be a person instead of a schedule."

---

"Hey Vin. Good to see you too. It's a nice day for a walk, even if the sky's got that gray Oakland look to it."

Pilar Ramirez is a paramedic who lives at Lake Merritt. She said it to her husband, Vincent, out walking the block. They had both seen the flyer for a workshop. Hers is the voice that goes on after the greeting: "It's easy to put up a sign, harder to actually change things. And Paul's got his A's opinions, sure. But you know how that goes—everyone's a manager until opening day comes."

Asked the A's question straight, she wouldn't make it bigger than it is. "To be honest, I'm not really sure how the community can benefit from the A's success. I mean, I love the team, but I've never really thought about it directly. I know my husband Vincent and I have argued about the A's and the Oaks, but that's more about our loyalty and curiosity than how it affects the community."

Of the workshops she said: "Seen too many of them come and go over the years. It's always the same folks at the podium and the same folks in the chairs, and nothing really moves."

The question she carries out of that conversation is her own: "Who's really gonna get out of these workshops? Will anything actually change this time?"

---

"I just know that when we had that parade with the kids on our block, it felt like everyone was happy and having fun, and that's what stuck with me."

Mariana Kapoor is a student in Chinatown. She wasn't following the season. "To be honest, I don't really know much about the A's success. I mean, I know they're the A's and they play baseball, but I haven't really been following how they're doing this season." She does know who's around. "Sometimes we see A's players around Chinatown, like Desmond Morton, he's a left fielder and my friend Eshe's mom loves watching him play."

Her night page goes somewhere the A's question didn't. "I keep thinking about how the grown-ups at the cultural center talk about things like rent and jobs, and I don't get all of it, but I feel it in my stomach. And then I think about that parade I made with the kids, and how for one second everyone was just laughing, and it felt like we were bigger than this block."

She ends the night on a question she carries: "Is something ending on this block, like my mom being tired or the A's leaving? How do I hold onto the good parts?"
