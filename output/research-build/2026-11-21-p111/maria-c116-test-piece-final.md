# Before the Shuttles

*Baylight District, by Maria Keen*

The quiet in Baylight at that hour has a sound. It's the waterfront promenade before anyone has asked anything of it. The engine's notes this cycle call the district's waterfront promenade peaceful, and I'd say that's fair. But a peaceful street and a full street aren't the same thing. Two people helped me see the difference.

## The woman who beats the shuttle

Liliana Salazar is an EV mechanic. She lives in Glenview, not Baylight, and she walks into the district in the morning on purpose, ahead of the shuttles that run along the waterfront.

"When I walk to the Baylight District in the morning, I see the shuttles rolling along the waterfront, and I time my walk to beat them," she told me. "People on the shuttle are probably staring at their screens or chatting with each other, but I'm outside, watching the city wake up."

I love that she says *probably*. She isn't judging the people on the shuttle. She just chose the sidewalk, and the sidewalk shows you things a window seat doesn't. "I see the empty storefronts, too," she said, "and it makes me wonder what's going on with the local businesses."

She isn't the only one who wonders. She's just one of the few who walks slowly enough to see it.

## The man who knows which windows

Ron Arenson doesn't have to wonder. He's a maintenance tech here, and the ledger lists his employer as Baylight Construction Authority. Maintenance people know a building the way night nurses know a ward. They notice when it changes before anyone writes it down.

This year he has watched three storefronts paper over their windows. He told me about each one.

"The first one was a small café; weeks before it closed, there were fewer customers, and the owners were rearranging the furniture, trying to draw in more people."

Anyone who has loved a small shop knows that move. When the customers thin out, you don't change the menu, you move the chairs.

"The second was a clothing store; before it closed, they had a big sale, but it didn't seem to bring in enough business."

"The third one was a new one for me; it was a tech store, and I saw them packing up equipment a few weeks before they boarded up the windows."

Then he said: "The neighborhood feels quieter with each one that closes."

He sees the packing and the rearranging, the weeks before the paper goes up. Nobody else hears the sale signs or the boxes. The rest of us only notice once the windows are covered.

## What Baylight is, on paper

Baylight is small. This cycle the district counts 65 students, 421 adults and 34 seniors. Compare that with Fruitvale, where I grew up, which has 426 students alone. Baylight added two adults since last cycle. It isn't draining and it isn't surging. It's holding at a size where three closed shops are very noticeable.

The money here is large but unevenly spread. My colleague Jordan Velez wrote last cycle that the engine has Ron drinking free cold brew in a company kitchen and feeling "completely disconnected from the neighborhood outside." Baylight has a lot of company kitchens. It also has a casino whose headcount has been shrinking, and Ron heard about those layoffs too. Marine Systems is the other firm in that weak column. It drew a notable visitor this cycle, according to the engine's notes, but I can't tell you what that visit means for the people who work there.

I can't tell you why the café, the clothing store or the tech shop closed either. Ron has watched them go but hasn't told me their reasons, and I won't guess for him.

## Who's holding the night

The district does have things meant to hold people up. The Oaks Community Night Fund, founded by Elias Varek, has existed since Cycle 99. The Oakland Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline has run here since Cycle 86. There's also an arts fund tied to the Keane gala. I couldn't confirm this cycle whether any of them reaches a storefront owner rearranging the furniture in March.

That's the question I'd put to all of them. I don't mean the big-picture one, about who the district is for. I mean the small one: who sees the café owner weeks before the paper goes up, and what can they do then?

Ron sees it. Liliana sees it from the sidewalk before the shuttle passes. I'd like to know if anyone with a budget sees it too.

I'll be back at that waterfront before the shuttles, and I'll count the papered windows again.
