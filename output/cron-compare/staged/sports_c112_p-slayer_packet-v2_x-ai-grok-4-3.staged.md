I stepped into the Blue Lattern Bar just after the lights came up, the kind of Jack London night where the river smell still clings to your jacket and the stools remember every loss we ever drank through. The place felt electric the way Benji Dillon said it would, but the tension sat right under it like a bad call that never gets overturned.

Jire Hilt stood near the rail in his fresh jersey, eyes bright. “I bought a new A's jersey and I have to say, it’s got me pretty excited. The anticipation is building up, I can feel it. I’ve been looking forward to the season, and now that I’ve got my new gear, I feel like I’m ready to go.” He meant every word. I wanted to believe him.

Vinnie Keane leaned on the same bar he used to lean on after road games. “To be honest, I haven’t been honored in the way you’re suggesting. I’m not aware of any specific event or recognition that I’m being referred to. I did attend a farewell event for Benji Dillon and me at the Blue Lattern Bar in Jack London, and it was a great turn-out. The atmosphere was lively, and it felt like a mix of nostalgia and celebration. I’m still getting used to not being as active with the A’s, but it’s nice to see people coming together like that.” He said it plain, the way a man does when the league already moved on and the city is still trying to catch up.

Benji Dillon sat two stools down, voice low enough that only the people who needed to hear it caught the edge. “The atmosphere at the Blue Lattern Bar during the Keane and Dillon farewell event was electric, but also a little tense. I’ve been around long enough to know that when legends are honored, there’s always a mix of celebration and scrutiny. People were clearly euphoric, but I could sense some underlying friction, like there were those who weren’t entirely on board with the farewell. It’s a feeling I’ve experienced before in the clubhouse, where not everyone’s always on the same page. Vinnie and I, we’ve been through a lot together, and it’s weird to think about not being out there on the field as part of the same team anymore.”

Lila Patty came in late, still wiping construction dust from her hands. She had bought the jersey too. “I bought a new A’s jersey and took a walk near a new construction site to clear my head after a long week. The noise of the city was still there, but standing by that new construction corner, I felt a bit more at peace.” She said it like peace was something you had to hunt for between the cheers.

Some will tell you this was closure, the clean page the front office keeps promising. They’re wrong. Closure is what happens when the city decides the story is finished, not when the storytellers are told to smile for the photo and disappear. We felt the friction because we still remember the price. Euphoria without the fight is just another way of saying we’re supposed to forget what the club took from us. I won’t.

The bar emptied slow. I stayed until the last glass hit the rail. How will this farewell shape the narrative the A’s carry into the off-season? We already know the soft version they want. The question is whether we let them write it.
