# Rockridge Keeps a Name Moving

The cafe chairs come off the sidewalk on Rockridge's main stretch, and the block gets quieter in the way a room does when the crowd has gone home but the lights are still on. Nobody here is holding a microphone. The sound is the hum of a refrigerator behind a shop window, a bike chain ticking as someone walks it past the bus shelter, and two people at the corner arguing softly about whether a show they both saw was worth the trip. Rockridge at this hour is not a stage. It is a place where a reputation gets carried home.

The name being carried this cycle is Tara Ellison's. The ledger lists her as a journalist in Media, based in Rockridge, and marks her fame as rising and surging, with her last appearance in the record logged this cycle. That is the whole of what the record gives on her, and it is enough to ask what a rising name does to a neighborhood that already knows the face.

Rockridge has a particular way of noticing. It does not chase. It watches who passes the bookstore window twice, who stops at the bulletin board, who is quoted by a neighbor in the line at the coffee counter. When a name rises, the change is quiet and uneven: a conversation that turns toward whether someone has seen the work instead of who the person is, a stranger who nods like they have met you. None of that is a measured trend. It is the texture of a block deciding whether a person belongs to it.

Across town, the same kind of motion runs the other direction. The ledger has Lena Cross and Rowan Pierce on the fading side, both attached to Piedmont Avenue, both logged as cooling. The where-are-they-now file is a strange genre. It is a list of people the city once could not stop talking about, now sitting in the quieter part of the memory. Piedmont Avenue is where that cooling is recorded. Rockridge is where the rising is recorded. Put the two streets side by side and the city's attention looks less like a spotlight and more like a tide, pulling some names up the beach while it leaves others on the sand.

The wider ledger holds a long list of culture figures rising this cycle, and none of them new to it. That is a bookkeeping fact, not a verdict on anyone's work. Still, it changes how the block reads its own names. Alma Vasquez, a sculptor logged in Laurel, has been rising in the record since she was last seen in an earlier cycle. Nina Reyes, an actress in Fruitvale, is surging. Theo Banks, a rapper based in Uptown, is surging too. Each of them works in a different room: a studio, a screen, a stage at night. What connects them on the ledger is only the arc. What connects them in the city is harder to see from a sidewalk.

Somewhere in this, there is a question that a rising name tends to raise without answering. When someone becomes the adult in the room, who was supposed to be standing there before them? The fading file suggests the room does not stay empty. It refills with whoever the city is looking at next, and the people who were there before get moved to the side, still in the building, still carrying their old work.

Rockridge does not decide any of this. The bulletin board keeps its own rhythm. The bookstore window keeps its display. The two people at the corner finish their argument and go their separate ways, one toward the BART station and one toward the hill, each carrying a different version of the same evening's talk.

The open question for this block is not whether Tara Ellison's work is being noticed. The ledger already says it is. The question is what kind of noticing it becomes when it arrives at a neighborhood that prefers to know its people by name, one conversation at a time, and whether a city that moves its attention this quickly leaves room for the ones it is moving away from.

# Eight Programs, One Church Name Since Cycle 78

The Foothill Baptist Tabernacle runs a food pantry and a seniors lunch program. Both have been on East Oakland's record since cycle 78. Eight programs are active in East Oakland this cycle. I can name six. Foothill's pantry is the mutual-aid one, and the lunch program is filed under health. That leaves the question I can't answer from here. How do these programs meet the money and the work that decide whether a family stays on this block? I'm still missing someone in line at the Foothill pantry, or a parent from the baseball league, saying what the program holds up in their own week and what it can't.
