# The Geometry of Third Base: Martin Richards in Fruitvale

### By Hal Richmond | Bay Tribune Senior Historian

The fog doesn’t clear off the Oakland estuary until well past nine in the morning, long after the diesel switchers have rattled the spur tracks along East 12th Street and the fruit vendors have rolled their steel grates up on International Boulevard. From the front window of the stucco bungalow near 34th Avenue, you can watch the tall container cranes at the Port of Oakland slowly emerge from the gray blanket like iron wading birds.

Martin Richards watches them with a ceramic mug of black coffee cupped between two hands that are insured for eight figures.

He is thirty years old. On the official transaction sheets filed with Major League Baseball, his employer is listed as the New York Mets, where his salary is recorded as $20,600,000 a year. In the Civis Systems property registries and county tax rolls, his net worth sits at $180,245,357. Yet when he steps out his front door into the morning air of District 3, there is no private security detail, no gated perimeter, and no glass-and-steel penthouse towering over the water. There is only the low redwood fence he mended himself two winters ago, a yellow AC Transit bus wheezing at the corner, and the familiar, heavy scent of carnitas and roasted chiles drifting up from Taquería El Andén down by the platform.

He shares household HH-0101-B032 with his wife, Sakura Foster. She is thirty-eight, an electrician carrying a union card with IBEW Local 595, whose ledger balance shows two hundred and twenty-three dollars in liquid savings and an annual take-home wage of $88,600. Every morning at five-fifteen, before the sun has touched the East Bay hills, Sakura kisses the crown of his head, pulls on steel-toed work boots, and catches the early coach down toward the estuary waterfront. All winter and throughout the spring of Y3, she has been pulling four-ought copper conduit through the damp sub-basements of the Baylight District until her shoulders ache.

At night, across a worn laminate kitchen table that predates his first major league at-bat, they eat tamales from Mercado Fruitvale and talk about the block. They do not talk about luxury boxes or chartered Gulfstreams. They argue, as Sakura did in Y3C2, over whether the municipal bonds and private investment pouring into the Baylight waterfront will ever actually pave the potholed asphalt of their alleyway, or why the Phase II construction on the Fruitvale Transit Hub seems perpetually stalled behind city planning memos.

"People think twenty million dollars changes the humidity in the room," Martin says. He speaks softly, in the measured cadence of an infielder who measures every movement in half-seconds. "It changes your tax bracket. It doesn't change the sound of the train on the crossing, and it doesn't make the coffee taste different."

***

The baseball world spent the better part of seven years struggling to find an adjective for Martin Richards.

When the Oakland Athletics drafted him out of Maryland in the first round of the 2028 draft and developed him, he was not the thunderous presence of Vinnie Keane, whose tape-measure home runs shook the Coliseum foundations and whose farewell tour in Y2 brought standing ovations in every ballpark on the Pacific rim. He was not Isley Kelley, gliding across the dirt at shortstop with that impossible, effortless grace that collected three MVP trophies and carved Kelley's name into inner-circle Cooperstown plaque bronze. Nor was he Mark Aitken, holding court at first base and serving as the civic conscience of the franchise, attending city council zoning hearings in Jack London and running youth leagues out of pocket.

Martin Richards was simply there. At third base. Every inning. Every cycle.

In 2041, playing on a pre-arbitration deal that paid him $750,000—a figure so absurdly depressed relative to his production that economists cited it as an anomaly—Richards posted a 6.8 WAR. He hit .302. He launched 27 home runs into the Oakland bleachers. He slugged .551. And most remarkably for a power-hitting corner infielder in an era of violent launch angles and soaring whiff counts, he struck out only 70 times in 463 at-bats—once every 6.6 trips to the plate. His fielding percentage was spotless. He did not commit a single throwing error across the entire campaign.

Yet when the sports pages argued about the future of the franchise, Richards remained almost invisible. In Y2C33, our own columnist P Slayer summarized the front office's dilemma in twelve brutal words: *"You can find a Martin Richards. You cannot find another Mark Aitken."*

The franchise believed it. With young phenom Ernesto Quintero tearing the stitches off the ball and demanding everyday at-bats, and with General Manager Mike Paulson facing the severe financial math of Danny Horn’s anchor extension, the arithmetic narrowed. Manager Deacon Seymour needed Quintero's bat in the everyday lineup. The corners were jammed. Aitken was under contract through 2043; Richards was in his walk year, asking for the market valuation that his glove and bat had earned ten times over.

The end came without ceremony. In Y2C49, at the trade deadline, Mike Paulson made the call. Martin Richards was dealt to the New York Mets for two prospects, sent across three time zones while the Oakland faithful were still buying playoff tickets.

In Y2C49, under the banner headline *Trade Deadline Earthquake*, this paper called it an ending Richards didn't get to write. P Slayer had already confessed to a journalistic debt long overdue in Y2C42, admitting that the paper had spent years documenting the pitching staff and the farewell tours while treating Richards like permanent furniture. We thought third base was self-cleaning. We forgot that someone had to stand there and take a hundred-and-ten-mile-an-hour line drive off the chest.

***

In New York, they gave him number 14, handed him a check that made him one of the wealthiest athletes in North America, and expected him to become someone else.

He didn't.

During the summer months, Richards lives in a quiet hotel suite near Flushing, taking ground balls under the flight path of LaGuardia Airport and doing precisely what he has done since he was twelve years old: staying down on the short hop, keeping his chin tucked, throwing strikes across the diamond to first base. But the moment the schedule grants an off-day or a series gap, he boards the flight west.

In Y3C4, when he landed back at Oakland International, the city was vibrating with the broadcast rhythm of UNDOCKED. While thousands tuned in to Bayline 6 to watch the pilots run the simulation boards, Martin sat on the couch in Fruitvale, eating leftover rice and checking the Undocked score sheets with the quiet, obsessive scrutiny of an old scout. "He checks those names the way guys in the dugout check out-of-town box scores," Sakura says with a wry grin. "He wants to see who held their ground and who slipped."

That quiet vigilance governs his life in the neighborhood. In Y3C6, walking past a produce market near the Fruitvale BART plaza, he stopped at a fruit stand where the owner had taped an old yellowed newspaper clipping to the glass register. It showed Richards as a rookie in green and gold, leaping high over a sliding runner. Richards stood there for three full minutes in his gray hoodie, staring at his own face, unable to recognize the kid in the photograph.

By Y3C7, as early-season rumors began trickling through the wire services, he found himself cornered at the corner bodega on International, patiently answering questions from an elderly clerk and three high school ballplayers about an analytics piece that had appeared in the Tribune. In Y3C8, a gaggle of kids in Little League jerseys spotted him near the plaza; he stayed for forty minutes, signing baseballs, posing for photos on cracked sidewalks, asking each boy about their footwork on the double play.

"People ask me why I didn't buy in Montclair or Rockridge," Richards remarked recently, leaning over the weathered side fence while a neighbor weeded a tomato bed. "Isley lives up in Rockridge. Mark bought his place down on the Jack London water. That fits them. That's who they are. But I built my life on these curbs. If I moved up into the hills, every time I looked out the window I'd just be looking down at where I actually belong."

In Y3C9, he stood at that same fence, comparing notes on property tax assessments with a retired warehouse worker whose family has occupied the adjacent lot since the container cranes were steam-powered. The housing market in Fruitvale is tightening; the proximity to the new transit lines has drawn speculators, and longtime tenants are feeling the squeeze. Richards listened with his head tilted, his expression grave. When an ambulance from Piedmont Avenue Medical Center wailed down Foothill, he waited until the siren died before speaking.

"The money doesn't make you bulletproof to what happens to your street," he told the neighbor. "It just means you can pay the bill when the water pipe breaks."

***

On a brisk Friday morning in Y3C10, Fruitvale was in full stride. On the community bulletin board outside the library, a yellow flyer for a seniors lunch program fluttered against the cork. Down near the corner taqueria, a white van from the Oakland Alternative Response Initiative sat curbside, its peer counselors resolving a heated sidewalk argument without a single police cruiser being summoned.

Martin Richards walked his usual loop. He waved to the counterman at the bakery, nodded to the crossing guard at the elementary school, and took a seat on a wooden bench near the plaza fountain.

Across the bay, the Mets were preparing for a weekend homestand at Citi Field. His equipment bag was already packed and waiting by the door at household HH-0101-B032: two raw-leather Rawlings gloves, three pairs of spikes, and the ash bats he rubs down with bone until the grain is hard as marble.

Someone asked him if he resented Mike Paulson, or if he held a grudge against Deacon Seymour for letting Ernesto Quintero take over the hot corner in Oakland.

Martin smiled—a small, rare crease at the corner of his eyes.

"Paulson did what a general manager does," he said, watching a flock of pigeons scatter as the morning train pulled into Fruitvale station. "He looked at a spreadsheet and saw that you could get eighty percent of my production for ten percent of the cost. You can't get mad at a calculator. But baseball isn't played inside a computer. It's played on dirt. And when a bad hop comes off the lip of the grass in the ninth inning, the ball doesn't know what you're being paid. It just wants to know if you're willing to take it in the teeth."

He stood up, adjusted the brim of an uncreased Oakland ballcap, and began the five-block walk back to the stucco house where Sakura would be coming home from the sub-basements. The fog was finally lifting over the cranes. The infield was clear.
