# Eastlake Led the City in Incidents This Cycle. Its Row Still Reads "Steady."

Eastlake is a neighborhood where, on a Winter weekday in Cycle 113, a person waiting on a curb for a patrol car was waiting longer than the stopwatch figure suggests. The Crime_Metrics row lists the average response at 9.9 minutes. Nobody on a curb experiences an average. They experience the gap between the call and the car, and then the paperwork that follows.

Eastlake logged 8 incidents this cycle, the most of any neighborhood on the table. That is 4 more than Cycle 112, which means the count doubled. Citywide, the table holds 130 incidents across 22 neighborhoods. West Oakland, Grand Lake, Laurel and Chinatown each logged 7. Glenview and Brooklyn logged 4 apiece, and Jack London logged 3.

This reporter read the Eastlake row in Crime_Metrics directly. It carries a property index of 37, a violent index of 23, a response average of 9.9 minutes and a clearance rate of 22 percent. Two fields stand out.

The first is the Trend column, which reads "steady." The second is the Hotspot column, which is blank. A neighborhood that went from 4 incidents to 8 in one cycle is not steady by the count. By the sheet's own movement summary, only Adams Point and Piedmont Ave are listed as rising, and Eastlake is not among them. This is a classification gap, and it may be a quirk of how the sheet scores movement, since the trend field is not a plain restatement of the incident count. Still, the effect is that the most incident-heavy neighborhood in the city carries no flag on the sheet that would tell a reader to look at it. That is analysis, not a finding of fault. The records searched do not say how the trend field is calculated.

I searched the paper's archive for prior Eastlake coverage on police response and clearance. It returned earlier editions and desk files, but nothing that explains the Eastlake figures this cycle. Nothing in the archive reports a department explanation for the jump.

## What the custody trail shows

The Care_Justice_Census rows trace what happened after the incidents. Eastlake shows 8 charges, which led to 2 arrests, and 3 people in custody at the close of the cycle. All 3 are listed as other residents, with none tracked individually.

The three numbers do not form a straight line. Eight charges produced two arrests, yet three people were in custody. The census does not explain the difference. A holdover from an earlier cycle could account for it, or a transfer, or a different way of counting. The reader is left with a pipeline whose stages do not add up, and no record to say why.

Eastlake is not alone in this pattern. West Oakland shows 7 charges, 2 arrests and 4 in custody. Grand Lake, KONO and Laurel each show 7 charges, 2 arrests and 3 in custody. Citywide, the census counts 29 arrests this cycle, 56 people in custody at the close and 30 who left.

## The slowest wait is elsewhere, and the clearance rate is the same

Temescal has the slowest response in the city, at 10.8 minutes. It logged 6 incidents, up 1, and its clearance rate is also 22 percent, the same as Eastlake's. So Eastlake is not the slowest neighborhood, and its clearance is not uniquely low on the table. What sets it apart is the volume. More incidents are landing on a response that takes 9.9 minutes, and only 22 percent clear.

## Who answers

The roster behind those numbers has 39 public-safety staff. At the top is Police Chief Rafael Montez, a 27-year department veteran according to the Civic_Office_Ledger. Under him are Deputy Chief of Operations Kimberly Cho and Deputy Chief of Community Engagement Leonard Briggs. None of the three has a statement on Eastlake in the records available for this piece, so nothing here is attributed to them.

The practical questions are narrow. A resident who calls from an Eastlake block this cycle should know that the sheet's average is 9.9 minutes and that the neighborhood's incident count doubled. What the sheet does not say is whether that is a spike or a new baseline. It does not say whether the 22 percent clearance rate reflects a backlog or a normal rhythm, or why three people sit in custody when two were arrested.

If Eastlake is the most incident-heavy neighborhood in the city and clearance is 22 percent, what does the department tell the people who live there? And what changes before the next cycle's table is printed?
