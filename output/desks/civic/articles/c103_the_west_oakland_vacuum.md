# The West Oakland Vacuum: When City Promises Stall, Shadow Leverage Moves In
By Farrah Del Rio | Bay Tribune Civic

The green-and-white flyers taped near the Allen Temple bus stop have been peeling at the corners for three cycles now. They advertise the Oakland Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline—a flagship initiative from Mayor Avery Santana’s office promising to funnel the city’s youth into union trades. 

But out of 86 applicants citywide, only 31 have been placed. In West Oakland, the numbers are effectively zero. The designated program space—an old warehouse on 12th Street—sits empty. The only vehicles arriving at odd hours are unmarked contractor vans. Pigeons roost in the rafters. The phone numbers on the flyers just ring out.

Gregory Mims leans against the chain-link fence on International Boulevard, watching a kid toss rocks at a dented stop sign. Mims is a Brooklyn transplant who has become a quiet fixture outside Crisis Coffee. He knows exactly what the empty warehouse represents. 

"Honest take? That warehouse been empty since before my daughter was born," Mims said. "City puts up them flyers every couple years, same faded ink, same promises. Elena used to say it's like planting seeds in concrete - might sprout for a minute, but the roots ain't got nowhere to go."

Mims scuffs his shoe against the sidewalk. "Thing is, folks round here ain't stupid. We see the A's getting new stadiums while our kids get pamphlets. You want apprenticeships? Start with paying the electric bill in that warehouse so it don't look like a set from some zombie movie."

The Mayor’s office has promised a review of the "bottlenecks." But in West Oakland—the epicenter of the city's decay metrics, where the retail vitality score has plunged to 5.5—the failure of a city initiative isn't just an administrative error. It creates a vacuum.

Elias Varek, the founder of Civis Systems and owner of the incoming Oakland Oaks NBA franchise, is building his empire on the edge of this same neighborhood. He sees the stalled real estate differently than the Mayor does. He sees who fills the vacuum. 

"Gregory’s not stupid. He sees the same thing I do—those warehouses are full of kids who showed up hungry, ready to work, and got handed a broom or a pallet jack and a dead-end," Varek said. "That’s the kind of gap that… attracts attention. His kind of attention."

Varek leaned forward, tapping his kneecap. The tension between his vision of structured systems and Mims's grasp of neighborhood leverage is palpable.

"I don’t think he’s the one who stalled the pipeline—city contracting did that all on its own—but he’s damn sure circling," Varek said. "You don’t leave that kind of vacuum in a neighborhood like this. Either you fill it with something real, or someone else fills it with whatever turns a profit."

Varek’s assessment captures the reality of the block. Mims isn’t the cause of the city’s failure; he is the consequence of it. He is a man who watches the street, understands the gaps, and knows that leverage is built precisely where official funding dries up.

The Mayor's office promises an audit of the pipeline by winter, but the math points hard toward the 12th Street warehouse changing hands—and influence shifting on the block—before the city ever opens its doors.
