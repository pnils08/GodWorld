## Civic Desk — Cycle 108

The dining hall at St. Esperanza Parish was packed past its usual capacity Tuesday afternoon, folding tables shoulder to shoulder for the seniors lunch program's milestone, while out back somebody was still working a hand truck of boxes through the loading door. New faces. Boxes unpacked. A full room. That's the version of Fruitvale the record wants you to see this week.

Rick Walker isn't buying it. He teaches at a K-8 a few blocks off International, ran a kitchen line before that, and has lived in Fruitvale long enough to clock the gap between activity and momentum. "I've seen some new faces around the neighborhood, which is great, but at the same time, I feel like there's a lack of energy, you know?" he told me on the sidewalk outside, his breath fogging in the marine chill. "It's like the community is just going through the motions. I've been living in Fruitvale for a while now, and I've noticed that there aren't as many initiatives or programs to bring people together or improve the area. It's a shame, because I think we have a lot of potential here."

Here's where it gets ugly. The numbers say two things at once, and nobody's picked a side. Fruitvale's sentiment reading sits at 0.44 — second-best of twenty-two neighborhoods, well above the citywide median of 0.33. On that leaderboard, Fruitvale looks like it's winning. But the same record logs the neighborhood in decay — sentiment falling, retail vitality falling, housing pressure climbing a full point to 1.5, ranked fifteenth of twenty-two against a citywide median of zero. Zero. Most of this city isn't carrying housing pressure at all right now. Fruitvale is.

So which is it — a neighborhood near the top of the pack, or one sliding backward with rising housing pressure and no name on the fix? I went looking for the initiative that's supposed to own this. The program, the fund, the office with Fruitvale stamped on a solution. There isn't one. Not on the roster. Not in the file.

Carmen Solis has organized on this block long enough to tell a photo op from a plan. "I've noticed that there are new faces on the block, and that's a good thing, but at the same time, I feel like we're not really doing much to address the issues that have been plaguing our neighborhood," she said. "As someone who cares about this community, it's frustrating to see things not moving forward."

And it's not just Fruitvale talking about Fruitvale. Jessie Hess, who pulls espresso downtown and has watched this city long enough to know a pattern when she sees one, put it plain: "I've seen how some neighborhoods in Oakland, like Fruitvale, seem to be struggling. As someone who's lived in this city for a while, it's disheartening to see areas that need help not getting the attention they deserve. It feels like there's a lack of effort to really address the issues and make a positive change."

A full dining hall and a hand truck of boxes aren't a housing plan. They're not a retail strategy either. The map says decay. The program roster doesn't answer it. So somebody downtown — whoever's supposed to be watching Fruitvale's numbers — explain how a neighborhood can rank near the top of this city's sentiment sheet and still get logged as coming apart, with nobody's name attached to the fix.
