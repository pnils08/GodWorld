THE CYCLE PULSE 89 — SUPPLEMENTAL — CULTURE & COMMUNITY_Benji Dillon
The Rhythm and the Tide
By Maria Keen | Bay Tribune

OAKLAND — On Tuesday mornings, before night games, Benji Dillon drives south.

Not to the stadium. Not to the training facility. To the Aquarium of the Bay at Pier 39 in San Francisco, where his son Rick Jr. presses his face against the glass and watches the leopard sharks circle in the shallow tank. Dillon stands behind him, coffee in hand, watching the same thing he’s watched since he was a boy in Santa Cruz — the way water moves, the way life adapts to it, the way rhythm governs everything that survives.

“He’s obsessed with the rays,” Dillon told me, smiling in that quiet way of his. “I was the same age when I got obsessed with the ocean. My dad used to take me to the tide pools before school. I’d watch the anemones close and open. I thought they were breathing.”

His father, Rick Dillon Sr., was a professional surfer. He taught his son that you can’t outmuscle a wave — you can only find its rhythm. He died in a shark accident during Benji’s junior year at UC San Diego, and the ocean has meant something different ever since.

“It still gives,” Dillon said. “It still takes. My job isn’t to control it. It’s to keep riding.”

-----

THE GOLDEN ARM

They call him The Golden Arm, but Benji Dillon doesn’t believe in magic.

“A golden arm is just a normal arm that didn’t quit,” he told P Slayer in an interview earlier this year. “Everyone likes to assign magic to a thing that took me twenty years to build. I’m not a sorcerer. I wake up, I do the same stretch, the same warm-up, the same throw progression.”

Five Cy Young Awards. Two hundred wins. More than three thousand strikeouts. Six championship rings. Two Science Monthly covers. And now, at thirty-eight years old, his final season in green and gold.

The numbers tell one story. The routine tells another.

“You put the burden on the routine so the game feels like sunlight,” Dillon said. “If you feel the mechanics during the game, that means the game is louder than you are. And I never let that happen.”

-----

THE SCIENTIST

Dillon majored in marine biology at UC San Diego. His professors praised his discipline. His teammates called him “Zen Ben.” He met Maya Torres — an artist and conservationist with a wild heart and patient eyes — during his sophomore year. She matched his calm with color.

When his father died, it was Maya who pulled him back from the undertow. They married two years later, barefoot on the same beach where Rick Sr. had caught his final wave. Their son is named for him.

The marine biology never left. Dillon still reads papers on ocean acidification and kelp forest restoration. He still tracks the health of the Monterey Bay sanctuary. And when Science Monthly put him on the cover — twice — for his data-driven approach to pitching and his work on water filtration technology, he treated it the same way he treats everything: as a system to understand, not a trophy to display.

“Does science make you better,” P Slayer once asked him, “or just more aware of how human you are?”

Dillon thought for a long time before answering. The silence made the piece.

-----

THE CONSERVATIONIST

Maya Torres-Dillon doesn’t give many interviews. She prefers the work to the spotlight — the same quality that drew her to her husband twenty years ago.

But her name appears in places that matter. She sits on the advisory board for the Bay Conservation Alliance, the nonprofit that monitors shoreline health from Richmond to Alameda. She’s consulted on the Baylight District’s environmental review, specifically the remediation protocols for the Jack London waterfront where the new stadium will rise.

“The Bay is a system,” she told a conservation journal last year. “You can’t build on it without understanding what you’re building into. The tides don’t care about your timeline.”

It’s a philosophy her husband shares. When I asked Dillon about the Baylight project — the stadium that will replace the Coliseum, the development that will reshape Jack London Square — he didn’t talk about the seats or the sightlines.

“I care about the water,” he said. “The shoreline remediation matters more than the scoreboard. If we get that wrong, everything else is just decoration.”

-----

THE LEASE

Dillon talks about pitching the way other people talk about mortality — with respect, not fear.

“Pitching isn’t a promise,” he said. “It’s a lease. You can’t own this forever. So you take care of the bolts. You rotate the tires. You don’t lie to yourself.”

He’s not pretending the cliff doesn’t exist. He’s learning its shape early.

“You’ve got two options in this league,” he said. “You can pretend the cliff doesn’t exist, or you can learn its shape early. I choose the second.”

His rough start against Cleveland this season — four innings, nine hits, seven earned runs — reminded everyone that even golden arms rust. But the routine doesn’t change. The Tuesday mornings at the aquarium don’t change. The rhythm continues.

“Arms are like bridges,” he said. “You don’t pray they hold. You inspect the bolts.”

-----

THE CALM AND THE STORM

In spring training 2025, a lanky kid from Santa Cruz with sea salt in his hair crossed paths with an undrafted spark plug from Massachusetts named Vinnie Keane. Fire met water. Chaos met calm. They’ve been inseparable ever since.

“He pitches like he’s meditating,” Keane said once. “And I hit like I’m waking the world up.”

They won six championships together. They built a dynasty that redefined what Oakland could be. And now, in what might be their final season together, they’re still the Calm and the Storm — still balancing each other, still pushing each other, still proving that the best partnerships are the ones where opposites don’t cancel out but amplify.

“Vinnie taught me that fire has its own rhythm,” Dillon said. “Different from water, but rhythm all the same. You can’t survive this game without both.”

-----

THE MOUND

I asked Dillon what goes through his head when the mound is quiet — dead quiet — and the count is full and the season is on the line.

“Math first,” he said. “Then sunlight. Then nothing.”

He paused.

“If I reach nothing, the hitter is already behind.”

That’s the mind on the mound. That’s the quiet revolution P Slayer wrote about — the way intellect and instinct coexist in modern baseball, the way a marine biologist from Santa Cruz turned pitching into philosophy.

Hal Richmond once wrote that Dillon “didn’t arrive with partners. He didn’t share the load. He didn’t fit into an era. He defined one.”

On Tuesday mornings, before night games, Benji Dillon watches his son watch the sharks. The rhythm of the water. The rhythm of the game. The rhythm of a life spent learning that you can’t outmuscle a wave — you can only find its shape and ride.

This is his final season. Or maybe it isn’t. The lease runs out when it runs out.

But the tide keeps coming in. And Benji Dillon keeps riding.
