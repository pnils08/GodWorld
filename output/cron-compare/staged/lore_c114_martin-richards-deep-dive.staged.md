# The Twenty-Million-Dollar Commute: Martin Richards at 35th and International

### By Hal Richmond | Bay Tribune In-Depth

The fog off the San Francisco Bay rarely climbs past the third floor of the apartment buildings on International Boulevard, but at six in the morning in Fruitvale, you can watch it curl along the silver bellies of the shipping cranes down at the Port of Oakland. 

Martin Richards stands at the front window of apartment 3B with a chipped ceramic mug in his hand. He is thirty years old, mid-career, with the heavy, squared-off shoulders of a man who has played ten and a half seasons of professional third base. On the ledger of the New York Mets, he is an enterprise asset: twenty million six hundred thousand dollars in annual salary, backed by an accumulated net worth of one hundred eighty million two hundred forty-five thousand three hundred fifty-seven dollars. On the rental registry for household HH-0101-B032, he is a tenant paying two thousand four hundred and fifty dollars a month on a walk-up lease, sharing four rooms with an electrician named Sakura Foster.

Behind him in the narrow kitchen, the kettle begins its steady iron hum. Foster, thirty-eight, sits on a stool still lacing up her steel-toed work boots. Her hands are rough from pulling copper wire through the freezing sub-basements of the rising Baylight District—work that earns her eighty-eight thousand six hundred dollars a year against two hundred and twenty-three dollars in liquid savings. She does not look at the Mets envelope resting on the counter. She looks at the steam.

"Anthropic shuttle blocked the transit lane again at twenty-ninth," Foster says, not looking up. "The city bus had to wait through three cycles of the light."

"Saw that," Richards says softly. 

They have lived inside this tension since winter. In Y3C2, over a dinner of carnitas and white beans, they had argued until the kitchen grew cold about whether the torrent of Baylight capital would ever cross the railway line and touch Fruitvale, or whether it would simply price out the mechanics at the corner. Richards had listening ears and twenty million dollars in an escrow account, but in this room, he was an Oakland resident watching the split sharpen across the asphalt outside his window.

He is a Striver by constitution—driven, steady, and uncomfortably principled. And despite the Mets contract that took him east, his fandom dial remains locked on green and gold.

### The Roster Math of Y2C49

To understand why a twenty-million-dollar third baseman still rents in Fruitvale, you have to go back to the trade deadline in Y2C49. 

General Manager Mike Paulson, operating out of West Oakland, had reached the apex of his dynasty construction. The Athletics were marching toward their seventh championship, but the payroll ceiling had hardened. Richards, who had posted a staggering 6.8 WAR while playing on an archaic seven-hundred-and-fifty-thousand-dollar contract, was due an extension that the Oakland front office refused to write. Columnist P Slayer had framed the choice in stark, painful prose back in Y2C33, when both Aitken and Richards were in contract years: *"You can find a Martin Richards. You cannot find another Mark Aitken."*

Aitken, the thirty-three-year-old first baseman and civic liaison who recently purchased his home in Jack London, had a legacy that seemed woven into the concrete of the city. Richards was simply elite. He was quiet, metronomic, and did not demand a media entourage. In the clubhouse, he took his ground balls, went two-for-three in spring openers, and walked home to Fruitvale.

Behind him, Ernesto Quintero was pounding on the door. Quintero, twenty-four, had emerged from the minor-league pipeline as a physical hurricane, smashing thirty-seven home runs by the deadline of the title year, on his way to forty-six, and demanding everyday plate appearances. Paulson made the calculation that every general manager makes when sentiment runs into arithmetic: he cleared third base. In Y2C49, Richards was traded to Queens for two top-ten prospects.

"Honestly? It's a gut punch," Richards told the Tribune that evening, his apartment half-filled with packing cardboard. "I've spent my entire career here—built a life in Fruitvale, know the streets, the people, the rhythm of this place. One day you're grabbing coffee at the corner market, nodding at Lucia on her way to the wellness center, joking with Allen about his last start, and the next? You're packing boxes for Queens. That stuff doesn't just transfer with a contract."

He flew east, signed the contract that made him generational wealth, and then did something the sports desks in Manhattan could not fathom: he kept the apartment keys in his pocket.

### The Science of Contact

The numbers that followed him across the country were not the erratic flashes of a streaky slugger; they were the results of a ten-year refinement.

In Y3C7, Data Desk analyst Elliot Marbury published a multi-year dossier tracing Richards' strikeout evolution. From five seasons spanning the middle decade through three seasons back, Richards struck out once every 5.1 to 5.6 at-bats, hitting as many as thirty home runs but paying for it with eighty-nine to one hundred thirteen punchouts a year. 

Then came the hinge. In the three campaigns culminating in the championship run, his strikeout rate separated from his historical band entirely. He struck out once every 7.9 at-bats three seasons ago; once every 6.7 at-bats two seasons ago; and during his final Oakland campaign, once every 6.6 at-bats, over four hundred sixty-three of them. He batted .302 with twenty-seven home runs and a .551 slugging average while striking out just seventy times all year. 

He didn't surrender power to purchase contact. He refined his plate coverage until pitchers had nowhere to hide.

The Athletics attempted to fill the gap with twenty-one-year-old rookie Kevin Clark, who batted .297 in an eighteen-game trial in Jack London, but eighteen games is not four hundred plate appearances. And while Quintero slugged forty-six homers in Baylight District's shadow to justify Paulson's gamble, Quintero did it while drawing only twenty-seven walks and striking out once every 6.2 at-bats. Richards was the anchor that never wobbled.

When Marbury's analysis hit the paper in Y3C7, Richards was standing by the cold-drink cooler at the corner market on 34th Avenue. The clerk slid the sports page across the counter.

"Hey, Martin," the clerk asked, pointing at the printed table. "This says you didn't swing at anything outside the zone since the fall before last. That true?"

Richards had laughed, tucked the milk carton under his arm, and answered: "I swung at plenty. Just made sure my hands were quick enough to hit 'em."

### Sidewalks and Red Tape

In Fruitvale, nobody cares about your slugging percentage when the rent check is due on the first.

During Y3C9, Richards was out by the wooden back fence behind his building, taking down winter ivy, when his neighbor called over. They stood in the cool morning air, watching the dust kick up from the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II construction site down by the BART station. The neighbor was holding a notice about rising property assessments; housing pressure in District 3 had ticked upward to 4.5, far above the municipal median. 

Richards, who earns more in a four-game series than the building pays in annual property taxes, leaned over the fence and listened to the math. He did not talk about his signing bonus. He talked about lease clauses, about how the commercial storefronts along International were changing hands, and about whether the transit hub's community benefits agreement would actually keep local trades on the payroll.

Elena Soria Dominguez, the forty-three-year-old planning lead for the transit hub, has spent cycles defending the project’s local-hire quotas in public hearings. But on the pavement, the skepticism is stubborn. In Y3C9, Foster had watched apprentice electricians get screened out while general contractors brought in outside crews.

A block away, Lucia Polito, fifty-five, was unlocking the front door of her aura wellness practice. A pillar of neighborhood stability, Polito nodded as Richards passed on his morning jog toward the Lake Merritt pergola. Across the street, her husband Miguel Allen, sixty, was loading heavy pipe wrenches into his service van, having just cleared a stubborn root obstruction down at the rec center without a word of complaint.

"Morning, Martin!" Allen shouted over the rattle of iron pipe. "How's the turf in New York?"

"Colder than here, Miguel!" Richards shouted back, waving without breaking stride.

In Y3C8, a cycle after Marbury's strikeout-rate memo ran in the Tribune, his phone lit up with texts from high school coaches and former minor-league roommates he hadn't heard from in seven years. He didn't answer them with banquets or promises. He walked down to the Fruitvale station plaza, bought two bags of oranges from a street vendor, and stood for twenty minutes posing for camera-phone pictures with kids wearing green-and-gold caps.

***

### The Dynasty’s Ghost

The men Richards shared a clubhouse with for a decade are scattering across Oakland, each negotiating their own settlement with the passage of time.

Vinnie Keane, thirty-nine, has stepped away into legend, opening Keane Mercantile in Grand Lake and settling into the role of elder statesman. Isley Kelley, thirty-three, plays out his senior campaign in Rockridge on a thirty-three-million-dollar deal, fielding inquiries with the serene composure of a three-time MVP who knows his numbers will outlive him. Mark Aitken works civic hearings and youth baseball leagues in Jack London, his legacy secured by deed and title.

Richards remains the outlier: the one who was forced out while still at the absolute peak of his athletic powers, yet refused to sever the lifeline to the neighborhood he had made his own. 

He still checks the UNDOCKED broadcast board on Bayline 6 every cycle, tracking the pilot standings the way older fans track box scores. In Y3C4, he watched the flight results near the transit center, noting how an electrician or a janitor could navigate the void for forty minutes and come back down with a clean sheet. He understood that instinct—the desire to fly into a different atmosphere, execute your shift with clinical precision, and return to solid ground.

***

### The Weight of Staying

At seven in the morning in Y3C10, the Fruitvale corridor is fully awake.

A paper flyer for a senior lunch program flutters on the wooden utility pole near the taqueria patio. An Oakland Alternative Response Initiative van cruises slowly down 35th Avenue, its team waving to a shopkeeper who is rolling up the corrugated steel shutters on a bakery.

Sakura Foster picks up her lunch pail and kisses Richards on the cheek. 

"What's on the day?" she asks.

"Nothing with a clock on it," Richards replies. "Off-season. A latch for the back fence and a loaf from the market."

"Don't let anybody talk you into more than you came for," she says. It is the same advice she gives him every time he leaves this apartment, the one with the radiator that clanks all night.

"I won't," he says.

She heads down the wooden steps, her heavy boots striking the concrete with a rhythmic, unhurried cadence, joining the stream of workers heading toward the Fruitvale BART platform.

Martin Richards watches her turn the corner from the window. No black town car will pull up to the curb today and no runway is waiting; by noon he will be standing in a hardware aisle with a latch in his hand, a man with twenty-million-dollar expectations and a very small errand.

He will take the day with the same ruthless, strikeout-resistant composure that Elliot Marbury charted on a spreadsheet in Y3C7. He will pick the right latch without fuss, wave at the regulars on the usual route, and let the checks drawn on an empire's bank account take care of themselves.

And by evening, with the day's errands done, he will unlock the door of apartment 3B, set the paper bag in the hallway closet, and look out the kitchen window at the Port cranes cutting through the night. 

He didn't get to choose how his Oakland Athletics career ended. Mike Paulson and Ernesto Quintero took care of that with a spreadsheet and forty-six home runs. But Martin Richards chose where he lives, and in Fruitvale, that is the only kind of contract that actually counts.
