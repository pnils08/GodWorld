# The Port Got Louder. Down the Block, the Ledger Barely Moved.

*By Jordan Velez, Business Reporter*

Elle Lilo still spends her shifts doing what she has always done at the Port of Oakland: bringing ships in. She is a harbor tugboat captain, one of 1,501 people on the Port's payroll this cycle. The Port's books grew by $765,000 since last cycle, to $425.1 million. On the water, Lilo says, the difference is mostly the volume of the noise.

"The Port's growth? It's been steady, but honestly, my day-to-day hasn't changed much," Lilo said. "I'm still out there guiding ships, same as always. The bustle's a bit louder, sure, but it's the kind of noise you get used to. What keeps me up at night is making sure we keep that balance—growth without losing the soul of this place."

That balance is the real Jack London story this cycle. It does not show up in the Port's top line. It shows up in how unevenly the money lands once it leaves the water.

Start at the repair end. Blair Patel, a ship repair foreman at the Port, describes the same growth as a workload that keeps climbing.

"The Port's expansion? Yeah, I've been watching it closely," Patel said. "More ships coming in means more work for us, but it's not all smooth sailing. The docks are busier than ever, and the pressure's on to keep up with repairs and maintenance. It's a double-edged sword—good for jobs, but the pace is relentless. And honestly, I'm not sure the infrastructure can handle it long-term without some serious upgrades."

Dmitri Soto, a container yard supervisor, describes it from the stacks. His account sounds less like a complaint than a description of a job getting bigger.

"The role's gotten busier, no doubt," Soto said. "More ships coming in means more containers to manage, more coordination with the teams on the ground. It's not just about stacking boxes anymore—it's about keeping the flow smooth, making sure everyone's on the same page. Feels like we're the backbone of the whole operation."

Busier yards, busier repair crews. My father worked the docks for thirty years, and I know what that sentence usually means. It means a heavier shift first. Whether a fatter paycheck follows is a separate question, and it doesn't always get answered.

Now walk inland from the water to the neighborhood's dining rooms and bar rails. That is where a port's growth is supposed to spill over: the crew grabbing a plate after a double, the warehouse worker stopping for a drink.

Green & Gold Tavern carries 30 workers, among them dishwasher Hector Leech, servers Trenton Nawan and Valentina Campbell, and bartender Guadalupe Lupe. The tavern's revenue rose $6,000 since last cycle, to $3.7 million. Blue Lantern Bar, where Yuki Ji and Guadalupe Lee pour, also employs 30 and added $7,000, to $3.9 million. Harborline Grill is a seven-person kitchen whose line includes Tomas Renteria, Quynh Le, Jalen Hill and Bruce Wright. It brought in $1,000 more than last cycle, for $860,000 total.

Put those figures next to the Port's and the shape is plain. The Port added three-quarters of a million dollars. The kitchen down the block added roughly what one line cook might take home in a couple of weeks.

Anchor Build sits between those extremes. The construction firm is led by managing partner Marguerite Salcedo and has 181 people on staff, including laborer Travis Golin and electrician Briar Soto. It added $241,000, to $146.6 million. Elena Leon, a longshoreman at the Port, has noticed what is going up around the docks.

"Elle? She's got that quiet strength about her—the kind that keeps the port running smooth even when the tides turn," Leon said. "I've seen her maneuver those tugs like they're extensions of her own body. As for Jack London... well, the money's moving differently these days. More glass towers going up where the old warehouses stood, but the docks? We're holding steady. You can feel the shift though—like the whole neighborhood's holding its breath waiting to see who benefits and who gets left behind."

None of these businesses lost ground this cycle. Every ledger in the neighborhood ticked upward. But a neighborhood where everyone is up can describe a good economy or a lopsided one, and the size of each increase decides which.

When a port grows by hundreds of thousands and the grill feeding its crews grows by a thousand, someone should explain where the difference goes. It could be payroll for Patel's repair crews. It could be the upgrades he says the docks will need. It could be the towers Leon is watching rise. Or it could end up somewhere the dishwasher at Green & Gold never sees.

The record doesn't say yet. That leaves the question Lilo raised from the wheelhouse. What does the Port's growth mean for the everyday worker in Jack London?
