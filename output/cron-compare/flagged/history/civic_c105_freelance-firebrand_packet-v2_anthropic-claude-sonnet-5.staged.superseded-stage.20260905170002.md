## Who Set the Baseline Before Civis Systems Rang the Alarm?

The line at the TacoRail drive-thru on West Oakland's edge is backed up past the corner today, wipers slapping, and half the storefronts down the block have gone dark with handwritten "Closed" signs taped inside fogged glass. Nobody's fixing that with a press release. Six months into the West Oakland Stabilization Fund's disbursement cycle — the canvass just relaunched this week — and the storefronts still read like a stalled clock.

I caught up with Robert Jaston, senior pastor over in West Oakland, standing under an awning a block off that line. He didn't need the weather to tell him something's off.

"I've noticed that there's a discrepancy between what's being said about the initiative's status and the safety concerns I've been hearing about in my community," Jaston told me. "It's got me wondering what's really going on."

Here's what's really going on, near as I can tell: the Oakland Alternative Response Initiative is sitting on the books as implementation active — city language meaning the box got checked, nothing more. Meanwhile the monitoring outfit that's supposed to track it, Civis Systems, is throwing flags on readings out of West Oakland, Fruitvale, and East Oakland that run against the safety direction the initiative is supposed to produce. Numbers going the wrong way while the paperwork says the thing is working.

Now here's the part that should stop you cold: the same audit throwing that flag also admits there's no prior read to check it against. No baseline. Nothing on record to measure the trend line by. Civis Systems is telling three neighborhoods something's wrong, and it can't tell you wrong compared to what.

I drove out to Fruitvale to see if the feeling matched. On a corner near the boarded-up strip, Ophelia Brenner, another senior pastor working that neighborhood, said almost the same thing back to me, word for word close enough to make you wonder if the whole city's having the identical bad dream.

"I've noticed that there seems to be a disconnect between what's being reported about the initiative's progress and the concerns I'm hearing from my community about safety," Brenner said.

Ramon Solano, retired now but still working his Fruitvale congregation, put it plainer.

"I've noticed that there seems to be a disconnect between what's being said about the initiative's progress and the concerns I'm hearing from people in my community about safety," Solano told me. "It's something that's been on my mind lately."

And Lucia Polito, who runs her own wellness practice out of Fruitvale, didn't dress it up any softer.

"I've noticed that there's a sense of uncertainty in the air," Polito said. "The initiative's status doesn't quite match the safety concerns I've been hearing about in my neighborhood, Fruitvale."

Four people, two neighborhoods, one line: the report and the street don't match. That's not a coincidence — that's a pattern of harm nobody's owning. The initiative gets to say "active" because the tracker has a status field with only so many boxes to check. The alarm gets to ring because Civis Systems has a threshold to trip. Neither one has to answer for the gap between them, because apparently nobody built the gap into anybody's job description.

So before the city hands me another status update on the Alternative Response Initiative, somebody owes me this: Civis Systems is flagging a contradiction in West Oakland, Fruitvale, and East Oakland with no comparison on record to validate the direction it claims to be measuring. What evidence supports the alert?
