# Chinatown Carries the Most Sick Residents, and the Care Trail Stops Short of the Bed

Millicent Kumar keeps her head down over a fitting and talks to the pipe more than to the question. She is a plumber working in Chinatown, and she says her days have not changed much. "I keep my head down, do my work." Then she stops, because the thing she came to say is about someone else's routine. "But I did hear from Selena that some of the older folks on her block have been staying in more, which isn't like them. That's got me a little worried."

Her worry has a number behind it. Chinatown carries 145 sick residents this cycle, three more than the cycle before and the most of any neighborhood in the record. Against a population of 2,185 in the neighborhood demographics, that is 6.6 percent, at or above the 6 percent watch bar the record sets. Those are the people the city counts as sick. They are not the same people as the six Chinatown residents the hospital record places in care.

That gap is where the care trail runs thin. Chinatown logged three admissions this cycle. At the close, six Chinatown residents were in care, and six beds were occupied. The census records no tracked residents in Chinatown's hospital count, only six other residents. The record does not say what tracking means in that column, and it does not say where those six are, what they are being treated for, or how long they will stay. A neighbor reading the census would see a number that is not a story, and a family would see a person in a bed or no one at all.

Daphne Stafford, a home health aide in Chinatown, lives on the other side of that gap. She describes what it looks like from inside a household: "It's been tough for families here in Chinatown. With more people getting sick, there's a lot of worry about how to care for loved ones while keeping up with work and daily life. Many are juggling doctor visits, medications, and trying to keep everyone healthy at home. It's a lot to handle, especially when resources feel stretched." She did not give a count of the households she has seen, and this piece will not supply one. Her words describe the load, and the load is what the record cannot show.

Yu Zen is one of the six residents the hospital record names this cycle. Yu Zen is a commercial insurance broker, listed in Rockridge, and was admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. The hospital record shows Yu Zen as active in care. From a bed, Yu Zen hears the nursing staff talking about Chinatown and speaks about it as a neighbor would: "From this hospital bed, all I hear are the nurses talking about how Chinatown's been hit hard. It's not just numbers—it's families, neighbors. When you're lying here, you feel the weight of it. Chinatown's tight-knit; when one suffers, it ripples through everyone."

Across the city the pattern is steady. The citywide hospital census shows 38 admissions this cycle and 77 residents in care at the close, with 76 beds occupied. The city's state summary puts the citywide illness rate at 4.5 percent. East Oakland logged 125 sick residents, up three, with six in care and six beds occupied. Ivy Hill logged 122, up three. Fruitvale logged 121, up two. Rockridge logged 110, up two, with two admissions and five in care. One of those five is Maurice Franklin, admitted in cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu and recorded as recovering. Adams Point logged 90 sick residents with four in care. Brooklyn logged 79 with four in care.

The record also shows some people turning a corner. Jessie Hess, in Downtown, finally shook the cold that had been hanging around. Jett Walker, in Rockridge, did the same. Jackson Sift dealt with a seasonal health concern in West Oakland. Ron Arenson recovered from a winter illness in the Baylight District. None of these four is quoted here, and the record does not describe how any of them was treated.

On the access side, the one health service line in the record this cycle is the Temescal Community Health Center, listed as operational in Temescal. The record does not say that it takes Chinatown patients, and it does not say how a Chinatown household would reach it, what the wait is, or whether it is one of the six beds in use.

What the community can do about the spread is a question the record does not answer, and it is the one Chinatown is asking. The next record this beat needs is the one that would connect the 145 people counted as sick to the six in care and the six beds: who is in each bed, who is covering the shift at home, and what a family in Chinatown is told to do when someone starts to get worse. What steps can the community take to mitigate the spread of illness in Chinatown? Until the record answers, Millicent Kumar will keep her head down over the work, and Daphne Stafford will keep juggling the visits.
