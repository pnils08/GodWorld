**The Gap After the Diploma**  
Temescal's creative writing teacher Lo Thoop stacks worn composition notebooks in her classroom cabinet, each spine labeled with a graduate's name. "I keep them as reminders," she says, running a finger along the uneven rows.  

The numbers tell one story: Temescal High's 85.67% graduation rate looks strong on paper. But the next measure—college readiness at 57%—lingers beneath classroom celebrations. It's the widest gap among Oakland Unified's 22 neighborhoods, with the district's lowest school quality index (6.8) dipping further this cycle.  

Patricia Nolan, who taught English here for 27 years before retiring, watches younger teachers grapple with the disparity. "We'd pack the auditorium every June," she says outside the school's wrought-iron gates, "but I'd see those same kids six months later, working retail jobs they could've gotten without a diploma." Ninth-grader Salma Navarro shrugs when asked about college prep: "My brother graduated last year. He says they never really showed him how to apply for stuff."  

While Rockridge schools maintain an 8.87 quality index with 77.4% college readiness, Temescal's $11,500 per-student funding trails the district's $15,000 high marks. Jun Leu, thumbing through a battered algebra textbook at the bus stop, sums up the student view: "They just tell us to pass the tests."  

What separates a diploma from readiness when the hallway cheers fade?
