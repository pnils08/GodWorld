## Chinatown Bleeds Ink, Not Money

Dragon Gate Lounge on a Tuesday night, fog thick enough to chew, and the karaoke machine is skipping between songs nobody picked. I'm two stools down from Tariq Harris, who spends his days scraping tartar off molars a few blocks from here and his nights watching the room fill up with faces he doesn't recognize.

"Honestly, I've noticed that Chinatown doesn't seem to be getting the attention it needs," Tariq tells me, turning his glass in a slow circle. "As someone who lives and works here, it's frustrating to see the decay and feel like nothing's being done about it." He's not talking in the abstract. He's talking about rent. "I've seen new people moving in from West Oakland, but it's not like that's necessarily a good thing — it just means they're being priced out of their own neighborhood. It feels like we're just trying to hold on to what we have, instead of actually moving forward."

That's the part nobody in a suit wants to say out loud: the lounge stays lively because people who got squeezed out of West Oakland are landing here, and landing here is getting more expensive by the week. New bodies in the room. Same bad math on the lease.

Two seats over, Jonas Patel is nursing something dark and not talking much — until he does. Patel's a medical examiner. He sees the tail end of things other people only feel in their gut. "I've noticed that Chinatown seems to be struggling, and it feels like there's not much being done to address the issues," he says. "As a medical examiner, I see the effects of this decay in my work." I don't push him on specifics. I don't have to. A man who processes the dead for a living telling you a neighborhood feels like it's failing is not a metaphor.

Jacquez Tearn washes dishes a few blocks from the lounge, been in this neighborhood longer than most of the regulars at the bar. He's not dramatic about it — just tired. "I feel like Chinatown's been struggling, you know, it's not as lively as it used to be and I've noticed people talking about how things are falling apart around here," he says. "I care about this neighborhood and I wish someone would do something about it."

Three people. Three jobs that have nothing to do with each other — a dental assistant, a medical examiner, a dishwasher. All three land on the same word without being asked twice: decay.

I went looking for the thing that's supposed to be catching this. A program, a fund, a task force, anything with Chinatown's name attached to it on the record right now. I didn't find one. Not a mitigator. Not an initiative. Nothing filed against this neighborhood while its numbers sit near the bottom of the city and its housing pressure sits well above where it should. Compare that to the noise coming out of West Oakland these days — offices, announcements, program names people can actually cite. Chinatown gets rent refugees from that same pressure and gets silence in return.

Nobody's claiming a crime here. Nobody's cutting a check and pocketing it. This is simpler and somehow worse: a neighborhood shows every sign of coming apart at the seams and there is no name on a roster anywhere that owns fixing it. Not a councilmember's initiative. Not a city program. Nothing.

So here's the question I'd like answered by whoever's supposed to be watching the board: the map says decay; the program roster does not answer it. Who's supposed to be holding that line, and why is it empty?
