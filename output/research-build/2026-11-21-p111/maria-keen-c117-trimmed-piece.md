# The Name Behind the Cup

I don't have the name of the person who pours the coffee at the cart outside the hospital. That's the gap in this piece, and I'd sooner say so up front.

Three people told me about the cart this cycle. None of them could tell me who runs it. One said so outright. The other two talked about the people at the cart as if they were already friends, and still didn't give me names.

**The quiet part of the morning**

Alisha Peña lives in Lake Merritt and models for a living. She gets to the cart around 7:30, just before the rush. "At that hour, it's pretty quiet," she told me, "and I mostly see hospital staff and a few early risers like myself grabbing their coffee to go."

Then she said what I keep thinking about. "I'm not sure who pours the coffee, to be honest, I've never really paid attention to that. I just know it's always good and gets me going for the day."

I don't hold that against her. Most of us treat the first good thing in a morning that way. We take it and don't ask where it came from. But it's a small version of how a city runs. Someone was awake before you, and the warm cup in your hand is the evidence.

**Someone who does know**

Tobias Jurko is a landscape architect who works along Piedmont Avenue. His relationship with the cart is different. "The people running it know me," he said. "We exchange a hello, and I get my coffee just the way I like it. It's a small moment, but it sets a positive tone."

His reason for coming matches Alisha's. "The cart is usually there before the hospital cafeteria gets busy, so it's a quiet moment to myself too."

Tina Kozlov, a dental hygienist from Grand Lake, stops on her way to work. "The people who work the cart are always friendly, and it's quiet before the morning rush hits," she said. "I notice the hospital staff and other early risers around me, they're usually in a hurry but it's a calm atmosphere. I appreciate the coffee cart, it makes my morning a bit easier."

These three live in three different neighborhoods and have three different jobs. Each of them builds a small piece of the day around the same cart before the building behind it fills up.

**What the record can't tell me**

I looked for the cart. I checked our earlier coverage and the Business Ledger. The Ledger lists coffee businesses in Brooklyn, Temescal and Adams Point, but nothing that looks like a cart outside a hospital. Our own archive mentions it once, in passing, as a place where someone grabbed a coffee before a meeting. In the record, the cart is a place people stop. The people behind it aren't in the record.

That's how it usually goes with early-hour work. The night watchers, the janitors, the kitchen crews and the people at the cart all show up in other people's stories as scenery. The regulars know them by a hello and a coffee made the way they like it. That counts for something. But a hello isn't a name, and I'd like to put a name in print.

**What's around it**

The rest of the city is already moving by the time the cart has been open a while. Moving boxes are going into buildings on Piedmont Avenue, where the medical offices have their usual steady stream of appointments. Vans are unloading at one of the older apartment buildings near the lake. Grand Lake has more "now leasing" signs this week. Piedmont Avenue gained the most people of any neighborhood on the demographic sheets, 15 more adults than last cycle. A lot of people are arriving in a lot of places, and plenty of them will go looking for a good first coffee.

I'm going back before 7:30, before Alisha's early-riser crowd. I'll ask the person at the cart for a name, and for how early they have to start to be ready. If they're willing, I'll tell you next cycle. If you know who they are, tell me. If you don't, go one morning this week and ask.
