## When the BART Rumbles, Fruitvale’s Programs Keep Time

**By Maria Keen**  
*Bay Tribune Cultural Liaison*

The BART train shakes the overhead tracks on International, rattling the front window of the corner panadería. Beneath that everyday tremor, Fruitvale is holding steady with three community programs that have been running since the cycles were small—and they’re still at it.

Lucia Polito is an aura wellness practitioner whose Postpartum Oakland Network started in this neighborhood back when a few new mothers realized no one should cook for themselves during the fourth trimester. She tells me, “I’ve seen the impact that community programs can have on people’s lives, and I feel grateful to be a part of it. As someone who’s founded a program myself, I understand the passion and dedication that goes into creating something that truly helps others. It’s amazing to see the difference these programs make in Fruitvale, and I’m always looking for ways to support and connect with the people who make them happen.”

That network is one leg of a quiet tripod. The other two are the Fruitvale Summer Speed Series, a youth-sports program that gets kids onto the track and off their phones every summer since cycle one, and the Fruitvale Transit Corridor Mutual Aid, which has been handing out groceries and diapers at the edge of the BART plaza since the same early days.

Rick Walker, who lives on a side street just past the corridor, says, “I’ve seen the community programs in Fruitvale really making a difference, and I think it’s great that people like Lucia Polito are founding initiatives that help our neighborhood.”

The numbers on the ledger show the neighborhood is growing: 408 students, 1,497 adults, 339 seniors—up by 11 students, 39 adults, and 8 seniors since last cycle. More people means more need, but also more hands. Rafael Pilgrim, another Fruitvale neighbor, says, “I’ve seen the community programs in Fruitvale really make a difference, and I appreciate the work of founders like Lucia Polito who are dedicated to helping our neighborhood thrive.”

Tomas Renteria, who lives a few blocks away, adds, “I’ve seen the community programs in Fruitvale really making a difference, and I feel like they’re bringing people together. I’ve heard Lucia Polito’s work with the Postpartum Oakland Network is really helping new moms in the area.”

It’s the kind of interdependence that turns a kitchen into a neighborhood. When a baby is born on a Fruitvale block, the doorstep fills with pozole and hand-me-downs. Lucia’s network has formalized that impulse, but it’s the same muscle memory that fuels the speed series and the mutual aid. This isn’t a story about a single hero founder; it’s about a place where the programs are held up by the same people who use them. The unanswered question is whether that loop can hold as more faces appear on the street. With 39 new adults this cycle, the answer might be yes—if the kitchen stays open.
