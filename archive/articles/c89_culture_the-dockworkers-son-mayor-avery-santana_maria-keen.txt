THE CYCLE PULSE 89 — SUPPLEMENTAL — CULTURE & COMMUNITY_Mayor Avery Santana
The Dockworker’s Son
By Maria Keen | Bay Tribune

OAKLAND — On Saturday mornings, before the city wakes up and the phone starts ringing, Avery Santana walks the Lake Merritt loop.

He starts at the pergola near Grand Avenue, where the joggers haven’t yet crowded the path and the water catches the first light like something borrowed from a dream. He walks counterclockwise — always counterclockwise — past the bird sanctuary, past the boathouse, past the spot where he proposed to his wife twenty-three years ago.

“She said yes before I finished the question,” he told me, laughing in that rare way he has — the laugh that doesn’t make it into press conferences. “I had this whole speech prepared. Three pages. She cut me off at paragraph two.”

Her name is Adrienne. They met at a community legal clinic in West Oakland in 1998, when he was a second-year law student and she was an elementary school teacher volunteering on Saturdays. They have two grown children: Marcus, twenty-seven, who works in renewable energy in Portland, and Elise, twenty-four, who just finished her residency at Highland Hospital.

The Mayor of Oakland lives in Montclair now — a three-bedroom craftsman with a view of the hills — but he grew up in East Oakland, on 82nd Avenue, in a two-bedroom apartment above a laundromat. His father worked the docks at the Port of Oakland for thirty-one years. His mother taught ninth-grade English at Fremont High for twenty-eight.

“They never missed a shift,” Santana said. “Either of them. That was the rule in our house. You show up. You do the work. Everything else is conversation.”

-----

THE ATTORNEY

Before he was the people’s mayor, Avery Santana was the people’s lawyer.

He graduated from Berkeley Law in 2000 and went straight into civil rights litigation — housing discrimination, police misconduct, employment retaliation. He never chased the big corporate cases. He stayed in the neighborhoods, in the storefronts, in the community centers where people who couldn’t afford downtown firms came looking for help.

“I had offers,” he said. “Good ones. San Francisco. Los Angeles. But I kept thinking about my father. He unloaded ships for three decades so I could have choices. What kind of choice would it be to leave the place that made me?”

He built a reputation as a tenacious litigator — the kind of attorney who wouldn’t settle when settling meant his client got less than they deserved. He won a landmark housing case in 2011 that established new protections for tenants facing illegal evictions. He represented families after the 2009 BART shooting. He never stopped believing that the law could be a tool for ordinary people, not just a shield for the powerful.

“The law is only as good as who’s holding it,” he said. “Put it in the wrong hands, it’s a weapon. Put it in the right hands, it’s a door.”

-----

THE CANDIDATE

He ran for mayor in 2036 because no one else would.

The incumbent had been indicted on corruption charges. The establishment candidates were retreading the same promises that had failed the city for a decade. Santana watched from the sidelines — he was fifty years old, comfortable in his practice, not looking for a career change.

Then Beverly Hayes called him.

She was on the board of the West Oakland Neighbors Association. She’d known him for years — he’d represented her cousin in a wrongful termination case back in 2008. She asked him to come to a community meeting at the recreation center on Peralta Street.

“I thought it was going to be ten people,” Santana said. “There were a hundred and forty. Standing room only. They weren’t asking me to run. They were telling me. Beverly looked me in the eye and said, ‘You’ve spent your whole life fighting for people who can’t fight for themselves. Now fight for all of us.’”

He won with sixty-two percent of the vote. The pundits called it an insurgent campaign. Santana called it a neighborhood campaign.

“I didn’t run against the establishment,” he said. “I ran with the people the establishment forgot.”

-----

THE MAYOR

Five years in office. Five years of budget fights and housing crises and infrastructure gaps and police reform and pandemic recovery and now, finally, Baylight.

The stadium is his legacy project — the thing that will define his tenure long after he’s gone. But when I asked him about it, he didn’t talk about the piles going in on October tenth or the TIF financing or the groundbreaking ceremony. He talked about a kid on 82nd Avenue.

“When I was eight years old, my father took me to the Coliseum for the first time,” he said. “We sat in the upper deck. The A’s lost. I don’t remember the score. What I remember is my father’s face — the way he looked at that field like it was the most beautiful thing he’d ever seen. He worked the docks his whole life. He never complained. But in that stadium, for those three hours, he wasn’t tired. He wasn’t worried about the bills. He was just… present.”

He paused.

“That’s what I’m trying to build. Not a stadium. A place where the dockworker’s son can sit with his father and feel like the city belongs to them.”

-----

THE PAULSON QUESTION

I asked the mayor about Mike Paulson. Two men who came to Oakland from elsewhere and decided to stay. Two men building things they want to outlast them.

“Paulson and I don’t agree on everything,” Santana said carefully. “We’ve had disagreements. Some of them heated. But I respect him because he does the work. He doesn’t just show up for the ribbon-cutting. He’s in the room at two in the morning when the deal is falling apart. He knows the names of the carpenters on the Baylight site. He knows Beverly Hayes by name.”

He smiled.

“You know what he told me once? He said, ‘The build never becomes the built.’ I think about that a lot. The work is never finished. You just keep showing up.”

It’s a philosophy they share — the dockworker’s son and the carpenter’s son. Two men who believe that what you build should outlast the hands that built it. Two men who chose Oakland when they could have chosen anywhere.

“Mike could run three teams if he wanted,” Santana said. “He could be in Chicago full-time. He could chase the next thing. But he keeps coming back here. He keeps saying Oakland is home. That matters to me. That’s not marketing. That’s conviction.”

-----

THE WALK

On Saturday mornings, before the phone rings and the city wakes up, Avery Santana walks the Lake Merritt loop.

He passes the spot where he proposed to Adrienne. He passes the boathouse where his daughter learned to row. He passes the joggers and the dog-walkers and the old men playing chess on the benches near the amphitheater.

Sometimes he stops at the pergola and looks out at the water. He thinks about his father, who never got to see him become mayor. He thinks about Beverly Hayes, who called him when the city needed someone to fight. He thinks about the kid on 82nd Avenue who sat in the upper deck and watched his father’s face light up.

“Oakland without the A’s is like a bay without tide,” he told me. It’s his motto — the thing he says at press conferences and groundbreakings and campaign rallies. But when he said it to me, standing by the water on a Saturday morning, it sounded different. It sounded like a prayer.

“The tide always comes back,” he said. “That’s the whole point. It goes out. It comes back. You just have to be here when it does.”
