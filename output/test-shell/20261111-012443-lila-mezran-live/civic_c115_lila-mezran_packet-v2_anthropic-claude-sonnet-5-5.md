# Chinatown's 135: Three Admissions, Six Beds, and the Houses Between

Akua Adeyemi keeps her voice low when she works in Chinatown, and she has learned to keep her wrench quiet too. The plumber has been in enough kitchens to know when a house is minding someone who is unwell.

"I go into a lot of homes, and you can tell pretty quick when someone's down," Adeyemi said. "It's not the mess or the dishes — it's the quiet. The TV off in the middle of the day. A chair pulled up by the window where somebody's been sitting a long time. Sometimes the medicine gets moved to the kitchen counter so it's right there where someone'll remember it. And you learn to work quiet, not to bang pipes, because somebody's sleeping in the next room. I see more of that in Chinatown than anywhere else I work. People here take care of their own — you'll see a neighbor bring soup, or a cousin come by after their shift. It's not always the hospital that carries the load. It's the family that rearranges the whole house around one sick person."

The neighborhood demographics sheet for this cycle puts 135 Chinatown residents among the sick. That is out of 2,205 residents, or 6.1 percent, at or over the 6 percent watch bar. It is the highest count in the city. East Oakland follows at 117, then Ivy Hill at 115, Fruitvale at 109, and Laurel and San Antonio at 107 each. Citywide the sheet counts 1,771 sick residents across 22 neighborhoods.

**The direction of travel is small, and it is down.** Chinatown is 2 below last cycle. The Tribune's C114 coverage printed 137. Its C112 coverage printed 142, from a population of 2,193, which came to 6.5 percent. Across those cycles the count has come down by 7, and the neighborhood has stayed above the line. Laurel was the only neighborhood in the lead group to rise this cycle, by 1. Every other neighborhood in that group fell by 2 or 3. In clinic shorthand, a slow decline is better than a slow climb. It is not the same as being clear of the bar.

**The hospital's part of the count is small.** The care census records 3 admissions from Chinatown this cycle. At the close it holds 6 Chinatown residents in care, in 6 occupied beds. None of the six is tracked, which means the census counts them without following them by name. Citywide the hospital took in 34 admissions, held 69 in care in 69 beds, and saw 35 leave. At the C113 close the paper printed 35 admissions, 71 in care and 37 discharged. The hospital load is steady, with only small changes between cycles.

Other neighborhoods show the same pattern at smaller scale. Adams Point has 82 sick residents, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Dimond has 90 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Eastlake has 86 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. East Oakland has 117 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Chinatown has the largest sick count and the largest number of beds in use, at 6. Even so, six beds is a small share of 135 people.

**None of the four residents named on this cycle's hospital and cause records lives in Chinatown.** Yu Zen, of Rockridge, was admitted in C105 after a workplace accident. Deacon Seymour, of Lake Merritt, was admitted the same cycle after a workplace accident. Maurice Franklin, also of Rockridge, was admitted in C106 with severe seasonal flu. The world summary lists Howard Travis, on Piedmont Ave, as recovering. He caught up on sleep and woke feeling like himself again. Together they make up the names on the record this cycle. Chinatown has none.

Seymour, the Lake Merritt manager of the Oakland A's, has spoken before about the difference between a named bed and an unnamed one. He returned to the point for this count.

"I spent my own stretch in a hospital bed not long ago, and I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you," Seymour said. "But I also know that bed I was in—that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city, and if the ledger doesn't show them, that doesn't mean they're not there. It means nobody's counting. And when nobody's counting, people get missed. That's the part that keeps me up at night."

The sheets cannot say what the missing counting would show. They do not say how many of Chinatown's 135 are cared for at home, or by whom. They do not say who covers a shift when a cousin is the one in the chair by the window. They also do not say whether the 3 admissions and the 6 beds reflect everyone who needed a bed, or only those who got one. The record is silent on all of it. Any answer would have to come from someone who counts households, not hospital beds.

The question for the health office is the next set of figures: how long each sick resident has been ill, and what each household is doing about it. Until those numbers exist, Chinatown's count can be reported, and what it means can only be guessed at.
