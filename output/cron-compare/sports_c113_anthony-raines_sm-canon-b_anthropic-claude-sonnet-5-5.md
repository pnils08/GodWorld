# The Outfield Math: Three Averages Within .026, One Opening

Desmond Morton's season line on his career card reads 80 games, 285 at-bats, .291 average, .348 on-base, .498 slugging, 7 home runs, 44 RBI and 28 steals. It is the least impressive average of three outfielders competing for one left-field spot, which is why it needs checking before anyone leans on it.

The feed's off-season rumor row sets the board. Danny Horn is the center fielder, and the row calls him the returning MVP. Vladimir Gonzalez holds right, and the row says he is coming off the best numbers of his career. A designated hitter is already under contract. That leaves one opening in left and three candidates: Morton, Frank Reyna and JR Rosado. The row sums it up as five outfielders for three positions.

## The averages do not separate them

Reyna's card shows .317 in 164 at-bats. Rosado's shows .298 in 184. Morton's is .291 in 285. On batting average alone the order is Reyna, Rosado, Morton, which is the order the narrative will likely take.

The averages sit close together, though, and the cards split on everything else. Reyna slugs .683 against Morton's .498 and Rosado's .487. Reyna's 14 home runs come at one every 11.7 at-bats on his card. Morton's 7 come at one every 40.7, and Rosado's 7 at one every 26.3. That gap is real, but Reyna's sample is 46 games.

## What the .291 carries

Morton's card shows 23 walks to Reyna's 6 and Rosado's 12, which lifts his on-base to .348. Reyna's is .339 and Rosado's is .340. So the bat with the lowest average has the highest on-base of the three. Reyna's .317 average needs a good deal of its support from hits rather than walks.

Morton also has 28 stolen bases on his card, against 7 for Reyna and 2 for Rosado. The feed's C102 line had him at 249 at-bats, .305, 7 home runs and 22 steals. His average has come down since and the home-run count has not moved. That fits a bat doing something other than power. The 7 home runs in 285 at-bats are a ceiling question, and the feed prints no pace or projection to answer it. What the card does support is volume: Morton has the most games and at-bats of the three by a wide margin. His 49 strikeouts come at one every 5.8 at-bats, the highest rate of the three. Reyna's rate is one every 9.1 and Rosado's one every 8.0. His contact is the softest of the three. He pairs it with the most walks and the most steals.

## Is it real?

The usual reasoning applies. A rate that needs volume to be believed should not be believed on 164 at-bats. Reyna's .317 and his 14 home runs came in 46 games. That is enough to say he hit well and not enough to say how a full season would go. The paper's C94 coverage had Reyna going deep twice in a game, and the C93 call-up note logged his first big-league hit. Those are the early entries in what is still a short record. Morton's 285 at-bats carry more weight because they cover more of a season. The C97 debut coverage and the C100 call-up piece covered him before this volume existed. The C111 piece framed the left-field job as a Morton-versus-Reyna contest for the one everyday spot, with the loser as fourth outfielder. Rosado has since joined that contest.

Rosado's card is the easiest to place. His .298 and .340 on-base look like Morton's with less speed, less patience and a lower sample. The feed's own wording calls him a possible role player, and the card does not argue otherwise. He does not separate himself on any single axis.

## Fit

The feed lists Reyna as a right fielder and Gonzalez as the right fielder it treats as settled. Putting Reyna in left is therefore a position question as well as a hitting one, and his card cannot answer it. The card holds no defensive line. Morton's card carries a left-field label that matches the opening.

The most defensible reading is that the three batting averages do not decide this. Morton's .291 holds up because it comes with the most at-bats, the most walks and the most stolen bases. Reyna's slugging is the strongest single number among the candidates, but it is the least tested.

The questions the record has not yet answered:

- Does Reyna's power hold over an at-bat total closer to Morton's?
- Does Morton's slugging, 7 home runs in 285 at-bats, come with enough extra-base production to justify the corner?
- Does the front office trust the player who proved he can play every day, or the player who proved he can hit when the matchup favors him?
