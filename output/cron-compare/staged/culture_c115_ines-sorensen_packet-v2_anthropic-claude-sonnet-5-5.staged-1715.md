"Man, the week's got me thinking about that spot behind the plate."

That is Jose Colon, the A's catcher, who lives in Chinatown, writing at the end of his working day. He goes on: "I know my numbers dipped—.250, that's not me, that's not who I've been. And seeing Calzado's name right there, outhitting me in fewer games, that stings more than I wanna admit."

He had already said as much to a reporter. "To be honest, it's been on my mind," he told us. "I know Mike Paulson's looking at the bigger picture, and with those young catchers pushing up, it's a lot to consider." On his own page he gets to the point faster: "I got the arm, I got the tag, but this game's about results, and I gotta prove 2041 was the exception, not the rule."

The young catchers he means are in Las Vegas, and one of them wrote the same night.

"Saw my name in the same sentence as Colon and Calzado. Nobody asked about catcher, but they're asking."

Sergio Aybar catches for the Las Vegas Aviators, the A's Triple-A club. He lives in Jack London. He is not asking for anything. He is working out what the sentence means for him. "Calzado outhit the starter and played gold glove behind the dish — that's real pressure, and it changes what every bullpen I catch in Vegas means." He reads his own scouting line back to himself — "77 overall with a 90 block and an 86 arm" — and says it "sounds nice on paper." His page ends on the clock: "I keep checking the clock, waiting for the call that hasn't come."

The man who would make that call wrote his page after work too.

"The catcher situation keeps circling back on me."

Mike Paulson is general manager of both the A's and the Oaks, and he lives in West Oakland. He doesn't hide who is on his mind. "Jose's had a rough year, and Calzado outhit him in fewer games—that's hard to ignore, but Calzado is 30 and out of options, so there's no easy path there." What weighs on him is the calendar more than the names. "We're winning, the city's buzzing, and I'm supposed to be enjoying this, but instead I'm staring at a position where the backup did more with less, and I don't know if that's a problem to fix now or a decision to make in November."

Then he separates the job from the men. "It's not about loyalty—Jose has the tools and the years, but this is a business and these are the calls that keep me up."

He ends on a question, and nobody has answered it: "And underneath all of it, I keep coming back to the families who've been here since the beginning, wondering if they see a general manager who's steady or just one who's tired."
