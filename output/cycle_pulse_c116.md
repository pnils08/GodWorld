Mags Corliss here, with the week in Oakland.

Start with the door. In Rockridge, Benji Dillon stood in the cereal aisle at Canopy Market with a basket on his forearm while a woman two carts down looked, looked away, and looked again. As Kai Marston reported, Dillon nodded the way a man nods at a neighbor. "I'm still just Benji, living on my block, and going to A's games," he told Marston. Across town, Tanya Cruz found the same man as a question nobody will force. Mike Paulson says he won't push a five-time Cy Young winner into a decision because the calendar wants one. Deacon Seymour says the club is planning as if Benji isn't coming back and leaving the light on anyway. Vinnie Keane, who walked through his own door first in C115, put it in the hallway: Benji has the clinic, Rick, Maya, and a city that remembers three no-hitters. So the man says it isn't a big change, and the people around him are building a roster around a gap shaped like him. I don't think either is lying. A man can be ordinary in a grocery aisle and still be the biggest open question in the building.

That building is emptying out in its own way. Keane is gone, and Mark Aitken is gone to Colorado. On the Lives Page, Tamsin Pruitt caught Edmund Alegero, a construction laborer who just got a place in Lake Merritt with only his name on the lease, saying it's tough to see Aitken leave because he's from around here and always involved in the community. P Slayer had Carmen Mesa, standing in the neighborhood where the news landed, describing folks still reeling from the same departure. That is the same man seen from two desks, and neither is writing about a stat line. Aitken's leaving registers in a laborer's new kitchen and in a pitcher's neighborhood, not on a transaction wire. When I say a ballplayer is a neighbor, this is what I mean.

Mesa himself became the week's argument. The A's sent him, with Porter Broadway and Jose Fuentes, to the Phillies for Giancarlo Hernandez and Antone Baez. Paulson calls it maximizing return on a player who didn't fit the rotation. Anthony Raines mostly backs the pitching side. The rotation had no open slot, Mesa had been optioned once Henry Rivas returned, and his strikeout-to-walk rates are the kind of stable signal a front office should trust, which is exactly why he sold well. Raines finds the infield half harder to defend. Hernandez makes three A-potential third basemen, and Ernesto Quintero had just taken the job. Slayer calls it inventory management and refuses to applaud. Kevin Clark, glove still on in the tunnel, said it's a competition and he's ready for it, and Paulson says the same word, battle. Quintero, who also turned up in Marston's culture reporting this week, is the one carrying it. The city is getting to know him as a face off the field just as the club hands his position to a contest. What the trade means for him is simple. Everything he was just handed is now being re-auditioned.

Now the contradiction I can't smooth over, from Carmen Delaine. Marcus Webb says for the West Oakland Stabilization Fund that the closeout is published and the roll call belongs to the Council. The Initiative_Tracker's own C116 note says final action requires a verified C114 closeout, business-and-resident scope, and the 23-day standard, and it schedules the verification for C117. Brenda Okoro wants the record on the table before the vote. Webb wants the proof to be in the hearing record. Nobody has said who checks the closeout before members raise their hands. Delaine's tranche arithmetic also leaves an $800,000 gap between what the milestone notes itemize and what the budget fields imply, and I haven't seen anyone explain it. This matters because Pruitt's Lives Page shows Wanjiru Liu, an electrician newly in a Rockridge rental, helping a neighbor through that same portal because the interface was confusing. The fund is a promise to real people who are navigating it by hand.

The people who hold the city up don't always show on the books. Dr. Lila Mezran counted Chinatown's six beds and found six "other residents" with no names, in the neighborhood with the highest sick count of the city's 22. The only names in the hospital ledger this cycle were Yu Zen, Maurice Franklin, and Deacon Seymour. Seymour is the same man who sat on the edge of his desk for Cruz and said you leave the light on. Zen is the commercial insurance broker who told Mezran about the weekend he kept working and the wife who held the house and the colleagues who carried his accounts. He said that in the bed you think about the people who carried you there, and the ones who carried you before you fell. Pruitt's page carries him too, and I'd read the two together. Seymour keeping a light on for a pitcher and Zen remembering who kept his house standing are the same impulse seen from different rooms. Being carried is how this city works, and Chinatown's six beds are people no one has yet named well enough to carry.

Ines Sorensen's fence belongs here as well. Tre Mingo, who works the port in Uptown, says the numbers look good and the folks on the ledger aren't the ones who get to stay. Guadalupe Lupe, bartending in Jack London, hears rent worry from the regulars, and Mingo's name came up. Omar Amici, in construction, finds the streets busier and every other place for rent. Mingo asks whether any of us who work here can still stay. I told Sorensen I don't have an answer, and I still don't. I'll say only that Alegero's own place in Lake Merritt, Liu's in Rockridge, and the youth apprenticeship flyer stapled over an older notice in Marston's piece, promising spots without saying how many or who decides, all sit on the same fence.

For the lighter side, Nia Rook's UNDOCKED digest says Luis Taz still leads the board for a third cycle. Marguerite Kimball-Ostrow and Manju Abebe flew until time ran out, and Soo Loo closed a contract. Drago Helio staked 460 and was paid 842. It's a reminder that a city can play for points and not only for rent.

What I'm watching next week, C117: whether the Council verifies the C114 closeout before it votes on charter renewal, and who says so out loud. Whether Benji Dillon walks through that open door, in either direction. Whether Hernandez, Clark, and Quintero turn a crowded third base into a fight or a fracture. And whether anyone puts a name beside one of Chinatown's six beds.
