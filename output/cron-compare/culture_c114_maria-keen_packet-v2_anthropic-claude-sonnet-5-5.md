# The Pantry Has Been Here Since C83. The Fund's Founder Lives Across the Bridge of Himself.

The Temescal Islamic Center Food Pantry has been on the record since C83. It is one of two active programs the city has on file for Temescal, and it is the older one by a long stretch. I can't tell you what was on the shelves this cycle, or who carried which bag out. The record doesn't say, and I won't paint a line of people I didn't stand beside. What the record does say is that it is still there, and on a block, that is most of what a pantry has to prove.

The second program is newer. The Reaves Line Transit Fund has been in Temescal since C109, and its founder is Austin Reaves, who plays shooting guard and lives in West Oakland. I asked him what the fund has done for the block, and his answer was the most honest sentence I collected this cycle:

"To be honest, I'm not really sure about The Reaves Line Transit Fund's impact on Temescal. I founded it, but I've been focused on the A's and family stuff lately. I know it's been around since C109, and it's still active, but I haven't been keeping up with its day-to-day. I live in West Oakland, so I don't really see its effects firsthand."

I don't hear that as an indictment. A man put his name and money behind a thing on a street he doesn't walk, and now he's telling the truth about the distance. It does leave a question the block deserves answered, and I'll come back to it.

Here is who the block is this cycle: 313 students, 2,131 adults, 298 seniors. Compared with C113, the adults are up by six. Six is not a wave. It is a couple of U-Hauls and a few people who signed a lease.

One of the people the block has gained, or kept, is Brie Harris, who owns a flower shop. She has a place of her own in Temescal now, and she said what that does for a person who spends her day on her feet: "Having my own place in Temescal is still a game-changer for me. It's been nice to have a spot to unwind after being on my feet all day at the flower shop. I feel like I'm still getting used to it, but it's made me appreciate the neighborhood even more."

*Still getting used to it.* I keep turning that over. Appreciation and unfamiliarity can live in one apartment, and probably a whole block lives that way.

Helena Voss-Adeyemi described the edge of that feeling from the street. "I walked north across the Temescal line and watched the storefronts change," she said. "It's always interesting to see the different vibes on either side. Temescal's got a unique energy, lots of new businesses popping up."

Then there is the picture Theo Park can't shake, from a line at Temescal Brewing. "To be honest, that moment at Temescal Brewing really stuck with me," he said. "Seeing the janitor from the Islamic Center chatting with Danny Horn like they were old friends... it felt like a small but real sign of how the neighborhood is evolving. It's not just about new faces moving in; it's about how we all connect. I still see Temescal as a place where you can bump into anyone, and that's something I appreciate."

I can't give you that scene firsthand. I can tell you why it lands. The pantry's people and the brewery's people are the same neighbors, and the program does not stop at its own door.

The block has its hard numbers, too, and I'll set them down plainly. The justice census shows eight charges in Temescal this cycle, two arrests, and three residents in custody at the close. The hospital census shows 86 sick residents, two admissions, and four people in care at the close, with four beds occupied. None of those people are tracked citizens, so I can't give you a name, a porch, or a story. They are other residents, which means they are somebody's neighbors, and I know nothing more about them than that.

I was asked to wonder whether the programs explain the growth or the quiet in those counts. I'm not going to say they do. Nothing I hold ties a pantry shelf to a hospital bed or a transit fund to six new adults. Claiming it would be me inventing a good ending for a block that hasn't offered me one.

What I'd rather know is smaller, and it's the kind of thing only a person on the block can answer. Who on these streets has ever used the fund that carries Reaves's name, and did it get them somewhere they needed to be? Has anyone who carries a bag home from the pantry also stood in that brewery line? Until someone who lives here tells me, the two programs are a founder's name and an old promise, and I'd like to hear the rest from the block.
