# Chinatown's Sick Are Counted by the Hundred and Named by No One

# The Mechanics of Release: John Ellis and the Arithmetic of Laurel

By Anthony Raines | *Bay Tribune* Special Feature

The morning sun over the Laurel district does not arrive with the dramatic fanfare it brings to the glass penthouses Downtown. It filters through old Monterey pines and tangled telephone cables, catching the dew on the corrugated metal roof of the Laurel Recreation Center before settling into the peeling paint of the 1920s craftsman bungalows that step down toward MacArthur Boulevard. 

On the rear patio of a rented two-bedroom bungalow on 39th Avenue, John Ellis sits on an iron folding chair, rolling a scuffed regulation baseball between the palms of his hands. He is twenty-five years old, 5-foot-10, and two hundred and nine pounds of compact, low-center-of-gravity mass. In the cold accounting of the league’s official books, he is an anomaly: an elite starting pitcher who threw one hundred and eighty-five innings across thirty-two starts in 2040, winning sixteen games against six losses with a 3.89 ERA and fourteen quality starts, yet earning $780,000 on a pre-arbitration contract. 

Down on Broadway, his rotation counterpart Travis Coles, twenty-two and carrying a superstar guarantee north of twenty million, rents high above the pavement with floor-to-ceiling views of the bay. In Rockridge, legendary southpaw Benji Dillon operates behind manicured hedges and runs the Dillon Clinic. Across the neighborhood line, Arturo Ramos owns his craftsman outright on an $8.1 million ledger line. 

Ellis pays $2,150 a month to a landlord who lives in Alameda and still hasn't fixed the sticky back door.

"People read the sports pages and assume every guy who threw six innings in the World Series drives a six-figure coupe," Ellis says, his voice quiet, clipped with the rhythmic vowels of his native New York. He tosses the ball an inch into the air, catching it with a soft slap against his calloused right index finger. "Then I walk down MacArthur to buy milk and the guy behind the counter asks me if I’ve got enough quarters for the coin laundry. I tell him I’m good. It keeps your feet on the concrete."

### I. The 99-Grade Lever

Inside the pitching lab at the Oakland Coliseum, the telemetry on John Ellis reads like a paradoxical blueprint. His raw arm strength is graded at 99—the absolute ceiling of the scouting scale, a metric usually reserved for flame-throwing closers who burn out after thirty pitches. Yet Ellis does not pitch like a man trying to dent the backstop.

His four-seam fastball sits at 94 mph, playing up with above-slot carry and high active spin that lives at the letters. Tunneling off that ride is a sharp, glove-side cutter at 92 mph that chews through the thumbs of left-handed bats, paired with a tight, late-biting 86 mph slider that serves as his primary whiff generator. His changeup, hovering at 84 mph, remains an evolving third offering—a contact-management pitch that works almost entirely because of his rare 70-grade command.

"Command isn't throwing the ball where the catcher puts the mitt," Ellis explains, tracing the raised red seams. "Command is convincing a hitter who makes fifteen million dollars a year that the pitch he’s waiting for is three inches further off the plate than it actually is. It’s an argument. You make your point three times in a row, and then you sit him down."

That philosophy proved decisive in the historic 2041 campaign, when the Athletics ripped through the league to a 127–35 finish. In Y3C3, under the suffocating October glare of the visiting clubhouse in New York, Ellis delivered the defining start of his young career: a four-hit, nine-strikeout complete-game shutout that left the Bronx silent. 

Three weeks later, in Game Five of the World Series in Y3C6, he took the mound with the title hanging in the balance, delivering six steady innings, surrendering only two earned runs and punching out five. Bay Tribune sideline reporter Tanya Cruz found him afterward at his locker stall, athletic tape still wrapped around his throwing fingers, eyes bright with adrenaline.

*“Honestly, Tanya, it’s all about staying locked in,”* he said into her microphone, the clubhouse behind him vibrating with bass. *“The vibe in here? Electric.”*

Yet when the champagne dried and the buses emptied, Ellis did not join the high-end nightclub circuit in Jack London Square. He drove back to Laurel alone. When he got home in the small hours of Y3C6, his body was still vibrating from the final out. He paced the narrow hallway of his rental until four in the morning, unable to sit down, catching himself quietly narrating the sequence of the ninth inning out loud, as if explaining the pitches to someone who was no longer there to listen.

### II. Sidewalk Friction

That interior solitude has made Ellis an attentive observer of the neighborhood he chose. Unlike the new-money showcase of the Baylight District or the inherited wealth encircling Lake Merritt, Laurel remains what it has always been: the family belt that stayed reachable. It is where bus drivers, line mechanics, nurses from the medical corridor, and small retail owners live side-by-side.

It is also where municipal neglect is measured in unpaved asphalt and cracked sidewalks. Back in Y2C50, Ellis vented publicly about the dangerous pavement near the Laurel Recreation Center, where youth clinics were held under a leaking dugout roof while out-of-town commuters swallowed parking stalls.

That unresolved grievance flared into open hostility in Y3C9. Outside a municipal administrative building Downtown, Ellis crossed paths with Gordon Tate, the 51-year-old Superior Court Liaison. What began as a routine exchange about street maintenance and courthouse logistics quickly turned sharp. Ellis pressed Tate on why capital spending flows effortlessly toward waterfront stadium amenities while neighborhood rec centers can't get asphalt patches. Tate, hardened by two decades of municipal budgeting and shifting court logistics, countered with the realities of citywide resource allocations and jurisdictional boundaries. 

The argument spilled across the sidewalk and did not settle. Tate walked away irritated by what he viewed as an oversimplification from a professional athlete; Ellis walked back toward the transit line with his jaw set, unwilling to yield the point.

"People downtown think if you don't wear a tie, you don't understand how budgets work," Ellis reflects, the edge in his voice sharpening. "I grew up in New York. I know what money smells like, and I know what excuses smell like. When kids are running drills on a field where the dugout roof is dropping rotted plywood every time it rains, that's not a jurisdictional dilemma. That's just people looking the other way."

### III. The Wall on 42nd

The true counterweight to that frustration arrived not from city hall, but from the pavement of Laurel itself. 

In Y3C10, on a brisk First Friday morning, Ellis walked up to the Laurel Recreation Center with a gray sweatshirt pulled over his broad shoulders and a pair of stained canvas work gloves in his back pocket. Overnight, a long string of graffiti had appeared across the cinderblock perimeter wall facing the basketball courts. 

Waiting on the asphalt with five-gallon buckets of municipal gray primer was Rosalind Young.

Young, forty-six, is the owner of the MacArthur Boulevard smoke shop three blocks away. A mother of three who has spent decades navigating the shifting economic currents of East Oakland, Young knows every shopkeeper and delivery driver on the avenue. She had previously painted over tagging on the wall during Y3C6, working alongside neighbors while the rest of the city slept. 

On this morning, Ellis took a long-handled roller from her truck bed, poured primer into a plastic tray, and started rolling paint alongside her and four other residents. 

"She didn't ask me about my cutter," Ellis says, a rare smile breaking across his features. "She asked if I knew that honeybees can recognize human faces. Learned that from her daughter’s science textbook. I spent two hours rolling gray latex over spray paint, talking to Rosalind about bee navigation and the wholesale price of tobacco tax stamps. By the time we finished, she wasn't some shopkeeper I nod to through a glass window. She was someone I’d call if my pipes burst."

That morning sealed a genuine bond between them. For Young, Ellis was neither a fantasy baseball commodity nor an athletic hero; he was simply a hardworking kid on 39th Avenue who knew how to keep paint off his boots. 

Down the block, Evan Lewis, fifty-five, steered his AC Transit bus past the rec center on his regular route, his air brakes hissing as he slowed to watch the work crew. Lewis, who drives past the Oakland Coliseum and the industrial flats of West Oakland every shift, nodded as he caught sight of the pitcher working the roller. In Laurel, recognition is silent and mutual.

### IV. The Arithmetic of Staying Put

The pressures of the business continue to mount. In Y3C10, as the Athletics sit in the off-season while the Oaks begin their first NBA campaign, Ellis’s name surfaced once more in the Bay Tribune sports pages. General Manager Mike Paulson is currently evaluating long-term contract extensions for the young championship core—Travis Coles, Vladimir Gonzalez, Ernesto Quintero, and John Ellis.

Ellis has seen the numbers. Back in Y3C5, during a quiet lunch break between winter throwing sessions, he scrolled through residential listings in Rockridge and was struck by the realization that on his current trajectory, he could comfortably buy a three-bedroom estate near College Avenue. 

Yet he stayed in Laurel.

When the noise of the front office and the glare of national media become suffocating, Ellis takes the bus down to Lake Merritt. In Y3C8, dodging the marathon training groups that loop the water, he found a quiet bench near the east-shore pergola. Sitting beneath the stone arches with a paper cup of black coffee, he watched the water lap against the concrete retaining wall, letting the tension drain out of his shoulders.

"In this league, everybody wants to tell you who you are before you’ve pitched five years," Ellis says, rising from the folding chair on his porch. "They call you an innings-eater. Or they call you a number-three starter. Or they write a column saying you're living in the shadow of Dillon and Ramos."

He tucks the baseball into his jacket pocket. Later this afternoon, he will run three miles along the ridge, complete ninety minutes of rotator-cuff band work, and throw a thirty-pitch flat-ground session into the net behind the garage. 

"Deacon Seymour told me my first spring under him that the only stat that matters on your headstone is whether the team won the day you had the ball," Ellis adds. "Out here in Laurel, nobody cares about your WHIP. They care if you painted the wall, if you waved at the bus driver, and if you sweep your own sidewalk. That's a good way to live. The rest is just sixty feet, six inches."
