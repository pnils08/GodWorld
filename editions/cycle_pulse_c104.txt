THE CYCLE PULSE — Y2C104
The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

There are mornings when you sit with the week's clips spread across your desk and you see, not a series of disconnected stories, but the shape of a city trying to make sense of itself. This was one of those weeks.

Start where the city started: with a number that defies easy absorption. Anthony Raines, whose sports copy usually lands with the clean thud of settled fact, turned in something different this cycle — a meditation on what zero means when a twenty-three-year-old rookie throws nine innings of hitless baseball for a franchise already winning at a pace that strains the imagination. Nine innings, no hits, one walk, ten strikeouts. Pablo Almanzar's debut didn't just make the box score; it argued with it. Raines framed the problem precisely: when a team has already won a hundred and twenty-four games against thirty-four losses, individual heroics risk being "absorbed into the machinery." Almanzar's line refused absorption. That's the right sentence, and it's the right question — what happens to a singular moment inside a collective one?

P Slayer caught the answer from the bar stools, which is often where the honest answer lives. Vinnie Keane, sitting in the Baylight District watching his own highlights, said that watching Almanzar was like watching the whole crowd feed off one person's energy. Ernesto Quintero put it more plainly: the belief the crowd carries for Almanzar says something about how much they've decided to trust him. That's not nothing. Trust, in this city, tends to be rationed carefully. And then Tanya Cruz came back two games later with the update that matters — Keane still delivering, the A's now at a hundred and twenty-six wins, the veteran doing the quiet work that box scores don't record. The gap between what Keane does on paper and what he does in the clubhouse is the same gap this city keeps navigating: the difference between what's measurable and what's real.

That gap showed up on Piedmont Avenue, too, though in a quieter register. Noah Tan followed a thread about Sandra Velazquez, whose morning runs are apparently enough of a neighborhood fixture that when her stride slowed, people noticed and began wondering aloud. Ariana Lee put it directly: Sandra doesn't get seasonal sniffles and power through — so what changed? Velazquez herself turned the question back on us, suggesting we ask whether what people are noticing is based on actual doctor visits or neighborhood chatter. That's a fair challenge, and the honest answer is we don't know yet. We're watching a community take inventory of one of its own, which is either attentiveness or rumor, and the line between them is exactly what Tan was pulling on. I'd like him to keep pulling.

Sharon Okafor spent the week at a corner most of us drive past without looking. Adams Point United Methodist — red doors, no banners, no flyers — became briefly significant when Mark Aitken was spotted there. What's remarkable about Okafor's piece isn't the sighting but the texture of the reaction: a café worker pausing mid-wipe, a taxi driver who noticed nothing out of the ordinary, a nurse aide who filed it under neighborhood chatter. Nobody knew what to make of it because nobody had the context to make anything of it. Okafor's closing observation stays with me — maybe the question isn't why he was there, but why we're all so surprised when someone steps out of frame. That's a good question for a neighborhood column. It's also a good question for a city that's currently watching a rookie pitcher, a slowing runner, and an unassuming church corner and asking, in each case, what does this mean.

Kai Marston found the Neon Harbor Club full for the Countdown Live New Year's Eve Special, which in a cycle this dense feels almost like counterpoint — the city gathering to watch itself be narrated back to it. Depak Ying, Melton Neilon, Rick Walker, Elio Perez, all from different neighborhoods, all landing on roughly the same sentiment: TV brings us together because it talks about what's going on. There's something both comforting and slightly circular about that, and Marston was right to end wondering what comes next rather than declaring community found.

What Nia Rook found downtown is worth threading back through everything else. Merkin Jumper, a social services caseworker, performed on UNDOCKED this cycle in a way that apparently sent ripples through the neighborhood — not because of celebrity but because of recognition. Dillon Trevor noticed business picking up at his establishment. Andre Turner felt it in the kitchen. Jessie Hess said it brought new energy and she was curious to see how it played out. The language is ordinary, but the pattern isn't: a man whose actual job is holding people's crises together gets treated, briefly, as the thing that holds a neighborhood together. That's the city doing something it does well when it bothers — collapsing the distance between the person serving and the person celebrated.

The thread I'm left holding: this cycle gave us a debut that may not repeat, a momentum that may not hold, a woman whose pace changed for reasons we haven't confirmed, a church visit nobody can explain, and a caseworker who became, for one week, downtown's pulse. What none of these stories answered is whether any of it compounds — whether Almanzar's zero and Keane's steady production and the crowd's borrowed belief add up to something that survives October; whether the noticing happening on Piedmont Ave is early warning or noise; whether the church door that opened for Aitken opens again and whether anyone asks why.

Next week I'm watching the A's rotation decisions with Almanzar now in the picture, and I'm asking Tan to go back to Piedmont and confirm what he's actually tracking. And I want someone to knock on those red doors at Adams Point and ask a question. Not about Aitken. About what the church has been doing quietly while the rest of us weren't looking.

By Sharon Okafor | Bay Tribune Culture

# The Quiet Sighting at Adams Point United Methodist

Adams Point United Methodist Church sits at its usual corner, the kind of place you pass a hundred times without noticing—until someone like Mark Aitken gets spotted there. Devon Willis wiped down the counter at a café two blocks away when the rumor reached him. "I've heard that Mark Aitken was spotted at the Adams Point United Methodist Church," he said, pausing mid-swipe, "but I don't really know what to make of it."

That’s the thing about these sightings—they ripple without explanation. Taxi driver Ezekiel Campbell shrugged it off between fares: "I've been driving my taxi around this neighborhood for a while, and I haven't noticed anything out of the ordinary." Mart Johns, a nurse aide, echoed the same detached curiosity: "It's just something I've heard around the neighborhood, that's all." 

The church itself gave no clues—no banners for a charity drive, no flyers for a speaking event. Just the same red doors, the same quiet hum of a place accustomed to being overlooked. So what draws a man like Aitken to a spot like this? Not spectacle, clearly. Not convenience either—this isn’t on the way to anywhere notable. Maybe that’s the point. Or maybe the real question isn’t why he was there, but why we’re all so surprised when someone steps out of frame.

By P Slayer | Bay Tribune Sports

I'm sitting at the bar in the Baylight District, watching the highlights of last night's game, and I'm still buzzing from the energy of the crowd. Vinnie Keane, the Designated Hitter for the Oakland A's, is sitting next to me, and I ask him about Pablo Almanzar's performance. "I've seen Pablo Almanzar's performance get the crowd really pumped up, it's like they're feeding off his energy and it's amazing to be a part of it," he says, taking a sip of his drink.

As we watch the replay of Almanzar's 9IP, 0H, 1BB, 10Ks performance, I can feel the excitement building up again. The crowd's reaction is infectious, and I find myself cheering along with the rest of the fans. Ernesto Quintero, another player, joins us at the bar, and I ask him about the team's momentum. "I've seen the crowd go wild when Pablo Almanzar's on the mound, it's like they're all energized by his performance. As a player myself, I can feel the excitement and the pressure to perform, and I think the crowd's reaction says a lot about how much they believe in him," he says, nodding in agreement with Vinnie.

Melton Neilon, a fan sitting across from us, chimes in, "I feel like Pablo Almanzar's performance is electric, it's like the whole city comes alive when he's on, and the crowd's reaction is just infectious - you can't help but get caught up in the excitement." The atmosphere in the bar is electric, and I can feel the sense of community and camaraderie among the fans.

As I look around the bar, I see a mix of fans, all united by their love for the team. Some are wearing A's jerseys, while others are sporting caps and hoodies. The walls are decorated with memorabilia, and the air is filled with the smell of nachos and hot dogs. It's a place where strangers become friends, all cheering for the same team.

But as the night wears on, and the excitement dies down, I'm left wondering - can the team sustain this momentum? Will they be able to keep the energy going, or will it fizzle out like a sparkler on a cold winter night? Only time will tell, but for now, I'm just going to enjoy the ride.

By Noah Tan | Bay Tribune Civic

Sandra Velazquez stood at the corner of Piedmont Ave, her hands buried deep in her pockets against the chill. She’d just finished her morning run, but something felt off. "I don’t usually have seasonal health concerns—I’m pretty careful about staying active and eating right year-round," she said, her breath visible in the crisp air. Sandra paused, glancing toward the Tribune’s office a few blocks away. "Maybe the Tribune should ask if this is based on actual doctor visits or just general neighborhood chatter."

Ariana Lee, passing by with a bag of groceries, noticed Sandra’s usual brisk stride had slowed. "I’ve lived here long enough to know Sandra Velazquez doesn’t just get seasonal sniffles—she’s the type to power through a flu without missing a beat," Ariana said. She adjusted the bag on her shoulder and added, "The Tribune should ask what changed this year that made it noticeable enough to mention."

The stretch of Piedmont Ave, usually bustling with Sandra’s energy, felt quieter this cycle. The usual rhythm of her footsteps, a constant presence along the sidewalk, had softened. Neighbors who’d grown accustomed to her reliability wondered what had shifted in the air—or in Sandra herself.

By Kai Marston | Bay Tribune Culture

## Countdown Live and the Pulse of the Community
As I stepped into the lively atmosphere of the Neon Harbor Club, I was greeted by the sound of chatter and the glow of screens displaying the TV slate for the night. The Countdown Live New Year's Eve Special was about to start, and the excitement was palpable. I spotted Depak Ying, a server from Rockridge, taking a break to watch the show with his colleagues. "I feel like the TV slate has a big impact on our community, it's always something we talk about at work and I think it brings us together, I'm curious to see what's on tonight," he said, his eyes fixed on the screen.

The club was filled with people from all walks of life, united by their interest in the TV slate. I noticed Melton Neilon, a sourdough baker from Temescal, chatting with a group of friends about the upcoming shows. "I feel like the TV slate has a big impact on our community, it's always talking about what's going on in the city and I think that helps bring people together, I've noticed it when I'm working at the bakery and people are talking about the same shows and news," he said, smiling as he sipped his drink.

As the night went on, the energy in the club only grew. Rick Walker, a line cook from Fruitvale, was watching the show with a group of friends, all of them cheering and laughing together. "I feel like the TV slate has a big impact on our community, it's always talking about what's going on in the city and it brings us together, I mean I've seen people gathered around the TV at home or at the local spots watching the same shows," he said, grinning from ear to ear.

Elio Perez, a server from West Oakland, was also enjoying the show, and he shared his thoughts on the TV slate's impact on the community. "I feel like the TV slate has a big impact on our community, it's something people talk about and look forward to, especially when there's a special event like New Year's Eve," he said, nodded in agreement by his friends.

As the Countdown Live New Year's Eve Special came to a close, the crowd at the Neon Harbor Club erupted in cheers and applause. It was clear that the TV slate had brought the community together, and the excitement would linger long after the show was over. But as I left the club, I couldn't help but wonder: what does the future hold for this community, and how will the TV slate continue to shape its pulse?

By Anthony Raines | Bay Tribune Sports

The first thing that jumps out when you look at Pablo Almanzar's debut line is the arithmetic of zero: nine innings pitched, zero hits allowed, one walk, and ten strikeouts. That is a complete game by any measure, and for a twenty-three-year-old rookie stepping onto a mound that had never before held his spikes, it is also statistical violence.

Nine innings constitutes the most complete possible debut sample—there are no partial frames to question, no inherited runners to complicate the earned-run math. The one walk is the lone blemish, a testament to command that never collapsed under the weight of history in progress. Ten strikeouts are not incidental contact; they are a repertoire announcing itself. 

Vinnie Keane's night—two hits in three at-bats including a home run and three runs batted in—would be the lead in most game recaps. On most nights, a designated hitter driving in three runs while the club extends its winning streak to fifteen games would command the story. But Keane's production exists here as footnote and foil, the necessary offensive complement to Almanzar's silencing of bats. The A's have now won one hundred and twenty-four games against thirty-four losses, a pace so dominant that individual heroics risk blending into the machinery. Almanzar's line refuses to be absorbed.

The scouting question is whether this is architecture or variance. Nine innings tell us nothing about his slider's spin efficiency or his four-seam fastball's perceived velocity down in the zone. What they do tell us is this: no major-league hitter managed to square him up. Not once. A single walk suggests his mechanics held under strain, and ten strikeouts confirm he missed bats when he needed to. The board scans as dominance, whether sustainable or not.

What remains is the sample problem. One game cannot reveal whether Almanzar's command will hold across thirty starts or whether his put-away pitch plays as well the third time through a lineup. But one game can—and in this case did—rewrite the boundaries of what a debut looks like. The zeros speak for themselves.
