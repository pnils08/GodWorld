I sat on a stool at The Last Out, the one off Jack London where the rail lights still flicker after the last BART train, and watched the condensation slide down my glass like it was trying to escape the room. Game 5 was over. We had taken the series lead. The city should have been loud. Instead the place felt like it had been holding its breath for nine innings and forgot how to let it out.

John Ellis took the loss in Game 4. He said it out loud later, the way a man does when the words have already been living in his chest for days. “The emotional toll of the game is real, I can feel it myself after a tough loss. It’s not just about winning or losing, it’s about the pressure to perform and the weight of expectation from the fans. Sometimes it feels like we’re carrying the whole city on our shoulders.”

Pablo Almanzar came in the next night and threw nine innings of one-hit ball like the moment had been waiting for him since he was a kid in the neighborhood. He did not sound like a man who had just saved a series. He sounded like someone who had watched the same weight settle on everyone else. “I think the emotional toll of the game can be really high, not just for the players but for the fans too. I’ve seen how much it means to people around here, like when I’m out in the neighborhood and everyone’s talking about the game. It’s like the whole city is invested in it.”

Some fans will tell you this is what we pay them for. That the money and the cheers even out the nights when the shoulders give. They are wrong. The ledger does not cover what Ellis carried into Game 4 and what the rest of them still carry when the lights come up again. Eric Taveras went 3-for-7 with two home runs across the two games and still sounded like a man who had left pieces of himself on the field. “I feel like the game can be really tough on us players, both physically and mentally. The pressure to perform is always there, and when we don’t do well, it can be hard to shake off the feeling of disappointment. I think the fans feel it too, they’re so invested in the team and when we lose, it’s like they’re feeling the loss just as much as we are.”

Jessie Hess was at the bar with me after the final out. She did not raise her glass. She just said, “I feel like the game can be really tough on the players and the fans, it’s like we’re all in it together and when they lose, we feel it too.”

We asked Ellis to be the one who could hold the city when the rest of the rotation could not. We asked Almanzar to step into the same uniform and make it look easy on the very next night. We keep asking. The 4-1 record and the streak do not change the cost. It only changes the scoreboard.

The question the city has not answered is how many more shoulders we are willing to load before we admit the price is paid in places the standings never show.
