# The Seven-Inning Question

The first thing that jumps out when you look at Pablo Almanzar's line in Game 3 is the simplicity of it: seven innings pitched, five hits allowed, two earned runs, seven strikeouts. No walks recorded. That is the kind of production that forces a conversation about whether a pitcher is solving the game or getting lucky against a specific opponent on a specific night.

Jose Colon caught most of those seven innings, and he was direct about what he saw. "Man, Pablo was locked in," Colon said after the game. "Seven strong innings, just two runs? That's the kind of performance that wins you games in October. I caught him a few times this season, and when he's on like that, it's not luck—it's him hitting his spots and trusting his stuff. The way he mixed his pitches kept their hitters guessing all night. That's not variance, that's just good pitching."

The architecture Colon described—the pitch sequencing, the command, the trust between pitcher and catcher—lives inside that seven-inning frame. Two runs over seven innings means an ERA of 2.57 for the outing. The strikeout-to-walk ratio of undefined (seven strikeouts, zero walks) is a clean statement: Almanzar found the zone when it mattered and made opponents chase when they shouldn't have. A pitcher who can thread that needle in October baseball is not a commodity.

Almanzar himself framed it as a team effort. "I felt like I executed my pitches well and trusted my defense," he said. "It's always a team effort, and I'm just glad I could contribute to the win." That modesty is familiar from the clubhouse—but the numbers don't soften. Seven innings at two earned runs in a playoff game is architecture, not variance. The question that remains is whether Almanzar can repeat that production when the stakes climb higher, when opposing hitters make their own adjustments, when the sample grows beyond a single outing in October.

On the offensive side, the A's got production from multiple bats. Ernesto Quintero and Jose Colon each drove in a run; Darrin Davis went four-for-four at the plate. That kind of depth—multiple contributors, no reliance on a single hero—is the other half of the equation that wins playoff games. But Almanzar's seven innings were the foundation. The question now is whether that foundation holds.
