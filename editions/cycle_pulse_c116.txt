The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

Mags Corliss here, with the week in Oakland.

Start with the door. In Rockridge, Benji Dillon stood in the cereal aisle at Canopy Market with a basket on his forearm while a woman two carts down looked, looked away, and looked again. As Kai Marston reported, Dillon nodded the way a man nods at a neighbor. "I'm still just Benji, living on my block, and going to A's games," he told Marston. Across town, Tanya Cruz found the same man as a question nobody will force. Mike Paulson says he won't push a five-time Cy Young winner into a decision because the calendar wants one. Deacon Seymour says the club is planning as if Benji isn't coming back and leaving the light on anyway. Vinnie Keane, who walked through his own door first in C115, put it in the hallway: Benji has the clinic, Rick, Maya, and a city that remembers three no-hitters. So the man says it isn't a big change, and the people around him are building a roster around a gap shaped like him. I don't think either is lying. A man can be ordinary in a grocery aisle and still be the biggest open question in the building.

That building is emptying out in its own way. Keane is gone, and Mark Aitken is gone to Colorado. On the Lives Page, Tamsin Pruitt caught Edmund Alegero, a construction laborer who just got a place in Lake Merritt with only his name on the lease, saying it's tough to see Aitken leave because he's from around here and always involved in the community. P Slayer had Carmen Mesa, standing in the neighborhood where the news landed, describing folks still reeling from the same departure. That is the same man seen from two desks, and neither is writing about a stat line. Aitken's leaving registers in a laborer's new kitchen and in a pitcher's neighborhood, not on a transaction wire. When I say a ballplayer is a neighbor, this is what I mean.

Mesa himself became the week's argument. The A's sent him, with Porter Broadway and Jose Fuentes, to the Phillies for Giancarlo Hernandez and Antone Baez. Paulson calls it maximizing return on a player who didn't fit the rotation. Anthony Raines mostly backs the pitching side. The rotation had no open slot, Mesa had been optioned once Henry Rivas returned, and his strikeout-to-walk rates are the kind of stable signal a front office should trust, which is exactly why he sold well. Raines finds the infield half harder to defend. Hernandez makes three A-potential third basemen, and Ernesto Quintero had just taken the job. Slayer calls it inventory management and refuses to applaud. Kevin Clark, glove still on in the tunnel, said it's a competition and he's ready for it, and Paulson says the same word, battle. Quintero, who also turned up in Marston's culture reporting this week, is the one carrying it. The city is getting to know him as a face off the field just as the club hands his position to a contest. What the trade means for him is simple. Everything he was just handed is now being re-auditioned.

Now the contradiction I can't smooth over, from Carmen Delaine. Marcus Webb says for the West Oakland Stabilization Fund that the closeout is published and the roll call belongs to the Council. The Initiative_Tracker's own C116 note says final action requires a verified C114 closeout, business-and-resident scope, and the 23-day standard, and it schedules the verification for C117. Brenda Okoro wants the record on the table before the vote. Webb wants the proof to be in the hearing record. Nobody has said who checks the closeout before members raise their hands. Delaine's tranche arithmetic also leaves an $800,000 gap between what the milestone notes itemize and what the budget fields imply, and I haven't seen anyone explain it. This matters because Pruitt's Lives Page shows Wanjiru Liu, an electrician newly in a Rockridge rental, helping a neighbor through that same portal because the interface was confusing. The fund is a promise to real people who are navigating it by hand.

The people who hold the city up don't always show on the books. Dr. Lila Mezran counted Chinatown's six beds and found six "other residents" with no names, in the neighborhood with the highest sick count of the city's 22. The only names in the hospital ledger this cycle were Yu Zen, Maurice Franklin, and Deacon Seymour. Seymour is the same man who sat on the edge of his desk for Cruz and said you leave the light on. Zen is the commercial insurance broker who told Mezran about the weekend he kept working and the wife who held the house and the colleagues who carried his accounts. He said that in the bed you think about the people who carried you there, and the ones who carried you before you fell. Pruitt's page carries him too, and I'd read the two together. Seymour keeping a light on for a pitcher and Zen remembering who kept his house standing are the same impulse seen from different rooms. Being carried is how this city works, and Chinatown's six beds are people no one has yet named well enough to carry.

Ines Sorensen's fence belongs here as well. Tre Mingo, who works the port in Uptown, says the numbers look good and the folks on the ledger aren't the ones who get to stay. Guadalupe Lupe, bartending in Jack London, hears rent worry from the regulars, and Mingo's name came up. Omar Amici, in construction, finds the streets busier and every other place for rent. Mingo asks whether any of us who work here can still stay. I told Sorensen I don't have an answer, and I still don't. I'll say only that Alegero's own place in Lake Merritt, Liu's in Rockridge, and the youth apprenticeship flyer stapled over an older notice in Marston's piece, promising spots without saying how many or who decides, all sit on the same fence.

For the lighter side, Nia Rook's UNDOCKED digest says Luis Taz still leads the board for a third cycle. Marguerite Kimball-Ostrow and Manju Abebe flew until time ran out, and Soo Loo closed a contract. Drago Helio staked 460 and was paid 842. It's a reminder that a city can play for points and not only for rent.

What I'm watching next week, C117: whether the Council verifies the C114 closeout before it votes on charter renewal, and who says so out loud. Whether Benji Dillon walks through that open door, in either direction. Whether Hernandez, Clark, and Quintero turn a crowded third base into a fight or a fracture. And whether anyone puts a name beside one of Chinatown's six beds.

By Anthony Raines | Bay Tribune Sports

# The A's Sold a Surplus Starter and Bought Into a Surplus Infield

Giancarlo Hernandez arrives in Oakland with a 96 fielding grade, an 80 reaction grade and a 70 arm, set against 65 contact against right-handers and 68 against left-handers. The feed grades him 78 overall with A potential. That is a glove-first third baseman at a position where the A's already held two other A-potential players. The case for the trade has to start there, because the pitching side of the ledger is easier to defend than the infield side.

**What left**

Carmen Mesa goes to the Phillies, the club the A's beat in five games for their seventh World Series title. The feed grades him 81 overall with B potential, and he is 23. Porter Broadway, a 20-year-old relief prospect graded 70 overall with C potential, goes with him. So does second baseman Jose Fuentes, 23, graded 63 overall with B potential. Oakland gets back Hernandez and left fielder Antone Baez, 24, graded 70 overall with C potential.

Mesa's TrueSource card shows one season in Oakland: 10 starts, 5-3, 56.0 innings, 44 hits, 8 home runs, 11 walks, 67 strikeouts, a 3.21 ERA. The rates worked from that line are 10.8 strikeouts per nine, 1.8 walks per nine and 6.09 strikeouts per walk. Stable signals like strikeout and walk rates are what a front office should weigh in a 56-inning sample, and his are very good. The ERA and the 5-3 record carry more noise.

His first start, the nine-inning, five-hit, 12-strikeout, no-run game against the Cubs, is the feed's line on him. It is one game, and the paper's C93 coverage already told that story. It does not move the board much now. The more useful fact is the rotation the feed lists ahead of him: Coles, Ramos, Rivas and Ellis, with Pablo Almanzar holding the fifth spot after a late-season call-up. Mesa was optioned to AAA once Henry Rivas returned. The feed puts his pay at $780K.

On those facts the pitching half of the deal sells depth from a position with no open slot. It also took the A's budget room from $129.42M to $130.35M, per the feed. The Phillies players carry no listed salary on the feed, so the paper cannot say what each side's payroll actually did. The record supports the room figure and nothing finer.

**What came back**

The infield half is harder to defend. The feed's own framing is that Hernandez makes three A-potential third basemen. Ernesto Quintero took the job from Martin Richards. Kevin Clark, 22, was supposed to be next.

Clark's TrueSource card shows 18 games and 64 at-bats in 2041: 19 hits, 3 home runs, 12 RBI, 6 steals, .297/.313/.547. That works out to one home run and one strikeout every 21.3 at-bats. The feed's player note carries a slightly different version of the same sample, 55 at-bats and a .327 average. The two lines disagree, and either is too small to settle anything. The feed puts Clark at 79 overall with A potential, 76 contact against right-handers and 72 against left-handers, 83 discipline and 87 clutch, against Hernandez's 65 and 68 contact and 62 discipline.

By the feed's numbers, Clark is the better hitter and Hernandez the better glove. The feed gives Clark's fielding at 62, his reaction at 62 and his arm at 55. Hernandez has 96, 80 and 70.

**Where the fit breaks**

The feed lists Clark with 2B and RF as secondary positions and Hernandez with none. Eric Taveras owns second base, and right field already has Vladimir Gonzalez and Frank Reyna. Quintero's path is first base if Mark Aitken leaves in free agency, which is a contingency and not a vacancy. Baez, per the feed, starts in the minors behind Desmond Morton, Frank Reyna and JR Rosado, so the outfield return is organizational depth only.

A single trade that fills nothing at its main position cannot be called a hole-closer. At best it converts a pitching surplus into an infield surplus, and the one thing the roster lacked, a second regular-quality defender at third base, is not something the record shows it lacked. Hernandez's contact grades read below Clark's. A glove at 96 can justify a lineup spot only if the bat does not drag it down, and the feed describes his bat as "behind the glove."

Clark's three remaining minor league options, his $780K for 2042 and his team control through 2047, per the feed, give the A's a cheap way to resolve the logjam. That is a real strength, and it is the board's own answer to the redundancy: Las Vegas is the pressure valve. It also means the trade's value depends on one decision the record does not yet show.

**The claim**

Selling Mesa from a full rotation is defensible on process, given his strikeout and walk rates and the five starters the feed lists ahead of him. The return is not a fit. It leaves one starting job at third base and three men graded A potential competing for it, with a defensive profile that only pays if the bat grows. The feed describes his attributes as improving, including +9 contact against left-handers. That is one cycle of movement, not a trend.

The open question for Deacon Seymour and Mike Paulson, the feed's own list, is who starts at third on opening day, and whether the club prices the 96 glove above the bat that is already there. Until that is answered, the trade has produced a surplus, not a solution.

By P Slayer | Bay Tribune Sports

I sat on the low wall outside the Lake Merritt station, phone still hot in my hand from the notification, the evening crowd pushing past like the trade never happened. Carmen Mesa’s name was already gone from the board. Paulson cashed him in the same week the kid threw nine shutout innings and twelve strikeouts. That’s the move. That’s the one that lands in the gut before the numbers do.

I found Paulson later near the batting cages, still in the windbreaker, talking like the ledger was already balanced. “The trade was about maximizing our return on Carmen Mesa, a player with high potential who didn't quite fit into our rotation. We got Giancarlo Hernandez, a third baseman with an A-potential glove, and Antone Baez, a left fielder with C potential. It's a good haul. Now, we've got a battle brewing at third base between Hernandez, Kevin Clark, and Ernesto Quintero. I'm excited to see how these guys compete and push each other to be better.”

Some fans will call that patience. They’re wrong. Patience is what you sell when you already decided the kid who just proved he belonged here is worth more on someone else’s roster. We watched Mesa come up from Midland, watched him shut the Cubs down, and the front office answered by shipping the arm out the same door the city was still cheering through. That’s not roster building. That’s inventory management.

Kevin Clark was in the dugout tunnel when I caught him, glove still on, staring at the dirt like it owed him an answer. “I'm not gonna lie, it's a little weird having Giancarlo Hernandez on the team now. I mean, we're talking about a guy with an A-potential glove at third base, which is my position. It's a competition, and I'm ready for it. I've been around long enough to know that the front office is looking for the best fit, and if that means Hernandez gets more playing time, so be it. I'm just here to help the team win.”

Clark’s already lived the thin sample—eighteen games, one season on the books—and now he gets to fight for his own position against the new A-potential glove Paulson brought in to replace the arm we just lost. That’s the competition they want us to applaud. I don’t.

Carmen Mesa said it plain when the deal broke, standing in the neighborhood where the news landed first. “I've seen a mix of emotions around the neighborhood since the trade. Some folks are still reeling from Mark Aitken leaving for Colorado, and now with Giancarlo Hernandez coming in, people are wondering what that means for Kevin Clark's chances. The A's infield is definitely a topic of conversation. I've heard some people saying it's a good move, while others are concerned about the impact on the team's local identity.”

He’s right. The local identity is the part the front office keeps treating like a luxury tax. We keep the seats warm, we keep the noise, and every time a piece starts to feel like ours they turn it into a chip. Paulson can call it a haul. The stands know what it cost.

We’re supposed to feel the front office heard us. Instead we get another name on the way out and three third basemen fighting over the one spot that used to be Clark’s. The fury isn’t about the return. It’s about who decided the return mattered more than the arm that just earned it.

By Kai Marston | Bay Tribune Culture

# Benji Dillon Doesn't Think It's a Big Change, and That's the Story

Benji Dillon is standing in the cereal aisle at Canopy Market with a basket on his forearm, and a woman two carts down keeps glancing over, then glancing away, then glancing back. He doesn't make a show of it. He nods at her, the way a man nods at a neighbor, and goes back to reading the back of a box.

"I've noticed people recognizing me more often around Rockridge, like at the grocery store," he says, low enough that the carts knocking past us have to work to cover him. "It's become a part of my daily life, but it doesn't feel like a big change. I'm still just Benji, living on my block, and going to A's games."

The aisles here run long and narrow, and sound carries down them the way it does in a room with hard floors. A kid is negotiating with a parent about a box of cereal shaped like something from a cartoon. Somewhere near the produce, someone laughs at a joke that didn't need to be that funny. The light is the flat, even light of a store that wants you to stay. Nobody here is performing for a camera. The pitcher is just a man with a grocery list.

That ordinariness is the thing I keep turning over. The Cultural Ledger has Dillon at a fame score rising, the kind of trajectory that the paper's C105 coverage tracked when he was seen at Dimond Pharmacy and a server in Adams Point said his presence brought "a lot of attention" to the neighborhood. Tonight, in Rockridge, the attention looks less like a wave and more like a series of small, polite interruptions. A glance. A nod. A name said under someone's breath by the registers.

Down the block, on the pole outside the market, a flyer for the Oakland Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline has been stapled over an older notice, its corners already curling in the damp. I read it on my way out. It promises spots. It doesn't say how many, or who decides, and I found myself wondering who was actually going to get them, the same way I wonder who gets the last ticket to any show that sells out before the doors open.

The question I can't shake is about the other side of that rising line. Darrin Davis, who plays left field and has his own run of attention out of Piedmont Ave, told me he isn't close to Dillon but has watched his name climb around the ballpark. "I've seen his name popping up more often around the A's, and it's clear he's gaining traction," Davis said. "You can tell when someone's rising through the ranks." Davis knows what it looks like from inside the rise. Danny Horn, out of Temescal, was more guarded: "I've seen him around the clubhouse, he's a talented kid, but I don't know him well enough to say much about his personal story." Travis Coles, who has heard fans bring Dillon up to him, put it plainest: "It's like the whole city is buzzing about the A's right now, and Benji's part of that."

The ledger says thirty-five culture figures are rising this cycle, with five new on the record. Ernesto Quintero, out of the Baylight District, is one of the new names, and the ledger has him at a lower fame score than Dillon, still near the start of the climb. The paper's C94 coverage made the Dillon-Keane story about a one-year return for one last run, and Vinnie Keane has since slid into the where-are-they-now file, his fame cooling as the year moves on. Rising and fading are happening on the same ballclub, in the same city, in the same week.

Dillon finally pushes the cart toward the register. The woman two carts down is still watching, not quite sure she should say anything. He catches her eye, lifts a hand, and she laughs and lifts one back.

So here is what I'm left holding. The city is buzzing, as Coles says, and Dillon is steady enough to keep buying cereal through it. What I don't know is whether the buzz reaches the kid with the cartoon cereal, or the parent reading the flyer on the pole, or whoever is actually going to get those apprenticeship spots. Who answers for that gap, when a name climbs and the block stays exactly where it was?

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

# The Fund Says Its Closeout Is Published. The Tracker Says It Isn't Verified.

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic Ledger

Marcus Webb's statement for the West Oakland Stabilization Fund this cycle runs one sentence and settles one question for him. "The closeout is published, the ordinance is on the calendar with the scope that works and the 23-day clock still running — the hearing record carries the proof, and the roll call belongs to the Council."

The Initiative_Tracker's C116 milestone note settles it differently. It says the charter renewal was "kept on Council calendar" and that final action "requires verified C114 closeout, business-and-resident scope and the 23-day standard." The next scheduled action, set for C117, is to "Verify C114 closeout and conduct Council charter-renewal consideration." The Fund's program director calls the closeout published. The tracker still carries verification as work not yet done.

Brenda Okoro, the Deputy Mayor for Community Affairs, put the same three conditions in her own terms this cycle: "Renewal means West Oakland businesses and residents, 23 days on the clock, and a closeout record on the table before council votes." Her sentence puts the record before the vote. Webb's puts the proof in the hearing record and the roll call with the Council. Neither says who checks the closeout against the other two conditions before members vote.

## What $6.6 million shows

The tracker's milestone notes give a way to count who the Fund has reached. They record 47 checks, about $4.2 million, disbursed under Okoro's authority from C84 through C90. The paper's C84 coverage reported 342 applications, more than double what the city had projected. The notes then record $400,000 tranches approved or executed in each of C109 through C114. That is six tranches and $2.4 million. Added to the earlier $4.2 million, it comes to $6.6 million.

That total does not match the tracker's budget fields, which show $28 million authorized and $20.6 million remaining. Those fields imply $7.4 million out, and the tracker lists the last disbursement as C116. The $800,000 gap is the size of two more tranches, but the milestone notes do not itemize any disbursement in C115 or C116. I read the Fund's row and searched the paper's prior coverage for an itemized figure and found none. The two numbers reconcile only if one assumes tranches the notes do not name. The closeout, once verified, has to say which total is right.

The one tranche the notes break down is C109. It released $400,000 in West Oakland, and the note itemizes two household grants totaling $81,188. The note does not say where the other $318,812 went, whether to businesses, to more households, or to costs. Under the C109 scope correction, the Fund serves West Oakland businesses and residents. A tranche in which only two household grants are itemized does not show whether the business half of that scope has been reached at all.

## A scope that changed twice

The scope Council has made a condition of renewal was itself corrected on the record. The C108 note read the charter as stabilization grants for long-tenure West Oakland residents priced out as the flats built out, "not hood-wide relief." The C109 note then superseded it: the Fund "serves West Oakland businesses and residents," and a "Downtown canvass" line and a "residents only" reading were both set aside. The note adds that Downtown is outside the Fund's area. That is INIT-008, proposed and not voted.

So "the scope that works," in Webb's phrase, is the C109 reading. A closeout covering C114 should show grants under both halves of it. The C108 note counted 47 checks to residents. The C110 through C114 notes record only the $400,000 amounts and the 23-day standard, with no split between businesses and residents. The tracker's text does not show a business recipient anywhere.

## The clock and the roll call

The tracker records the 23-day figure as an achieved average in C110 and as a maintained standard in C111 and C112. The notes for C113 through C116 carry it forward as a condition. They do not say what the 23 days are measured from or to, or whether it is a ceiling or an average. The notes also do not say whether the closeout uses the same measure as the Council's C115 order. Webb says the clock is "still running," which fits a standard that has been kept but does not show how it is checked.

The Fund passed 6-3 in C78. The tracker's vote note names three members: Ramon Vega yes, Leonard Tran no, and Elliott Crane yes, remotely. It does not name the other six seats, and I will not fill them in. Whoever reads the closeout in C117 is voting to renew a fund whose original margin was three votes.

The Treasury closed C116 at $131.8 million, up from $126.6 million. The Fund's $20.6 million remaining is the figure that matters here. Whether it keeps moving in $400,000 steps depends on a charter the Council has not yet renewed.

The record cannot yet answer one question: who certifies the closeout, and against which definition of the 23 days? Webb says the proof is in the hearing record. The tracker says verification is still pending in C117. If those two are the same event, no one has said so on the record.

By Dr. Lila Mezran | Bay Tribune Civic

# Six Beds in Chinatown, and No Names Attached

Chinatown's line on the hospital census has barely moved in the cycles the paper has followed it. Three residents were admitted this cycle and three left. The neighborhood opened with six in care and closed with six in care, in six occupied beds. None of the six is tracked by name. The census records them as "other residents," which means the city counts them and has no record of who they are.

The neighborhood's sick count for this cycle is 132. That is three fewer than last cycle, and it is still the highest of the city's 22 neighborhoods, according to the neighborhood demographics sheet. The same sheet lists 396 seniors and 83 unemployed residents among Chinatown's adults. It does not link either figure to illness.

The ledger holds three names for the whole city's hospital record this cycle. None belongs to Chinatown. Yu Zen and Maurice Franklin are listed in Rockridge. Deacon Seymour is listed in Lake Merritt. Dr. Lila Mezran spoke with each of them about the numbers the record attaches to Chinatown and not to them.

Zen is a commercial insurance broker, and the ledger shows him admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. He started with the route his own chain took.

"The walk from Rockridge to the hospital is a straight shot down College Avenue, past the shops and the families out on the sidewalks, then over toward Broadway," Zen said. "It's a route I know well, but I wasn't walking it before I was admitted. The days before, I was working through another weekend, telling myself it was fine."

He went on to describe who had covered for him. "My wife held down the house, the kids, the meals, the school runs — she always does. At work, my team covered my accounts, my clients, the paperwork. I didn't have a shift to hand off; I just had to trust people. And I did. But I'll tell you, when you're in that bed, you think about the people who carried you there — and the ones who carried you before you even fell."

His account has two parts: how he reached the bed, and who held the household up while he was in it. The census can't show either part for a Chinatown bed. It has a count and no one behind it.

Franklin is a drone fleet coordinator. The ledger shows him admitted in cycle 106 with severe seasonal flu. He described the days before.

"The days before I went in, I kept telling myself it was just a bad cold," he said. "I'd wake up, cough, check my temperature, and head to work anyway — you do that, you know? You've got a job, people depending on you, and the card statement's already sitting there. So you push through. By the time I couldn't catch my breath walking from the car to the front door, I knew I'd waited too long."

He drew a line from his own delay to the neighborhood with the most illness. "But that's what it takes for a working person to get to a bed — you don't go until you can't stand, and then you hope there's a bed waiting. I was lucky. I got one. But I think about the folks in Chinatown, all those sick people, and how few of them show up on that hospital list. It's not that they're not sick. It's that they can't afford to stop. I know that feeling in my chest, and I know what it costs to ignore it."

Franklin speaks for himself here. The record does not say why Chinatown's 132 produce three admissions a cycle, and he does not claim it does. His reading is one resident's account of how waiting works, and the numbers cannot confirm or rule it out.

Seymour manages the Oakland A's. The ledger shows him admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. He said the numbers from Chinatown stay with him.

"I'll tell you what my chain looked like, because it's the only one I can speak to from the inside," Seymour said. "I got hurt at work, somebody called it in, the ambulance came, and I was admitted. Simple as that. I didn't have to think about whether the ambulance would come, or whether the hospital would take me, or whether I could afford the bed. I just had to lie there and let it happen."

He did not treat that as a credential. "My accident didn't make me tougher or smarter than anybody else. It made me lucky. And luck isn't a system. If I'd lived a few blocks over, if my job didn't carry the insurance it does, if I didn't have people who knew my name and could make a call — that chain looks different. It looks like waiting. It looks like hoping. It looks like a neighborhood carrying its sick the way Chinatown is carrying its sick, with no name on a ledger to show for it."

He stopped short of claiming to know more than he does. "I don't know who's in those six beds. But I know what it feels like to be in a bed and have people check on you. And I know what it would feel like to be in a bed and have nobody even know you're there."

## What the census can and cannot say

Other neighborhoods give a point of comparison, though not a cause. Adams Point has 80 sick residents, 2 admissions and 4 in care at the close. Dimond has 89 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Eastlake has 84 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. East Oakland has 114 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Chinatown has 132 sick, 3 admissions and 6 in care. Each of those neighborhoods also has no tracked residents in a bed.

Citywide, the hospital census shows 34 admissions this cycle, all of them other residents and none tracked. It shows 68 in care at the close in 68 beds, and 35 who left. The cycle summary puts the city illness rate at 4.0 percent. That is the summary's figure for the city as a whole, and it says nothing about who sits in Chinatown's six beds.

The paper's C112 and C113 coverage also found six Chinatown residents in care, untracked, and the C114 coverage asked who does the caring at home. The census row for cycle 115 shows the same six at the open and the close. The new fact this cycle is the turnover. Half of Chinatown's six beds changed hands, and the total did not move.

Piedmont Ave carries a recorded medium-severity health cluster this cycle. The record does not tie it to Chinatown. It is a reminder that the world summary reports health clusters in more than one place.

In clinic language, a steady census is "interesting." Interesting is doctor-speak for concerning. A steady number says the beds are in use and that people are being admitted and discharged at a matching pace. It does not say whether the people who leave are well, or whether the people who never arrive are sick at home. The sheets hold no row for that.

The question stays open, and it is about people the record cannot see. Who are the six in Chinatown's beds, and who is covering for them at home? And when 132 residents are counted as sick, who is responsible for knowing where the other 126 are?
