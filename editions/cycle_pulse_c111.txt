The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

The week came down to a gap between what gets counted and what gets felt, and I kept finding that gap in every desk's copy.

Start at the water. Jordan Velez reported that the Port's books grew, and then he went and asked the people doing the work. Elle Lilo, who captains a harbor tug, told him her day hasn't changed much and the bustle is just louder. Blair Patel, a ship repair foreman, called the growth a double-edged sword with a relentless pace. Dmitri Soto, who runs the container yard, said the job is bigger than stacking boxes now. My father worked the docks for thirty years, and I know what "busier" usually means. The shift gets heavier first, and the paycheck is a separate conversation. Jordan carried the question inland to the dining rooms and bar rails, where a port's growth is supposed to land. Guadalupe Lupe, Hector Leech and Trenton Nawan turn up in his business reporting and again in Mason Ortega's culture pages. Briar Soto turns up in Jordan's work too. I'll say what I can say honestly: the same people are being read as commerce in one section and as culture in another. Nobody in this city lives in a single desk. A bar rail is a balance sheet and a scene at once, and when the money lands unevenly, the scene feels it.

The same split shows up in transit. Trevor Shimizu reported that the 12th Street station ran better and emptied out at the same time. Service got more punctual while a huge number of riders simply weren't there. Joel Wren said it plainly: people used to pack in, and now it's quiet. Trevor was careful, and I'll be too. Baylight construction is a plausible reason, not a proven one. Calvin Mwangi, who drives the bus, hasn't heard his regulars say they're dodging the station. Other stations showed the same pattern, with trains on time and platforms thin. A system can improve the thing it measures and lose the thing it's for.

Jax Caldera found the mirror image in Fruitvale, a lunch line out the door at the taqueria while the neighborhood's own readings slide. Mood is still near the top of the city and retail still sits well above the middle, but both are dropping and housing pressure is climbing. Two programs list Fruitvale in their footprint, and neither owns the problem that's actually hurting it. Jax asked who answers for that, and no one with a title would say. I'd add one thing. Carmen Delaine reported on the West Oakland Stabilization Fund, where Marcus Webb says ninety-one households are stabilized and every file will survive an audit. Another $400,000 went out this week, and the processing standard held at twenty-three days. Carmen was honest that an average hides the slowest case, and the record doesn't show whether any family waited far longer. Councilmember Denise Carter said the Fund is being taken to where the need is moving. Fine. Jax is standing in a place where the need appears to be moving, and the Fund isn't listed there.

Then there's the part of the week that was just joy, with a cost attached. The A's won the title, and Anthony Raines laid out Game Five. John Ellis threw six innings with two earned runs and five strikeouts. Eric Taveras homered and drove in two. Darrin Davis had three hits and a homer, and Mark Aitken added one more. Anthony also reminded us that one night is one night. It was the seventh championship under Mike Paulson, and Paulson and Taveras both show up in Nia Rook's undocked reporting as well. I won't guess at what's in it beyond that. But it matters that the man on the parade bus and the man at the plate are also in a file about something else. A championship doesn't suspend the rest of a person's life. It sits on top of it.

Tanya Cruz took us onto that bus. Paulson, Vinnie Keane and Benji Dillon rode at the front, waving, and back in the clubhouse the confetti was still in their sleeves. Aitken said the quiet moments are where the weight sits, and that nobody knows what's next. Benji talked about the quiet after the noise, when you realize you don't have to do it again. Paulson said the part nobody sees is doing the math on who stays and who goes while the confetti is still in your hair. For Paulson, the manager, that math is the job, and it starts the day after the parade. The city got a party, and he got a roster problem. Both are true, and the city should remember the second one while it's celebrating the first.

Elliot Marbury added a quieter piece of good news, the kind that comes from patience. Martin Richards has struck out less in each of the last three seasons than in any season before them, and he hasn't given up power to do it. Elliot flagged that the best of those seasons came from the thinnest sample, and that the next two seasons are what actually settle the claim. That's the right way to hold a hot streak.

The Oaks are the other side of the sports page. Talia Finch sat in a Jack London bar with Melton Neilon, who plans his life around the games but doesn't have room to decode them between his starter and his bakery, and with Rick Walker, who is just riding the wave between grading papers and everything else. General manager Elliot Abraham has acknowledged the losing and offered reassurance, and Draymond's return is still an open question. The Oaks haven't lost the neighborhood. They just haven't won a place in people's busy lives yet.

Kai Marston captured the same distance from the culture side. Tara Ellison is listed as one of the city's fastest-rising journalists, and standing on a Rockridge sidewalk she said she isn't in a room with anyone. Alma Vasquez in Laurel and Nina Reyes in Fruitvale hadn't heard much about her. Being counted and being talked about on the block turned out to be different things.

Here is what nobody has answered. Who owns Fruitvale's slide? Where did the 12th Street riders go, and will the council vote force an answer? And do the 91 households, the Port's louder noise and the party downtown reach the same people, or different ones?

Next week I'm watching the month-eleven review for the Stabilization Fund, and whether the case-level times ever come out. I'm watching the council vote on Baylight and whether the platform fills back in. I'm watching Paulson's roster math, and Aitken's name in particular. I'm watching whether anyone claims Fruitvale. And I'm watching the Oaks as their season opens, to see whether anyone in that bar glances back at the screen.

# The Port Got Louder. Down the Block, the Ledger Barely Moved.

*By Jordan Velez, Business Reporter*

Elle Lilo still spends her shifts doing what she has always done at the Port of Oakland: bringing ships in. She is a harbor tugboat captain, one of 1,501 people on the Port's payroll this cycle. The Port's books grew by $765,000 since last cycle, to $425.1 million. On the water, Lilo says, the difference is mostly the volume of the noise.

"The Port's growth? It's been steady, but honestly, my day-to-day hasn't changed much," Lilo said. "I'm still out there guiding ships, same as always. The bustle's a bit louder, sure, but it's the kind of noise you get used to. What keeps me up at night is making sure we keep that balance—growth without losing the soul of this place."

That balance is the real Jack London story this cycle. It does not show up in the Port's top line. It shows up in how unevenly the money lands once it leaves the water.

Start at the repair end. Blair Patel, a ship repair foreman at the Port, describes the same growth as a workload that keeps climbing.

"The Port's expansion? Yeah, I've been watching it closely," Patel said. "More ships coming in means more work for us, but it's not all smooth sailing. The docks are busier than ever, and the pressure's on to keep up with repairs and maintenance. It's a double-edged sword—good for jobs, but the pace is relentless. And honestly, I'm not sure the infrastructure can handle it long-term without some serious upgrades."

Dmitri Soto, a container yard supervisor, describes it from the stacks. His account sounds less like a complaint than a description of a job getting bigger.

"The role's gotten busier, no doubt," Soto said. "More ships coming in means more containers to manage, more coordination with the teams on the ground. It's not just about stacking boxes anymore—it's about keeping the flow smooth, making sure everyone's on the same page. Feels like we're the backbone of the whole operation."

Busier yards, busier repair crews. My father worked the docks for thirty years, and I know what that sentence usually means. It means a heavier shift first. Whether a fatter paycheck follows is a separate question, and it doesn't always get answered.

Now walk inland from the water to the neighborhood's dining rooms and bar rails. That is where a port's growth is supposed to spill over: the crew grabbing a plate after a double, the warehouse worker stopping for a drink.

Green & Gold Tavern carries 30 workers, among them dishwasher Hector Leech, servers Trenton Nawan and Valentina Campbell, and bartender Guadalupe Lupe. The tavern's revenue rose $6,000 since last cycle, to $3.7 million. Blue Lantern Bar, where Yuki Ji and Guadalupe Lee pour, also employs 30 and added $7,000, to $3.9 million. Harborline Grill is a seven-person kitchen whose line includes Tomas Renteria, Quynh Le, Jalen Hill and Bruce Wright. It brought in $1,000 more than last cycle, for $860,000 total.

Put those figures next to the Port's and the shape is plain. The Port added three-quarters of a million dollars. The kitchen down the block added roughly what one line cook might take home in a couple of weeks.

Anchor Build sits between those extremes. The construction firm is led by managing partner Marguerite Salcedo and has 181 people on staff, including laborer Travis Golin and electrician Briar Soto. It added $241,000, to $146.6 million. Elena Leon, a longshoreman at the Port, has noticed what is going up around the docks.

"Elle? She's got that quiet strength about her—the kind that keeps the port running smooth even when the tides turn," Leon said. "I've seen her maneuver those tugs like they're extensions of her own body. As for Jack London... well, the money's moving differently these days. More glass towers going up where the old warehouses stood, but the docks? We're holding steady. You can feel the shift though—like the whole neighborhood's holding its breath waiting to see who benefits and who gets left behind."

None of these businesses lost ground this cycle. Every ledger in the neighborhood ticked upward. But a neighborhood where everyone is up can describe a good economy or a lopsided one, and the size of each increase decides which.

When a port grows by hundreds of thousands and the grill feeding its crews grows by a thousand, someone should explain where the difference goes. It could be payroll for Patel's repair crews. It could be the upgrades he says the docks will need. It could be the towers Leon is watching rise. Or it could end up somewhere the dishwasher at Green & Gold never sees.

The record doesn't say yet. That leaves the question Lilo raised from the wheelhouse. What does the Port's growth mean for the everyday worker in Jack London?

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

# Ninety-One Households, One Ledger

"Ninety-one households stabilized means ninety-one West Oakland families who won't face displacement this month," Marcus Webb said on the record this cycle, "and every case file meets compliance standards that will survive audit." The statement is the only place in this cycle's record where the Stabilization Fund is counted by household rather than by dollar, and it is the figure the city is standing on.

The money is real and it is moving. The West Oakland Stabilization Fund carries a $28,000,000 budget, with $22,600,000 still remaining, and its latest disbursement landed this cycle, Cycle 111: a month-ten payment of $400,000. Avery Santana, who proposed the Fund, announced that approval in two statements. "Month-ten disbursement approved — $400,000 to keep West Oakland families in their homes," she said at the gavel. At the open, she added: "The families who qualified for this money six months ago shouldn't wait another day." Brenda Okoro put the stakes in the same terms: "Stabilization is a race against displacement — month-ten disbursement keeps us ahead."

The Fund's path to this point runs through a single vote. In Cycle 78 the measure passed 6-3, against a required threshold of 6-3, and Santana signed it that cycle. The record names three of the nine seats: Ramon Vega voted yes, Leonard Tran voted no, and Elliott Crane voted yes remotely. The file does not place the other six, and this ledger will not infer them from a tally.

Denise Carter, whose District 1 office issued this cycle's council statement, framed the Fund's reach around movement rather than fixed addresses: "When storefronts go quiet, families go next—so we're taking the Stabilization Fund to where the need is moving." That sentence points at a geography the tracker does not map. The Initiative is logged to West Oakland, and nothing in the file breaks the $400,000 down by block, by household, or by case.

The clock is the strongest part of the record. Month-nine, in Cycle 110, cleared at $400,000 with a 23-day average processing time. Cycle 111 held the 23-day processing standard again. The next action is the month-eleven disbursement review in Cycle 112. An average is a single number, and the file carries no case-level processing times. A 23-day mean can hold cases that took far longer, and the record does not say whether any did.

Two neighboring Initiatives show the same pattern of announced money and thin public accounting. The West Oakland Tenant Defense Fund is scheduled for a vote in Cycle 112 at a $4.2M budget. Commercial Stabilization for Grand Lake sits at legislation-filed with $2.5M. Neither is in disbursement, and neither has a payment on the books this cycle.

What the Packet does establish is this: the Fund moved $400,000 on schedule, the processing standard held, and the city's account of the families involved is one household count and one compliance claim, both from the office that runs the Fund. What it does not establish is where the money lands once it leaves the treasury, which block, which tenant, or which landlord. The city treasury took in $5.1M this cycle and closed at $110.3M, and nothing in the file traces the Fund's dollars through that balance.

Webb's ninety-one families are a real number in a real statement. They are not yet a place on a map or a name in a file the public can read. Ask who should answer the question the disbursement invites. The Stabilization Fund's office signed the audit claim. The mayor's office announced the payment. The council seat that moved the Fund toward "where the need is moving" has not said where that is in the record this cycle.

If the money is moving on time, why can't the city show us where it lands?

# Ellis's Game Five Line Carries the Title

The line that matters from Game Five is short: six innings, two earned runs, five strikeouts for John Ellis. On the feed, those three numbers are the whole of his box for the night, and they are enough to explain why the A's are one win from the cup of their chapter and why the rest of the lineup's stat lines read as support rather than headline. The club's team record on the feed stands at 4-1 with a three-game winning streak, and Game Five completed the sequence that delivered the A's their seventh World Series title under Mike Paulson.

Start with the pitching, because it did the most work. Six innings against a single game's ceiling is a full outing, and two earned runs over that span is the kind of line a manager builds a closing argument around. Five strikeouts add a second layer: Ellis did not survive on contact alone, and the feed records the strikeouts as the element that kept the outing controlled. The strikeout total is a number a reader can check against the innings without any further arithmetic from this desk, and it points toward a pitcher who was getting outs with his own stuff rather than waiting for the defense to help.

The hitters carried the rest of the sheet. Eric Taveras went 1HR and 2RBI, which is the power the club needed in a game that was not going to be decided by singles. Darrin Davis put up 1HR, 2RBI and a 3-4 night, the most complete offensive line the feed records for any Game Five bat. Mark Aitken added 1HR and 1RBI. The feed frames Taveras as the source of the power and the two veterans, Davis and Aitken, as the contributors around him, and the stat lines support that division: three players with home runs, two of them with multiple runs driven in, one a three-hit night from the middle of the order.

Here is where the analyst has to slow down. The Is-It-Real test asks whether a pattern has the volume to bear the weight placed on it. One start is one start. Six innings and five strikeouts on a single night is evidence of what Ellis did in Game Five, not a sample that says how he will handle the next high-leverage inning, or the next series. The same logic applies to the hitters. A home run, two runs driven in and a three-hit game are the kind of single-game production that makes a box score look like a turning point. A reader should not confuse the feed's clean line with a season-long identity for any of these players.

The Box-Card Read, applied properly, asks for the card before the story. The card on the feed is a clean one: Ellis's innings and earned runs, the hitters' homers and RBI, and a team record that now carries the club through a title. What the card does not supply is the context that would turn a single game into a trend. There is no salary on this feed row, no season-to-date ERA beyond what the Game Five line implies, and no prior-start sequence that would show whether the six-inning outing was a step in a pattern or an isolated good night. Those are the missing pieces.

Vinnie Keane and Benji Dillon also appear in their final games, a detail the club's record acknowledges without this feed giving a line for either beyond their presence in the closing sequence. That absence is itself a reason to wait for the fuller record before judging the roster's construction.

The question the game leaves open is the one the feed cannot answer on its own: what does Ellis's Game Five performance reveal about his ability to handle pressure in high-stakes moments? The answer requires more than one line on one night, starts beyond this one, and the season-level numbers that have not yet been printed on this feed.

That's when you feel the weight of everything you've carried, and it's finally okay to put it down."

When the last bus pulls away and the confetti settles, what does a championship room look like when it knows it won't all be back?

# The Oaks Have Not Won the Room Yet

**Talia Finch | Bay Tribune Sports**

Melton Neilon is talking over the clink of a glass in an unnamed Jack London bar, the kind of place where the game can be on without owning the room. He is a fan, he says, but not the sort who wants to turn following the Oaks into a second job.

“Look, I'm a fan—I plan my whole life around the games—but the analysis side of things? That's a different beast. Between keeping my starter alive and running the bakery, I just don't have the bandwidth to get bogged down in the stats. I'd rather just watch the game and hope for the best than try to decode some column in the Tribune.”

That is the Oaks’ early-season problem in its most ordinary form: not hatred, not devotion, but the work of deciding whether this team belongs in the part of a life that already has other claims. The ground read around the Oaks is uncertain. The room mood is frustrated. The club’s losing has become something GM Elliot Abraham has had to address with media before the season is set to start next cycle, while questions hang around the team, including whether Draymond is coming back to the Oaks. Abraham acknowledged the losing and offered reassurance.

No one in this bar looks like they are panicking. They look busy.

Rick Walker, who follows the talk around the neighborhood between grading papers and everything else, gives the other half of the room’s answer. His confidence is not certainty. It is motion.

“Yeah, that's still where I'm at. Look, I'm a fan, I love the game, but between grading papers and everything else, I don't have the time to be a full-time analyst. I hear the talk around the neighborhood and I follow the news, but honestly, it's just a lot to keep up with. I'm just riding the wave like everyone else.”

That wave has not carried the Oaks into unquestioned belonging. It has carried them into conversation, which is a smaller thing and, for a new club, maybe the first thing that matters. Melton wants the game without the decoding. Rick is willing to keep moving with it, even while admitting he cannot keep up with every turn.

Abraham’s reassurance may be a strategy for holding the room steady. It may also be the shield a struggling early start requires. From where I sit, the answer is not in the media room. It is in the pause before somebody chooses the Oaks game, the glance back at the screen, the decision to care when nobody has demanded it yet.

The television keeps playing without sound. A glass settles on the bar. Nobody leaves.

By Jax Caldera | Bay Tribune Civic

# Fruitvale Is Packed at Lunch and the Map Says It's Decaying. Who Owns That?

I stood outside a storefront office in Fruitvale at noon, reading transit hub plans through the glass. The pages curl at the corners where the sun has been working them. Nobody inside looked up.

Down the block the taqueria was slammed. Work boots at the counter, blazers in line behind them, one door and one register for both. I ate standing up. I counted two people leaving with their lunch for every one coming in, and I counted nothing else, because nobody there was telling me anything and I made up nothing.

Then I went and read the map.

The map says Fruitvale is in decay. Mood is down by about a sixth of a point. Retail vitality is down by two full points. Housing pressure is up a full notch, to 4.5, in a city where the median is zero.

Now the part that doesn't compute. On mood, Fruitvale still ranks first of twenty-two neighborhoods. On retail, fifth of twenty-two. The mood number is more than half again the city median, and retail is well above it. Housing pressure, on the other hand, sits near the bottom of the pack. So this isn't a dead block. It's a block that tops the table on feeling good and is still sliding.

The sliding is what I keep staring at. A lunch line out the door is exactly what a place looks like right before the people in it can't afford to stay. I'm not saying that's what's happening. I'm saying the map can't tell me it isn't, and neither can anyone I could find with a title.

Here's what I could verify. Two programs on the city's roster list Fruitvale in their footprint: the Oakland Alternative Response Initiative and the Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline. Both are running. Neither one owns this break. The record is plain on that: no active initiative, no mitigator, nothing with a name on it that answers the housing squeeze or the retail slide.

Translate that. A neighborhood can be on the roster and still be on its own. Putting a place in a program's footprint doesn't mean anyone is working the problem that is actually hurting it.

I also don't know what came before. The map gives a direction but not the earlier readings I'd need to say how long this has been going on, or whether last week's dip was a blip. That's missing. And nobody from any office has said a word to me about the mismatch. I can't tell you whether that's silence or whether nobody was ever asked. Somebody's job should be to know.

Meanwhile the transit hub plans sit in a window. Pages curling. A hub means new people, new money, new rent. I won't claim any of it is coming for these blocks, because the record doesn't say that. But a plan in a window and a pressure number going up on the same street is a coincidence somebody at City Hall should be able to explain without a press officer.

So I'll ask the way I'd ask the guy next to me at any bar in town. Who is watching Fruitvale's housing? Not who's funded in the footprint. Who is watching the actual rent pressure, today, with a name and a phone number?

The taqueria will be packed again tomorrow. That's not an answer.

The map says decay, and the program roster doesn't answer it. So whose name goes on the answer?

By Trevor Shimizu | Bay Tribune Civic

# 12th Street Is Running Better. The People Are Somewhere Else.

*By Trevor Shimizu*

At 7:40 at 12th St Oakland City Center, Joel Wren was looking at a quieter station and a smoother service. The station recorded 8,293 riders in Cycle 111, down 15,192 from the previous cycle. Its on-time performance improved to 86%, five points higher than before.

Those two measurements are supposed to describe the same transit system. Here they do not.

“I’ve been noticing fewer folks at the 12th Street station lately, even though the buses are running smoother than ever,” Wren said. “It’s strange—people used to pack in there, but now it feels quieter. Maybe it’s the Baylight construction approvals making folks rethink their routes. Hard to say, but something’s shifted.”

The fare gate continues its small mechanical argument with each person who uses it. The schedule can be better while the station becomes less useful. On-time performance measures whether service arrives when expected. Ridership measures whether people still choose to be there. A system can improve the first measure and lose the second.

Calvin Mwangi, an AC Transit bus driver, said he has not heard regular riders identify 12th Street as a specific avoidance point.

“Nah, can't say I've heard my regulars talk much about avoiding 12th Street specifically,” Mwangi said. “Most folks just grumble about the usual—delays, construction, you know how it is. But no, nothing about the council vote coming up in conversation.”

That distinction matters. The record does not establish that Baylight District construction caused the missing riders. It does establish that Baylight District construction is active around Downtown, while the station posted its largest decline among the eight stations tracked this cycle. Construction is a plausible explanation, not a completed diagnosis.

The same separation appears elsewhere. Nineteen St Oakland fell by 10,950 riders while its on-time result slipped to 77%. West Oakland fell by 5,974 while on-time performance rose nine points, to 84%. Fruitvale fell by 7,557 while on-time performance rose seven points, to 85%. The pattern is not limited to one station or one kind of schedule.

Ray Neal, an AC Transit Bus Operator, described the operational symptom without assigning it a cause.

“You know, it’s quieter now. Fewer folks waiting, fewer folks boarding. Feels like the energy’s shifted somewhere else, but I can’t quite put my finger on where.”

Across the eight stations, ridership totaled 36,303 in Cycle 111, compared with 91,564 in the prior cycle. That is a large change in the system’s use, but the record also marks the cycle as a holiday period with events in Downtown, Fruitvale, Dimond, East Oakland, Jack London, Rockridge and Piedmont Ave. Those conditions complicate any clean explanation. A quieter platform can be the result of construction, a holiday pattern, changed destinations, or several pressures operating together.

Evan Lewis, a bus driver, put the gap between the dashboard and the sidewalk more directly.

“You know, it's funny—on paper, the numbers look good, right? Better on-time stats should mean happier riders. But driving the route, I see it different. When they start tearing up streets for these big projects, people just... find another way. Doesn't matter if the bus shows up on time if the sidewalks are blocked and the air's full of dust. Folks in my neighborhood talk about it at the stop—they're tired of detours and noise, so they walk, bike, or just stay home. The stats don't show that frustration.”

The infrastructure question is therefore not only whether a bus arrives on time. It is whether the full path to the bus remains attractive enough to use. Cycle 111 shows a service that is, in several places, more punctual and less occupied. Before Baylight District construction advances further, the unanswered maintenance question is simple: where did the 15,192 missing 12th Street riders go?

# Richards Struck Out Less in 2039, 2040 and 2041 Than in Any Season Before Them

Martin Richards, the Athletics third baseman, hit .302 in 2041 with 27 home runs and a .551 slugging average over 463 at-bats. He struck out 70 times, once every 6.6 at-bats. His career line shows that rate has stood outside his earlier band for three straight seasons.

## The claim

The claim is narrow. In 2039, 2040 and 2041, Richards struck out less often per at-bat than in any of the five seasons from 2034 through 2038. The power did not shrink to pay for it.

## The line, season by season

Larger numbers mean fewer strikeouts. Each figure comes from his career line, with the at-bat count beside it.

## The hinge, and what it is made of

The hinge is 2039. It is the best strikeout rate on file, and it comes from the thinnest full-time sample: 90 games and 348 at-bats, with 44 strikeouts. A season that short is variance until another one confirms it. Two did. The 2040 line (499 at-bats, one strikeout every 6.7) and the 2041 line (463 at-bats, one every 6.6) are both near the size of his 2034 through 2038 seasons.

The better evidence is those two seasons, not 2039. The rate moved from 7.9 to 6.7 to 6.6, so the 2039 figure has not held. It has settled lower, and the band is still well clear of the 5.1 to 5.6 that preceded it.

The home-run rate runs with this. His 2038 season, at one every 16.9 at-bats, is the closest earlier match to 2041's one every 17.1. In 2038 he also struck out once every 5.1. In 2041 the home-run rate is about the same and the strikeouts are fewer.

## What the line does not show

Two columns run against the contact reading. His walks fell from 62 in 2040 to 35 in 2041, and his on-base average fell from .377 to .355. That is the lowest on-base figure since 2034's .342. The strikeout improvement did not arrive with more on-base value in 2041. His slugging average of .551 is the highest on file, so the 2041 gain came from power. This memo does not reach whether the walk drop is a pattern or one season.

The line also says nothing on cause. The career record does not say why the rate changed in 2039, and no one has been asked.

The 2039 through 2041 stretch would then be a three-year dip, not a change. A rate at 6.5 or above across a full season would extend the claim to four seasons.

# Tara Ellison Is Rising, and Nobody Around Her Can Say Where

Tara Ellison is standing on a Rockridge sidewalk with her hands loose at her sides, and when the question comes she takes a second before she answers. "I'm not sure what you mean. I'm just here in Rockridge, and I'm not in a room with anyone right now."

The ledger has a different account of her. This cycle it lists Ellison as a journalist in Media, Rockridge, climbing fast enough that it flags her as surging. Her name sits among the 39 culture figures the ledger counts as rising this cycle, and one of those 39 is new to the record. She was last logged the cycle before this one. Between those two points, the ledger doesn't say what she did, only that the numbers moved her up.

So the question becomes: what does a rising name look like from the street it supposedly belongs to?

I went looking for the answer in the neighborhoods around her. In Laurel, sculptor Alma Vasquez was at a stoplight with the window down, and she did not know Ellison's name well enough to say much about it. "To be honest, I don't really know much about Tara Ellison or her rise in Rockridge. I live in Laurel, and while I'm aware of the general vibe around Oakland, I don't have a clear view of what's going on in Rockridge. I do see that there are a lot of cultural figures rising in fame, and that's interesting. Sometimes when I'm driving through other neighborhoods, I catch snippets of the local culture, like the bassline from a car at the stoplight here in Laurel."

That bassline is the closest thing to evidence I have of a scene. It came through an open window, moved past in a second, and belonged to nobody I could see. Vasquez hears culture the way a driver does, in pieces, between red lights.

In Fruitvale, actress Nina Reyes was not thinking about any of it. "To be honest, I haven't really thought about Tara Ellison's fame or what's happening over in Rockridge. I've just been focusing on my own life and things here in Fruitvale." Reyes is also listed among the rising figures, which means that the ledger's rising list and the people who live near those figures are not the same group. Being counted is one thing. Being talked about on the block is another.

The ledger keeps its own balance. It has moved Lena Cross and Rowan Pierce onto its where-are-they-now file, a quieter list for names that have stopped climbing. Rising and fading sit in the same record, and the same neighborhoods hold both.

Walking back toward the car, I kept thinking about how little of this is visible. Ellison's rise is real on the ledger. Vasquez's bassline is real at a light in Laurel. Reyes's indifference is real in Fruitvale. None of those three places touches the others in any way I can document. A surge in one name does not prove a neighborhood is moving, and a neighborhood humming with music does not prove that a single name is the reason.

So who should answer for that gap? The ledger keeps the count, the figures know their own lives, and the people on the corner are busy with something else. Between the three of them, the connecting thread is still missing.

Is this a sustainable surge, or only a momentary trend that will pass before the next stoplight?
