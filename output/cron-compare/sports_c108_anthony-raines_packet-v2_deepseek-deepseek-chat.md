The first thing that jumps out on Danny Horn's postseason card is the zero in the home run column. Through forty-two at-bats this October, Oakland's center fielder is hitting a meager .190, with just three runs batted in and a slugging percentage that would make a light-hitting middle infielder blush. For a player expected to anchor the A's lineup, those numbers are hard to ignore.

"Look, I know my numbers haven’t been where I want them this postseason," Horn admitted in a quiet moment before Game 1 of the World Series. "But I’m grinding every day. This team’s got heart, and I’m gonna do everything I can to help us bring home that championship."

The sentiment is admirable, but the Phillies’ pitching staff won’t care about heart when Horn steps to the plate. His struggles are amplified by the success of his teammates. Eric Taveras is hitting .359 with four home runs. Vladimir Gonzalez has matched Taveras' power output while batting .286. Ernesto Quintero, often hitting behind Horn, has three homers and a .324 average. In a lineup that has otherwise thrived, Horn’s slump stands out like a sore thumb.

"Danny Horn’s been grinding, and I respect that," manager Deacon Seymour said. "The numbers don’t tell the whole story—he’s a guy who shows up every day, puts in the work, and keeps his head in the game. Right now, he’s not finding the gaps, but I’ve seen him turn it around before."

The A’s have managed to win despite Horn’s struggles, but the margin for error becomes razor-thin in the World Series. Game 1 starter Arturo Ramos, who carries a 1.93 ERA into tonight’s contest, knows the stakes. "Numbers don’t always tell the whole story—he’s got the kind of at-bats that wear pitchers down," Ramos said of Horn. "We’re not worried. When it clicks for him, it’s gonna be big."

The question isn’t just whether Horn can turn it around—it’s whether the A’s can survive another series if he doesn’t. With Philadelphia’s rotation looking formidable on paper, Oakland may not have the luxury of carrying a slumping star. For a team chasing its seventh ring, the margin for error has never been smaller.
