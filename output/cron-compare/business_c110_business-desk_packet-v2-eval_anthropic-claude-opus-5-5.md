# A $1.5 Million Cycle Downtown, and a $376 One

*By Jordan Velez, Bay Tribune Business Desk*

Priya Nair's job is to make sure Oakland's water keeps arriving after the conditions the pipes were built for stop holding. She is a climate adaptation engineer at EBMUD, one of 1,900 people on the utility's payroll. This cycle the utility's revenue came to $656.6 million, up $1.5 million from last cycle.

That is the biggest number on the Downtown business ledger this cycle. The most honest way to read it is next to the smallest one.

Miso Metro, a Downtown restaurant with two employees, booked $250,000 in revenue. That is $376 more than last cycle. One of those two employees is Andre Turner, a line cook. When a business has two people on payroll, there is no department to absorb a slow stretch and no headcount to trim before the owner's own hours go. A $376 gain spread across a kitchen that small is not a trend. It is a margin, and margins at that size decide whether the lights are on next quarter.

I keep a printout of the port's monthly numbers taped to my wall because my father worked the docks for thirty years. I learned early that the headline figure tells you how the building is doing, not how the people inside it are doing. The Downtown ledger reads the same way. Every business on it moved up this cycle. Not one of them moved up by the same kind of money.

Between the utility and the kitchen sit the firms that draw Downtown's buildings.

- **Atlas Bay Architects** carries 86 employees and $42.3 million in revenue, up $78,000 from last cycle. Its key personnel include Sarah Huang, the firm's healthcare director. Its roster includes Tobias Jurko, a landscape architect.
- **Pacific Standard Architecture**, led by principal Theodore Lindqvist, is the slightly larger shop. It has 96 employees and $48.3 million in revenue, up $69,000.

Two architecture firms of roughly the same size posted roughly the same gain. That kind of steadiness usually means work is on the boards. Whose work, and for which clients, the ledger does not say.

Then there is the Oakland Entertainment Talent Ageny. It has 10 employees and $1.3 million in revenue, up $1,000. Nina Rines, an actress, is on its roster. For a performer, the agency's thin cycle-over-cycle gain is not an abstraction. The question is whether the next call comes in. A talent agency's revenue is built out of other people's bookings, and $1,000 of growth does not tell an actress whether her phone will ring.

Nair is the one worker on this slice of the ledger who spoke for the record. She framed her job less as maintenance than as a bet on the future of the neighborhood she works in.

"My work at EBMUD focuses on ensuring our water systems can handle the pressures of climate change, especially as the city grows," Nair said. "It’s about balancing the need for reliable infrastructure with the realities of a changing environment. Downtown’s growth adds complexity, but it’s also a chance to innovate and build resilience into the system."

Downtown's growth is the phrase I'd underline. It means something different depending on where you stand in it.

- For a 1,900-person utility, growth is a load on the system: more demand, more pressure, more reason to engineer ahead of it. Nair's work exists because that growth is assumed.
- For a two-person kitchen, growth is whether the people filling the new office floors come down to the counter at lunch.
- For a ten-person agency, growth is whether the money moving through the neighborhood reaches the people it represents.

This is exactly why I am skeptical of development talk. It gets announced as one thing and lands as several. Resilience built into a water system protects everybody who turns on a tap Downtown. That is the kind of public good the word is supposed to describe. The revenue that grows alongside it does not spread itself the same way. A $1.5 million cycle and a $376 cycle happened on the same ledger, in the same neighborhood, in the same stretch of weeks.

None of these businesses shrank this cycle. Nobody on this list shed workers, and the ledger shows no closure. That is worth saying plainly, because Downtown rarely gets good news that isn't attached to a press release.

But the fact that everyone went up is where the reporting starts, not where it ends. Here is the question I'd put to every name on this list, from the utility's engineers to the cook on Miso Metro's line: what does steady growth look like for the people clocking in every day? And when the next tight cycle comes, which of these payrolls has room to absorb it, and which one is two people deep?
