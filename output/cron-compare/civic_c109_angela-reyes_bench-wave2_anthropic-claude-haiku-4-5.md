# Temescal's Quiet Gap: 85% Graduate, But What Comes After?

Lo Thoop stands in the hallway outside his creative writing classroom in Temescal as students file past at 3:05, their backpacks already on. He watches them go—311 students in all, moving through a school system that reads as the weakest in the city on paper. Temescal's quality index sits at 6.8, nearly two full points behind Rockridge across town. But the gap that troubles Lo isn't the one between neighborhoods. It's the one between a diploma and what happens next.

"You know, living here in Temescal, I've seen kids graduate—they walk across that stage, families cheering—but then there's this quiet worry afterward," Lo says. "Are they really ready for what's next? It's like we're celebrating the finish line, but the race isn't over. I teach creative writing, and I see it in their essays—bright ideas, but sometimes they're missing the tools to build on them. It's not just about getting them out the door; it's about making sure they're equipped to walk through the next one."

The numbers bear out that worry. Temescal's graduation rate holds steady at 85.67%—a respectable figure that gets celebrated at ceremonies every spring. But only 57% of those graduates test as college-ready. That means roughly half of every graduating class crosses the stage unprepared for the next step, whatever that step might be.

Patricia Nolan, who spent decades teaching in Temescal before retiring, has watched this pattern repeat across cycles. She speaks quietly, with the weight of someone who has seen the disconnect accumulate. "You know, after teaching for so many years, I've seen kids walk across that graduation stage who can barely write a proper paragraph. The numbers might say 85% graduate, but half of them aren't ready for what comes next. It's not just about passing classes—it's about whether these kids can actually think critically when they leave here. We celebrate the diploma, but we're not always honest about what it really means."

In the same building, Jun Leu, a student moving through Temescal schools, doesn't yet carry the weight of those statistics. "I don't really think about graduation rates or college stuff yet. At school, we just do our work and go home. Some kids talk about college, but most just wanna play basketball or hang out after class." His cousin mentioned that high school is harder than middle school. That gap—between where a student stands and where they need to be—is not measured by indices.

Salma Navarro sits with similar immediacy. "I don't really think about the numbers. At school, everyone's just trying to get through the day. Some teachers really push us, but others just let things slide." She has a brother who went through this. "My brother Hector says high school was like that too—you could graduate easy, but actually being ready for what's next? That's different."

The Oakland Unified School District, which serves 5,201 employees across the city, allocates $11,500 per student in Temescal—a material difference from the $15,000 that flows into Rockridge, where 94.91% of students graduate college-ready. Teacher quality in Temescal reads as 6.96 on the index; in Rockridge, it's 9.04. These are not small gaps. They are structural.

But the real question—the one that the stage and the cheering families do not settle—is whether the next step is even visible from here. What does Temescal need, and who is supposed to build it?
