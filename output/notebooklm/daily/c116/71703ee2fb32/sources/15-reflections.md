# Rockridge, in their own words

## Benji Dillon — Pitcher, Oakland A's Legend

> The door's open because I haven't walked through it yet, and that's not indecision—it's honesty. I know what a bullpen role looks like at 38, and I know what my arm has left; the question isn't whether I can help, it's whether helping from that seat feels like finishing or like hanging on. Oakland's the only place I'd do it, but $13M is a lot to ask a club that's building around kids who need the innings more than I need the closure. If Mark stays, that changes the math—not the money, but the reason. If he goes, maybe that's the sign I've been waiting for: the city's next chapter doesn't need my name on the roster to carry my weight.

## Yu Zen — Commercial Insurance Broker

> The walk from Rockridge to the hospital is a straight shot down College Avenue, past the shops and the families out on the sidewalks, then over toward Broadway. It's a route I know well, but I wasn't walking it before I was admitted. The days before, I was working through another weekend, telling myself it was fine. My wife held down the house, the kids, the meals, the school runs — she always does. At work, my team covered my accounts, my clients, the paperwork. I didn't have a shift to hand off; I just had to trust people. And I did. But I'll tell you, when you're in that bed, you think about the people who carried you there — and the ones who carried you before you even fell.

## Maurice Franklin — Drone Fleet Coordinator

> The days before I went in, I kept telling myself it was just a bad cold. I'd wake up, cough, check my temperature, and head to work anyway — you do that, you know? You've got a job, people depending on you, and the card statement's already sitting there. So you push through. By the time I couldn't catch my breath walking from the car to the front door, I knew I'd waited too long. But that's what it takes for a working person to get to a bed — you don't go until you can't stand, and then you hope there's a bed waiting. I was lucky. I got one. But I think about the folks in Chinatown, all those sick people, and how few of them show up on that hospital list. It's not that they're not sick. It's that they can't afford to stop. I know that feeling in my chest, and I know what it costs to ignore it.
