The warehouse doors at 7th and Mandela stood open to the wind, though no trucks rolled in or out. Dust from the vacant lot next to the old rail yard had begun to coat the sidewalk markers where the West Oakland Stabilization Fund had promised grants would anchor. No banners hung. No residents lined up. The only motion was the flicker of a city tag—**West Oakland Stabilization Fund, Disbursement-Active**—pasted beside a peeling mural of a woman holding a key, her face turned toward a skyline that hadn’t yet arrived.

Cycle 109 marked the eighth month of disbursement under the $28 million fund, approved in Cycle 78 by a 6-3 council vote and signed into law by Mayor Avery Santana. The initiative’s charter, as recorded in Cycle 108, narrowed its scope: stabilization grants were reserved for long-tenure West Oakland residents displaced by flat construction, not for broad neighborhood relief. Deputy Mayor Okoro’s office oversaw the release of 47 grants totaling approximately $4.2 million by the close of Cycle 108. In Cycle 109, the month-eight tranche of $400,000 was released, with $81,188 allocated across two household grants.

The fund’s remaining balance stands at $23,400,000. Its next scheduled action—a month-nine disbursement review—is set for Cycle 110. The policy domain remains economic, the implementation phase unchanged: disbursement-active.

The vote to pass the fund in Cycle 78 split along no clear bloc lines. Ramon Vega voted yes. Leonard Tran voted no. Elliott Crane cast a remote yes vote. The full council composition at the time met the 6-3 threshold required for passage. No absences were recorded in the final tally.

Other initiatives remain in motion. The Temescal Community Health Center, passed in Cycle 80 with a $45 million budget, is now operational. The Baylight District project, approved in Cycle 83 with a $2.1 billion allocation, is in construction-active phase. Mayor Santana maintains a 69% approval rating. District Attorney Clarissa Dane and Public Defender Caleb Reyes both hold 65% approval. Councilmembers Denise Carter (District 1), Leonard Tran (District 2), and Rose Delgado (District 3) report approval ratings of 62%, 65%, and 64%, respectively.

The fund was proposed by Mayor Santana, who retains executive oversight. Deputy Mayor Okoro continues to administer disbursements under the charter’s narrow criteria. No changes to eligibility, scope, or leadership have been recorded in Cycle 109.

Yet the silence at 7th and Mandela persists. Two grants issued. A fraction of the tranche spent. The majority of the fund still unallocated. The stated purpose—stabilization for those priced out—remains bound to a definition that excludes broader displacement pressures now visible in commercial vacancies, shifting foot traffic, and unoccupied lots where small operators once held ground.

Who qualifies, and who does not, is written into the charter. But what the charter does not name—the corners of need beyond its lines—remains unmeasured, unclaimed, and unaddressed.
