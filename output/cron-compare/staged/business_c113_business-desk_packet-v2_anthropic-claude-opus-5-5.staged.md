# Temescal's Ledger Is Growing. The Question Is Who It's Growing For.

*By Jordan Velez, Business Reporter*

Da Dough's books moved $128 this cycle. That is the whole gain on $371,000 in revenue, at a five-person bakery where Melton Neilon is on the roster as the sourdough baker. A few doors down the economic ladder, OakTown Social, a seven-person room where Vanessa Treary and Vanessa Ramas work the espresso machine, added $308 on $521,000.

At Gridiron Analytics, the sports-analytics shop founded by Daniel Yoon and Christine Nakamura, revenue rose $16,000 to $2.9 million. The firm carries 20 employees and a growth figure of 27.9 percent. That is the fastest number on any Temescal ledger line this cycle, and it is not close.

Every one of Temescal's five named businesses is in the black and moving up. On paper, that is a good-news story. I have covered this city long enough to read a column of plus signs the way my father read a quiet dock. The question is not whether the numbers went up. It is whose rent they pay.

## Five businesses, twelve names

The ledger lists twelve named workers across those five businesses this cycle. They sort into three different economies sharing one neighborhood.

**The analytics firm.** Ariana Reyes and Jade Soy are data analysts at Gridiron. Dawson Nguyen and Wesley Clark are sports analytics leads. Two more workers sit on the roster beyond them. This is laptop money: high growth, a small headcount, and revenue that can come from clients who never set foot on the block.

**The contractor.** Calderon-Nishi is the biggest employer of the five, with 34 people, $9 million in revenue, a gain of $14,000 and growth of 8.2 percent. The names on its roster are trade names: Jett Walker, a plumber, and Ronald Silk and Cristina Norwood, both electricians. When construction money is working, it is supposed to show up in wrenches and wire.

**The counters and the clinic.** At the counter level sit the bakery and the coffee bar. Then there is the outlier.

## The health center's numbers

Temescal Community Health Center reports $45.1 million in revenue with 14 employees. Its growth is 1.2 percent, and its revenue rose $10,000 since last cycle. Clearence Leighton works there as a physical therapist. Bobby Chen-Ramirez is the project director.

Chen-Ramirez does not talk like a man reading his own balance sheet.

"Look, I run a health center project, not a spreadsheet," he said. "But I walk that block every day, and I see the same thing you're describing — the numbers on the wall don't mean a damn thing if Mrs. Okafor still can't get her blood pressure checked without taking two buses. What creates accountability for me is simple: I've got 14 employees on my roster, and every one of them lives in this neighborhood. When I see hiring signs in every storefront on the Jack London corridor, I want to know who's actually getting those jobs — is it the kid who grew up here, or is it somebody who commutes in from the hills? That's the story. That's what I want answered. Because if growth just means the ledger gets fatter while the sidewalk stays the same, then we're building a ghost town with good accounting."

A ghost town with good accounting is a line that should be taped to the wall of every office that announces a "development" initiative.

## Where the money lands

Here is the analysis, and I am labeling it as analysis. The dollar gains in Temescal this cycle are not evenly spread.

Three businesses account for nearly all of them: Gridiron, Calderon-Nishi and the health center. Together they added $40,000. The two storefronts where a neighbor actually buys something with cash in hand, the bakery and the coffee bar, added less than $450 between them. A baker and two baristas are holding steady. They are not getting a raise from this growth unless somebody decides to pass it down.

None of that makes the growing firms villains. A 34-person contractor that pays electricians and plumbers is exactly the kind of employer this city says it wants. A health center with a $45 million budget is an anchor. But anchors stay put whether or not the boats around them float.

When my mother's laundromat on 7th Street closed in 2017, the neighborhood around it was growing too. The building sold. The growth was real. It just was not hers.

## The view from Ivy Hill

For contrast, the one contraction on this cycle's business list is across town. Ivy Hill Market, in Ivy Hill, shed two workers since last cycle, and its growth is now negative 3.9 percent. Two jobs is not a headline anywhere but on that block. On that block, it is the only headline.

Temescal is not there. Nothing on its ledger is shrinking. That is precisely why Chen-Ramirez's question matters now, before it becomes a post-mortem.

## The open question

Who is getting the Gridiron seats? Who is pulling cable for Calderon-Nishi? Does the health center's budget buy the Mrs. Okafors of the block a shorter trip, or just a bigger number?

The ledger cannot say. The people at the counter might.

If every business on the block is growing, why does it still feel like the neighborhood is being pulled in two directions?
