THE CYCLE PULSE 89— CULTURE & COMMUNITY_Darrin Davis
The Outlaw and the Badge
By Maria Keen | Bay Tribune

OAKLAND — Darrin Davis keeps a police badge in his locker. Not hidden. Not tucked away. Right there next to his batting gloves, where he can see it before every game.

It’s not a prop. In 2032, his hometown of Columbus, Ohio made him an honorary police captain — recognition for years of charity work, youth programs, and a commitment to public safety that predates his first MVP trophy. He wears it on his heart the same way he wears his World Series rings on his fingers.

“Baseball’s what I do,” Davis told me, leaning forward in the dugout with that restless energy that never quite settles. “Justice is what I believe.”

I’ve covered Oakland for forty years. I’ve watched athletes come and go, most of them treating the city like a way station between paychecks. Darrin Davis is different. The Ohio Outlaw — as the fans call him — has made Oakland’s problems his own. And right now, one of those problems has a name: OARI.

-----

THE BADGE AND THE MOU

The Oakland Alternative Response Initiative has missed its 45-day implementation deadline. Twelve responders hired. Dispatch integration in testing. And one unsigned document sitting on Chief Rafael Montez’s desk — the memorandum of understanding that would formalize how police and behavioral health teams work together.

Davis has been watching.

“I know Rafael,” he said. “We’ve talked about this. The badge doesn’t make you the enemy. It makes you responsible. And responsibility means knowing when to step back and let someone else handle the call.”

Davis and Montez met three years ago through the A’s community policing initiatives — ride-alongs, youth clinics, joint appearances at schools in West Oakland and Fruitvale. What started as PR evolved into something else: a friendship between a two-time MVP and a police chief, both of them trying to figure out what public safety looks like in a city that doesn’t always trust the people carrying badges.

“Rafael’s not stalling because he doesn’t believe in it,” Davis said. “He’s stalling because he wants it to work. There’s a difference.”

I asked Davis what he’d tell the council members who’ve called for ultimatums, who’ve accused the department of obstruction.

“I’d tell them to ride along,” he said. “Spend a night in the car. See what the officers see. Then tell me the MOU is simple.”

-----

COLUMBUS TO OAKLAND

To understand Darrin Davis, you have to understand where he came from.

Born in 2009 in Columbus, Ohio, the youngest of three brothers. His father was a steelworker — the kind of man who could fix anything with his hands except his own exhaustion. His mother worked long shifts at a clinic. The only lights in town, people used to say, came from Friday night games and the Fourth of July fair.

Davis was fast, funny, and fearless. He played shortstop like he was showing off for the whole county. But beneath the bravado was something more human: a young man who became a father in his teens, married his high school sweetheart before graduation, and learned to balance diapers with double plays before he ever had a scholarship.

By the time he walked onto Ohio State’s campus, he already had the kind of grit coaches couldn’t teach. He majored in criminal justice. He said once it was a nod to his hometown roots and a future he never ruled out.

“I thought I might be a cop,” he told me. “If baseball didn’t work out. I wanted to serve something.”

Baseball worked out. The Mets drafted him in 2026. Oakland traded for him two years later. But the badge never left his mind.

-----

THE OUTLAW

They call him the Ohio Outlaw of Oakland, but the nickname is a misdirection. Davis doesn’t play like a rebel. He plays like a man who knows exactly what he’s doing and refuses to apologize for how he does it.

At five foot nine, he looks more like a sprinter than a slugger. His career numbers say otherwise: 247 home runs, 269 stolen bases, a .277 lifetime average. Two MVPs — 2037 and 2040. Two World Series championships. Three consecutive Home Run Derby crowns. Seven All-Star appearances.

“Chaos scares people because it moves without asking first,” he said. “I make it ask me.”

His 2039 season nearly ended him. A .186 average across 74 games. Then the ACL tear. The whispers started: Davis is done. The Outlaw ran out of road.

He came back. Not the same player — better. More patient. More deliberate. Still electric, but now with a veteran’s sense of when to spend the energy and when to save it.

“You get one career,” he said. “One. I’m not saving any for later. I’m spending it all right now.”

-----

FIVE DAUGHTERS AND A PHONE CALL

Davis has five daughters. He calls his wife before every night game — same as he did when they were teenagers in Columbus, before either of them knew what their life would become.

“She keeps me honest,” he said. “The girls keep me humble. I come home after a two-homer game and my youngest asks me if I remembered to pick up milk.”

The family anchors him. So does the badge.

In Columbus, his honorary captain role isn’t ceremonial. He funds youth programs. He shows up at community centers. He talks to kids about choices — the ones that open doors and the ones that close them.

“I know what it’s like to be a teenager with a kid,” he said. “I know what it’s like to feel like the world already decided who you’re going to be. I want those kids to know: you decide. Not the world. You.”

-----

THE BRIDGE

John Ellis, one of the A’s rookies, calls Davis “the heartbeat in spikes.” It’s an apt description. In a clubhouse with Vinnie Keane’s farewell tour, Benji Dillon’s final rotation, and Isley Kelley’s last season at short, Davis is the bridge between the dynasty that was and the team that’s becoming.

He’s not the numbers champion — that’s Dillon’s realm, and Kelley’s. But in the theater of memory, he’s the one fans will remember most vividly. The slides that raised dust into the next inning. The throws from the warning track that cut down runners at third. The grin that never quit, even when the body did.

“Every franchise needs a pulse,” Anthony wrote in his deep dive last year. “Something that reminds them winning is supposed to feel like rebellion, not procedure.”

Davis gave Oakland that pulse. And now, in his fourteenth professional season, he’s giving something else: a voice for the city’s hardest conversations.

-----

WHAT HE’S WATCHING

I asked Davis what he wants to see happen with OARI.

“I want to see it work,” he said. “I want to see the responders deployed. I want to see the data. I want to see Rafael sign that MOU and I want to see the council give him room to do it right.”

He paused.

“But mostly I want to see Oakland trust itself. Trust that we can try something new without tearing each other apart over the details.”

For a man who plays baseball like controlled chaos, it was a measured answer. Deliberate. The kind of thing a veteran says when he’s learned that some games are won in the ninth inning, not the first.

“I’m an outlaw,” he said, smiling. “But I believe in the law. I believe in what it’s supposed to be. I believe in the people who wear the badge and the people who need them to show up differently.”

He leaned back. The batting gloves were half-on, half-off. The badge was in the locker, waiting.

“That’s not a contradiction,” he said. “That’s just being human.”
