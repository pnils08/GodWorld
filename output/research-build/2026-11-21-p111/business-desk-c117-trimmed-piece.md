# Oakland Casino Town Loses Three More Workers. The Roster Is Down Six Over Three Cycles.

**By Jordan Velez | Economics & Labor**

Oakland Casino Town in the Baylight District has 94 employees in the C117 business ledger, down 3 from last cycle. The ledger lists its average salary at $70,000. Multiply that out and the three departures take roughly $210,000 a year off the payroll. That is my arithmetic from an average, not a figure from the casino's books.

**Who left**

Casino manager Philly Rodriguez says all three were voluntary. "One of the dealers left for a job down in San Jose, another moved back east to be with family, and one of the pit supervisors just retired," he said. "Nobody got fired, nobody got pushed." He says senior floor staff are picking up extra hours and shifts are being shuffled. "It's tight some nights, but we're managing."

I could not verify those three departures independently. The ledger records the headcount change and nothing about reasons. The three people aren't named in the record, and neither Mims nor Owens could name them.

**Who is still on the floor**

Manfred Owens keeps the book near the back of the room, and his seat and schedule haven't changed. He describes a thinner floor. "Before, you'd see a few extra bodies around, people covering shifts, someone to grab a drink order or run a chip tray. Now it's leaner," he said. "The regulars notice it more than the tourists do." In his words, the ones still working "are moving a little faster."

That matches Rodriguez's account of senior staff covering extra hours. The cost is workload, and the ledger doesn't show it.

**Is it a pattern?**

Rodriguez says no: "casinos always have some turnover." Gregory Mims, who the ledger lists under organized crime at the casino, says yes. "That's not a blip, that's a pattern," he said, calling it "death by a thousand cuts." He concedes he doesn't know who left or why. His argument is about how the cuts get described, not about names.

The record supports part of Mims's point. Tribune coverage in C115 put the casino at 99 employees, one fewer than the cycle before, so about 100 at C114. Today's 94 minus the 3 lost this cycle implies 97 at C116. That is three straight cycles of decline:

| Cycle | Employees | Change |
|---|---|---|
| C114 | ~100 | — |
| C115 | 99 | −1 |
| C116 | ~97 (implied) | −2 |
| C117 | 94 | −3 |

The C116 figure is inferred from this cycle's change, not read directly. The total is 6 jobs, or 6%, and each cycle's loss has been larger than the one before.

Rodriguez's account also holds up. A retirement, a job change and a family move are ordinary churn, and the ledger can't separate churn from contraction. What it shows is that the departures aren't being backfilled. The count falls each cycle instead of dipping and recovering. I don't know whether the casino has stopped hiring or just hasn't found replacements, and that is the question for management.

The ledger's growth rate for the casino is −1.5% a year. That is up 1.0 point from last cycle even as headcount fell, so the rate and the roster aren't moving together.

**Context**

The casino isn't alone in shrinking, but the other losses are smaller. The city's economic-event record tags the casino loss as a services/retail sector impact, magnitude −8. Several small Glenview businesses each shed one job this cycle: Glenview Cafe, Parkridge Cleaners and Glenview Kitchen. Two dental practices lost three each, Northshore Dental in Adams Point and Hillside Family Dental in Glenview. Ivy Hill Family Dental closed, a magnitude −25 event.

Baylight District, where the casino sits, shows a median income of $139,000. Casino Town's $70,000 average salary is about half of that. The district's growth is in construction and tech, which are different employers hiring different people. The Baylight Construction Authority has 451 employees and DigitalOcean has 801, up 1 this cycle.

**What I'm watching**

- Whether Casino Town posts openings, and for which jobs.
- Whether the C118 headcount stabilizes near 94 or keeps falling.
- Whether the "extra hours" become scheduled overtime or a permanent change to how the floor runs.

One cycle of three departures is churn. Three cycles of declines that aren't made up is a trend, whatever the reasons for leaving.
