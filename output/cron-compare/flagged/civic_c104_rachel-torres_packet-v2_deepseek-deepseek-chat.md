# Alternative Response Teams Still Earning Trust in Pilot Neighborhoods  

The breakfast rush at the West Oakland diner where Elio Perez works hasn’t slowed enough for him to notice any concrete changes from the Alternative Response Initiative, but the chatter among regulars suggests cautious optimism. "I've heard folks at the diner talking about how it's good to have someone other than cops showing up for certain calls—makes people feel safer opening their doors, you know?" said Perez, wiping down the counter between orders. "But honestly, I'm still waiting to see if it really changes anything long-term."  

The initiative, now in its pilot evaluation phase across West Oakland, Fruitvale, and East Oakland, dispatches unarmed teams for non-violent calls involving mental health crises, public intoxication, and neighborhood disputes.  

Rick Walker, a line cook in Fruitvale, overhears similar conversations at his workplace. "I’ve heard folks around here talking about the Alternative Response Initiative, and it seems like people are hopeful but still waiting to see how it really plays out in the neighborhood," he said.  

For Depak Ying, a server pulling double shifts in Rockridge, the initiative registers more as background noise than tangible shift. "I've heard some folks talking about the Alternative Response teams in the neighborhood, but honestly, I'm too busy working double shifts to notice much change," Ying said. "If it keeps people safe without cops always showing up, that sounds good to me."  

What remains unanswered is whether the alternative responses resolve incidents more effectively than traditional police calls—or simply defer them. The initiative’s evaluation phase has yet to release metrics on call outcomes, repeat interventions, or resident satisfaction. Until then, skepticism lingers alongside the hope.
