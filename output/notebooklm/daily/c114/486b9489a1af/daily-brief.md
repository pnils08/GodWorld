#### **West Oakland Additions and Ballpark Proximity**
*By Celeste Tran, Wire Desk*

West Oakland added nineteen residents this cycle while **Vinnie Keane’s** public mentions climbed to six. Outside his apartment building, Athletics starting pitcher **Henry Rivas** watched a moving van unload boxes on the sidewalk, reflecting on what the team represents for a changing neighborhood:
> *“The A’s are a big part of my life, and I plan my days around the games. When I’m not pitching, I’m thinking about the next start or recovering from the last one. I’ve lived in West Oakland for a while now, and I see the neighborhood changing. More people are moving in, and it’s getting livelier. I think the A’s bring a sense of community and pride to the area. I’m proud to be a part of it, and I hope we can keep being a positive presence here.”*

Across town, reactions to the ballclub’s presence reflect differing distances:
*   **Kai Tanaka** (Fruitvale Artist) noted over coffee that the team’s presence feels distant from his daily block: *“I live in Fruitvale, and while I’m aware of the team, it’s not something that’s directly impacted my daily life... It’s not something I engage with regularly.”*
*   **Evan Lewis** (AC Transit Bus Driver), driving his route past the Coliseum, observed: *“I drive past the Coliseum all the time, and you can tell when there’s a game or event going on, but it’s not like they’re out in the community that much. I mean, I’ve seen Arturo Ramos and John Ellis around Laurel, they’re nice guys, but that’s about it.”*
*   In Grand Lake, **Vinnie Keane** fielded two recognitions at the corner grocery store, underscoring how citywide attention drifts to familiar sports figures even as entire blocks absorb new residents.

#### **The Board in the Trailer Says 9.5 Percent. Enzo Walker Is Counting Something Else.**
*By Jordan Velez, Business Desk*

On West Oakland construction sites, company boards show steady expansion, but trade crews doing the heavy lifting tell a different story. At **Coastline Construction**, plumber **Enzo Walker** starts his shift walking past the job-site trailer where the firm lists 221 employees, a 9.5 percent growth rate, and \$187.1 million in revenue—up \$341,000 from the prior cycle.

Walker described the disconnect between corporate numbers and street-level displacement:
> *“Look, I see the numbers on the board in the trailer — 9.5% growth, revenue up, all that. And sure, we’re busy. I’m not complaining about work. But the numbers don’t tell you what it’s like on the ground. The buildings going up, they’re not for us. They’re for the tech folks coming in, the ones from Oakmesh and Civis. I fix pipes in places that didn’t exist last year, and the people who used to live on those blocks? They’re gone. The work’s changing, alright — but it’s changing in a way where I’m not sure the folks signing the checks even notice who’s not at the table anymore. The ledger says growth. The street says something else.”*

Nearby, managing partner **Helena Voss-Adeyemi** leads **Northgate Construction** (95 employees, 8.8% growth, \$78.8M revenue), while **Mariner Construction** carries 165 workers. Alongside plumbers **Hazel Reed**, **Kenton Pearl**, **Maeve Pillai**, and **Yadira Rao**, electricians **Elijah Brown** and **Jude Hess**, and carpenter apprentice **Minh Raimon**, trade crews balance living costs against expanding tech firms like **Oakmesh Systems** (founded by Marcus Tan, up 34.1% to \$9.5M revenue) and **Civis Systems** (\$60.2M revenue).

Coastline managing partner **Desmond Achebe** acknowledged the quiet retention strain:
> *“What keeps me up at night isn’t the growth on the ledger — I can read a spreadsheet. It’s the quiet stuff the numbers don’t show. I walk a different street home sometimes, just to see what’s actually happening on the block, and I see the new folks settling in from Chinatown... But I also see the workers — my plumbers, the electricians, the carpenters at the other shops — and I know a 9% growth number doesn’t tell you whether a guy can afford to stay in the neighborhood he’s helping rebuild. The contraction isn’t loud. It’s the guy who’s been on the crew for twelve years quietly looking at rents across the bridge.”*

**Marcus Tan** of Oakmesh responded to the neighborhood's growth:
> *“Oakmesh is growing, and that growth means we hire more people, we pay more taxes, we take up more space. That’s real. But the neighborhood isn’t a ledger. It’s the people who’ve been here through the ups and downs. What do we owe? We owe it to not forget that. We owe it to hire locally when we can, to be a good neighbor, to not let our success push anyone out.”*

Contractions remained sharpest across town in **Ivy Hill**, where **Ivy Hill Market** lost three employees, alongside staff reductions at Ivy Hill Cafe, Ivy Cleaners, and Ivy Hill Family Dental.

#### **Coliseum Lost 799 Riders on a Weekend With No Event on Its Row**
*By Trevor Shimizu, Civic Desk*

Coliseum Station logged 4,796 passengers in Cycle 114, dropping 799 riders from 5,595 in Cycle 113—the largest drop of any station on the network—even as systemwide ridership rose slightly to 46,839 across eight stations:

*   **Jerald Jensen**, an AC Transit janitor, reads platform activity through the trash cans:
    > *“When the Coliseum empties out like that on a weekend, I see it in the trash cans — fewer cups, fewer wrappers, fewer folks lingering after the last train. The platform records don’t catch the quiet. They don’t catch the feeling that people aren’t just missing a train, they’re missing a reason to be there.”*
*   **Evan Lewis**, driving his AC Transit bus past the station, noted:
    > *“On a weekend, yeah, the whole city thins out a bit — that’s just how it goes. But when you pull up to that station and it’s quieter than it ought to be, even for a Saturday, you start wondering... They don’t tell you about the family that used to catch that train to a game and decided it wasn’t worth the hassle this month. But nobody’s asking the folks who aren’t there anymore why they stopped.”*
*   **Eleanor Rivera**, BART station manager at 12th Street (where boardings rose by 670), explained:
    > *“When a station drops more than the usual weekend pattern, it’s rarely one thing. It’s usually a rhythm stacking up — something at the station, something on the line, something in that neighborhood.”*
*   Meanwhile, **Elena Soria Dominguez** noted that Fruitvale gained 510 riders as station life thrived alongside local food carts and community events.
