# Temescal's 314 Students Stayed Put, and the Table Ranks Them Last

At 3:05 the hallway of Temescal's school empties toward the street. Jun Leu is among the kids who walk home. He is a student there, and he doesn't carry a number with him.

"A typical day is pretty normal, I guess," Jun said. "I walk to school, sit through classes, eat lunch with my friends, try to pay attention when the teacher's talking. Some days are boring, some days are fun. I don't really think about numbers or ratings or whatever. I just know my teachers show up and try, and most kids are just trying to get through the day like anywhere else."

The number is there anyway. In the cycle 115 table, Temescal's school quality index is 6.3. That is the lowest of the city's 22 neighborhoods, a little under the 6.36 it showed a cycle ago. Next up is East Oakland at 6.65. Rockridge sits at the top, above 8.4.

Enrollment is the figure that moved this cycle, and it moved elsewhere. West Oakland gained three students, East Oakland three, Glenview two, Rockridge one and Piedmont Ave one. Chinatown lost two. Temescal's count held at 314.

The rest of Temescal's row reads the same way. About 84 percent of students graduate, and 54 percent are college-ready. Teacher quality is 6.84, and funding is 11,500. Rockridge's row shows 249 students and graduation above 94 percent. It shows 74.53 percent college-ready, teacher quality above 9, and funding of 15,000. Oakland City Schools, the district, has 5,201 employees citywide, and its workforce is growing 7.37 percent.

Patricia Nolan taught for thirty years and lives in Temescal now. She still walks past the building. "I see the same kids I always saw," she said. "The ones who show up early to help the janitor, the ones who pass notes in the back, the ones who ask the question that stops the whole lesson because they actually want to know. The index doesn't see any of that. It sees a number."

She doesn't dismiss the number so much as ask what it leaves out. "I know the difference between a kid who can't learn and a kid who hasn't been given a reason to yet," she said. "That 6.3 — it doesn't tell you about the girl who reads to her little brother every night because their mom works late, or the boy who fixes the classroom door hinge because he notices it's loose. Those kids are there, all 314 of them, and they're not flat numbers. They're just waiting for someone to see them the way I got to see them."

Rick Walker teaches K-8 and used to work a restaurant line. He reads the same table from the staff side. "I see that 6.3 and I think about what it means to teach in a place that isn't growing," he said. "When the district puts money and attention where the numbers are moving, a flat school just... sits there. Those 314 kids and their teachers aren't a statistic to me — they're showing up every day doing the work with less, and nobody's knocking on their door asking what they need."

Jun, asked what he'd want people outside the neighborhood to know, said it plainly. "I wish people outside the neighborhood wouldn't judge our school just by some score. We're not broken or anything. We're just a school. The people here care about us, and we're doing fine."

I checked the city's program registry for anything youth-facing in Temescal. It lists a food pantry at the Temescal Islamic Center and the Reaves Line Transit Fund. It holds no after-school or tutoring program for the neighborhood. That is a gap in the record. It doesn't show that nothing exists.

The table can say what Temescal is and where it ranks. It can't say why the numbers sit where they do. It also can't say whether any of the district's growth is reaching these classrooms. What the next table should show, and what someone should ask the building, is what the school needs and who is coming to ask.
