## Sports

The counter at the back of the room where Trenton Nawan works does not stop moving on a night like this, and neither does Jack London Square — if you can call a neighborhood a single room, which after forty-four years I have learned you cannot, not here, not anywhere a ballclub still owns a piece of the air people breathe. Trenton draws a glass down to the rail, sets it, draws another, and talks the way bartenders have always talked — half to the room, half to no one.

"I've seen a lot of excitement around the A's player features and roster moves during the playoffs," he tells me, not looking up from the tap. "It's all my customers can talk about when I'm serving them. I feel like the team's been making some bold moves, but I'm no expert — I just know it's been fun to watch. I'm hoping they keep it up and make Jack London proud."

Two stools down, Ollie Campbell nods into his glass before he's even asked a question. "I've heard some buzz about the A's making some interesting roster moves during the playoffs," he says. "It's been a topic of conversation around Jack London." Beside him Guadalupe Lupe says almost the same thing in a different key — "I've heard some talk about the A's player features and roster moves during the playoffs, it's been interesting to see how it's all playing out" — and there is something in the way both of them say *I've heard* rather than *I know* that tells you more about this cycle than any line card could. The city is listening to itself talk about this team before it fully understands what it is listening to.

Even Martin Richards, a third baseman wearing a Mets uniform three thousand miles from this counter, got cornered on the question when a reporter caught him after his own game. He gave the honest answer a visiting man gives. "To be honest, I've been so focused on my own game and the Mets' season that I haven't had much time to think about the A's player features and roster moves during the playoffs," he said. "But from what I've seen, it seems like they've made some bold moves that have paid off for them." Paid off is a modest word for what the record says. One hundred twenty-seven wins. Thirty-five losses. No streak running under either column tonight — the ledger simply sits there, settled, like a man who has stopped needing to prove the point.

I do not traffic in grades. A number like that is not a grade. It is a door, and on the other side of it is every other number I have ever had to hold up to history and found wanting by comparison, or — rarer, and better — found history wanting by comparison to it. There was a Philadelphia club once, built by a man named Connie Mack, that strung together consecutive pennants on the strength of a lineup so complete that opposing dugouts spoke of it the way Ollie Campbell speaks of buzz — secondhand, reverent, slightly afraid. That club is not this club. The uniform crossed a continent and a century to get here, and nothing about 127 and 35 belongs to Connie Mack any more than it belongs to me. But the ratio is the same shape of thing he used to build: a team playing so far above the room's expectation that the room stops arguing about it and starts just repeating the fact to each other, the way Trenton's customers do, the way Guadalupe does, the way the wire pulled Martin Richards into saying something kind about an opponent he hadn't had time to watch.

What none of them can tell me — what I cannot tell you — is what the number means once the playoffs are finished and the counter goes back to being just a counter. Thirty-five losses is not nothing. A hundred twenty-seven wins is not permanent. I have covered enough seasons to know that pride like this is loaned, not owned, and the bill always comes due in October's last week, one way or the other. Tonight it is simply true, and the room believes it, and for a city that spends most years arguing about everything, believing the same thing together — even for one cycle, even secondhand — is its own kind of quiet pride.
