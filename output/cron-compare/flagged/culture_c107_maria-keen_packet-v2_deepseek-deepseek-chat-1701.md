The sound of basketballs echoes through the cracked pavement of the Eastside Hoopers Coalition court, where Marcus Whitfield oversees a dozen kids in mismatched jerseys. Whitfield founded this program back in Cycle 101, a response to what he saw as a lack of safe spaces for East Oakland’s youth. "I’ve seen how community programs can really bring people together and make a difference in their lives," he says, wiping sweat from his brow as the kids scramble for rebounds. "As someone who's founded a program myself, I know how much work and dedication goes into making it happen. It's not just about the program itself, but about the people behind it and the impact it has on the community."

Down the block, Allen Temple Baptist Church’s food pantry hums with activity. Seniors shuffle in with reusable bags, greeted by volunteers stacking canned goods and fresh produce. The pantry has been a cornerstone of East Oakland since Cycle 78, a quiet testament to the neighborhood’s resilience. Nearby, the Masjid Al-Islam Emergency Assistance Fund operates in a similar rhythm, distributing aid to those in need. "I’ve seen that community programs in East Oakland are really making a difference," says Maria Conteras, a regular at both sites. "It’s great to see people like Marcus Whitfield founding initiatives that help our neighborhood."

Jerome Pittman leans against the chain-link fence outside the Hoopers Coalition court, watching Whitfield demonstrate a layup. "I feel like community programs and their founders in East Oakland are really making a difference," he says. "I see the impact they have on people's lives around here."

Still, as the kids dribble down the court and the pantry hands out its last bag of rice, a question lingers: how do these programs reflect the values and priorities of the people who founded them?
