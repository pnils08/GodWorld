# Three rooms, no single crisis: what Oakland's congregations are holding this cycle

Three congregations logged crisis responses this cycle. Adams Point United Methodist activated an emergency assistance fund. Temple Beth Or held a vigil. Masjid An-Nur Oakland organized a community grief gathering.

I went looking for the event behind them and didn't find one. Brenda Okoro, the Deputy Mayor for community development, told me so plainly.

"There's no single event on my record that explains all three, and I won't manufacture one to make a tidier story," she said. "What the ledger shows is three congregations reading three different needs in their own rooms and responding in the way their tradition tells them to."

So this is a story about three different kinds of weight, and about the people carrying each.

## The fund

At Adams Point United Methodist, the Rev. Daniel Han described a fund that opens when people run out of room. "When people come to us for help, they often ask for assistance with food, rent, and utilities," he said. "It's heartbreaking to see the impact of financial strain on our neighbors, but it's also inspiring to see the community come together to support one another."

The ledger records 50 people at the church this cycle. It does not record how many households the fund reached or how much it paid out. Han didn't give figures and I haven't seen any, so I won't guess. That is the first thing I'll ask him next.

The city's numbers sit oddly beside his. Okoro says housing pressure in Adams Point reads zero this cycle, with median rent at $2,361 against a median income of $94,430. On paper it is not a rent crisis. But the neighborhood has the only negative sentiment on her map, and migration is negative.

"Adams Point's problem isn't that rent is chasing people out," she said. "It's that the neighborhood has gone quiet. When a block empties out slowly, the church and the masjid and the synagogue are the first to notice, because they're the ones counting chairs." She added: "A zero on housing pressure is not a zero on need."

Both things can be true. A median describes the middle of a neighborhood, and a fund like Han's exists for the people below it.

## The vigil

At Temple Beth Or in Rockridge, 91 people came to a vigil held for community healing. Rabbi Naomi Sterling said the room was mixed: "members of our congregation, neighbors, and people from other faith communities."

"It's hard to put into words, but there was a sense of shared grief and a desire to support one another," she said.

She did not say what the grief was for, and the ledger entry doesn't either. I didn't press it, because a vigil doesn't always need a named cause to be real. I'm also not going to supply one.

Sterling made a point of separating the vigil from the congregation's food pantry. The pantry served neighbors last cycle, with 89 people through the door, and she calls it "a separate but related effort." She sees both as one commitment: "caring for one another."

## The gathering

The one cause I can name belongs to Masjid An-Nur Oakland in East Oakland. Imam Aziz Rahimi said the community grief gathering, attended by 56 people, was about Mark Aitken signing with the Rockies. The A's coverage feed confirms Aitken signed with Colorado this cycle.

Some readers will find it strange that a mosque held a grief gathering over a first baseman's contract. Rahimi anticipated that.

"He's more than just a baseball player to us; he's a neighbor, a friend, and a voice for our community," he said. "This isn't just about baseball; it's about the impact one person can have on a neighborhood."

Aitken once told this paper that "people think compassion is soft. It isn't. It's architecture." If that's how he built it, then a congregation grieving when the builder leaves is not an overreaction. Rahimi's request was specific: "We need their support and presence as we continue to uplift the values Mark represented." That sounds like someone asking his neighbors to keep a structure standing after its builder has moved on.

## The ordinary work goes on

None of this displaced the regular work. New Covenant Pentecostal Assembly in West Oakland ran its food pantry this cycle, with 129 people. Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship hosted a community health fair, with 50. The pantries, lunches and workshops that fill the ledger kept running while three congregations also made room for something heavier.

## What I don't know

I don't know what the Adams Point fund has paid out, or whether the quiet Okoro describes is what's driving the requests. I don't know what Temple Beth Or's vigil was for. I don't know whether Aitken is aware that a mosque in East Oakland held a gathering over his departure. I'll be asking.

Okoro said her office calls a congregation when it logs a crisis response, to find out what it's seeing and whether a city resource fits. She believes "faith communities see the need before the numbers do." That's her claim, and I'll hold it up against the numbers in the next few cycles.
