Nine cycles. That's the number Trevor Shimizu and Luis Navarro keep returning to, and it keeps refusing to resolve into anything comfortable. The Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II has not moved in nine cycles — not through a funding fight, not through a design dispute, not through any of the friction that at least explains a delay. It has stalled in the visioning phase, which is the phase before the hard work even starts. Tomas Renteria, who cooks on the line and hosts a podcast and rides these streets, put it to Shimizu as plainly as it can be put: "I feel like the visioning part should be the easiest — why's it stuck there?" Calvin Turner, a mechanic and Fruitvale resident, told Navarro the stall reads to him as a statement about whose neighborhoods get urgency and whose get process. Neither of them is wrong to ask. What neither Shimizu nor Navarro has been able to return with — and I want to be precise here, because the absence of an answer is not the same as an absence of one — is any record from a responsible office explaining what the actual obstacle is. That's the question sitting on my desk going into next week, and it doesn't get smaller.

Meanwhile, the city opened this cycle in winter conditions — 49 degrees, northwest wind, overcast — and Noah Tan's brief on the weather is the kind of deceptively thin entry that carries more than it seems. A city that cold at the start of a cycle is a city where outdoor workers feel it in their hands, where foot traffic migrates or disappears, where the gap between neighborhoods with warm commercial corridors and those without becomes physical. Which is one way to read what Jordan Velez has been watching in Dimond. Foot traffic down. Storefronts quieter than the rest of the city. Velez has the condition on record but not yet the cause — whether this is seasonal, structural, a rent story, a transit story, or something the numbers won't name until someone goes and talks to the people locking up early. I'd like to know before we write Dimond off as simply cooling.

The Oakland Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline announced its pilot this cycle, and Angela Reyes flagged it for us. I want to say clearly: an announcement is not a program, and a pilot is not a promise. Reyes identified the question correctly — there's a human story here that needs a clear and careful account, and we don't have it yet. Who are the young people in the pilot? What are they learning, and toward what kind of work? The city has a way of announcing these things and letting the announcement do the work of the follow-through. We're not going to let it.

On the other side of this week, the A's are doing something that barely has a grammar for it. Simon Leary has the record: 124 wins, 34 losses, fifteen straight. Those numbers are not a story by themselves — the story is what it feels like inside a season that historic, which is a kind of pressure that looks like joy from the outside and is probably more complicated from the dugout. Benji Dillon is moving to the bullpen for the rest of the season, and Leary got him on record saying it plainly: "doing whatever it takes to help this team finish strong and make history together." That's a professional answer. The question Leary hasn't cracked yet is the one underneath it — what does a pitcher actually give up when he makes that move, and who asked him to, and how long did that conversation take? A man can believe what he says and still have given something up to say it. That's the texture Leary needs to go get.

Tanya Cruz picked up the Oaks preseason, and the ledger reads 0 and 1, which means nothing yet except that Adash Stanley is worth watching. Twenty-three points and seven assists in a loss is a particular kind of performance — generous, visible, not enough. Cruz's open question is the right one: what does this team look like when the games count?

Maria Keen was at Nightline Station in West Oakland, and what she found was quiet. Not closed, not troubled — just quiet. Mason Ortega was on Piedmont Avenue, where Piedmont Heights is open for evening business. Taken together, those two dispatches are the city at night in miniature: one corridor humming, one going still, and no clean explanation for why. That asymmetry is worth sitting with.

What I'm watching next week: I want Shimizu or Navarro — or both — inside a city office with a specific question about what it would take to move Fruitvale Phase II out of visioning and into anything else. I want Velez talking to the people behind those quieter Dimond storefronts before we decide what the story is. I want Reyes with a young person who is actually inside the apprenticeship pilot, not a spokesperson for it. And I want Leary in a real conversation with Dillon — not the postgame version, the other one.

The cold opened this cycle and it hasn't lifted. The city is moving in ways that don't always add up to a coherent direction. That's when the job matters most.
