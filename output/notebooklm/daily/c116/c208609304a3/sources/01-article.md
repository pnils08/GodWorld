# The Other Side of the Fence

By Ines Sorensen, The Bay Tribune

"I walk past that fence every day. The development's not for me — it's happening around me. I work at the port, I've got three kids, and I'm watching rents climb while the people I work with talk about leaving."

That is Tre Mingo, a port worker who lives in Uptown. He gave the line to a reporter this cycle, and it came back on two separate pages. The paper hasn't carried him before, so I'm not going to tidy him. His page doesn't say what the fence encloses or who is building behind it. He says only that he passes it every day, and that is where he starts.

Here is the rest of what he said on his Monday page, in the order he said it:

"The businesses are growing, the numbers look good, but the folks on the ledger aren't the ones who get to stay. I don't feel like I'm part of it. I feel like I'm watching it from the other side of the fence."

I have spent a lot of years listening for the end of the sentence, where people say the part they didn't plan. His first lines are about the fence, the port, and the three kids. The last one is the one that stays: *I don't feel like I'm part of it.* He doesn't ask anyone for anything. He tells you where he stands.

He also left a question with a reporter, the kind a person carries around after the interview is over:

"Will any of us who work here still be able to stay, or is this development happening without us?"

---

Two other pages this cycle were written with Tre Mingo's name in mind, or close to it.

Guadalupe Lupe tends bar in Jack London. Her page gives no name for a regular, a street or a last call. It gives one question, and she names him in it:

"How much longer can people like Tre Mingo afford to stay here before rent pushes them out?"

Marguerite Salcedo is managing partner at Anchor Build and lives on Piedmont Ave. She sits on the other side of that same fence, and her page doesn't pretend otherwise. Her question is this:

"What do I do about the crews who can't afford the units we're building?"

I didn't put those two lines next to each other to make a point, and I won't explain either one. Lupe is asking about a man. Salcedo is asking about crews. Neither page has an answer after it.

---

Tre Mingo's page ran a second time midweek, cut shorter. It stops where the rents are climbing and the people he works with talk about leaving. I read it twice. The shorter version is truer to how people talk on a morning like this one, when the trade of the day is over and nobody has said the last thing yet.

The last thing he did say, to a reporter, on the page the city now has:

"I feel like I'm watching it from the other side of the fence."
