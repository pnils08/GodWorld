Two doors down from a pole stapled thick with block-party flyers, there's a newspaper clipping taped flat to a building's front door with four strips of clear tape. Kai Marston found it this week. The headline is about Benji Dillon, who lives in the building. A neighbor put it up, and Benji told Kai he was surprised, a little surreal about it, and trying to stay grounded. He didn't pull it down. He had his grocery bag in one hand and his keys in the other, and he looked like a man who'd been stopped four times that week and had decided to be gracious.

Now put that beside what Tanya Cruz heard in the clubhouse. Vinnie Keane made his retirement formal, and Benji, two lockers down, still in his road jacket, said he wasn't at the press conference. He was home, and Rick asked why he was quiet at dinner. He said the passing of the old guard isn't the speeches, it's the silence after, and that no one is left who remembers the bad years the way he and Vinnie do.

That is one man in two rooms. On Rockridge's sidewalk he's the rising name, the icon Anthony Raines says the neighborhood "loves, and he loves it back." In the clubhouse he's a veteran going quiet over dinner because the one person who shared his memory is leaving. The clipping on the door says the city has claimed him. The silence at the table says he knows what that claim costs. Vinnie's own words to Tanya fit the pairing: the voice people talk about "was never just mine," and the clubhouse sees itself "hungry, young, and ready to prove something." Taveras and Quintero get the runway, as Mike Paulson put it, standing in the office doorway looking at the empty chair by the tunnel. A neighborhood tapes up its pitcher's name while the clubhouse works out how to carry a retired hitter's. The city is good at celebrating the person in front of it and slow to ask what the person behind him is carrying.

Names travelling ahead of people was the pattern this week. Tamsin Pruitt's pages on the household ledger gave us Lena Marchetti, a crisis clinician who moved into a rental in East Oakland. Three strangers were asked about her. Dominic Okeke-Brandt in Laurel, Kai Davis in West Oakland and bus driver Evan Lewis all said they'd heard the name and knew nothing else. Evan, to his credit, went straight to the A's trade news and the way people are keeping to themselves lately. I read that as a city a little braced, not a city indifferent. Tamsin's page on Maurice Franklin carries the other side of it. The paper has put Franklin in Rockridge as a retired transit supervisor, and the ledger now has him as a drone fleet coordinator born in 1978. The record doesn't reconcile the two, and I won't pretend to. A name can be on a door or in a ledger and still not be a person you know.

That gap, between a record that names and a record that explains, runs through the civic desk too. Carmen Delaine laid out the West Oakland Stabilization Fund: about a quarter of it is out the door, the renewal ordinance is with the clerk, and the council takes it up at Cycle 116. Marcus Webb and Deputy Mayor Brenda Okoro gave nearly identical sentences, that the closeout is published, the standard holds at 23 days, and renewal is the council's call. Two offices, one answer. Neither said who got the money. The tracker names two households in a single tranche and leaves most of that tranche unexplained. The Fund is meant to serve West Oakland businesses and residents, and nobody has told us which. I'd like that answered before anyone votes.

Luis Navarro found the same silence in the Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline. The Pipeline is a $12.5 million promise to West Oakland, East Oakland and Fruitvale kids, and it's been three cycles without a move. The budget fields read full, the last-disbursement field is blank, and Luis was careful to say that's a field on a sheet, not proof no money moved. The placement-gap analysis is due at Cycle 116. A kid in Fruitvale who wants a trade can look up the program, and for now that is about all the record lets him do.

Jax Caldera, on Telegraph, ran into a different kind of contradiction in KONO. Priya Raghunathan, who dispatches rail for BART, and Dmitri Soto, who runs a container yard, both said the neighborhood is busy, retail is strong and new households are moving in. The map agrees. The city's audit flags decay anyway, on sentiment and housing pressure, and Jax's point is that nobody on the initiative list owns it. A street can be loud, full and bleeding at once. I won't call this a fight between the street and the audit, because they may both be right. The unanswered part is who is supposed to be watching the rent.

Trevor Shimizu's ridership reporting is the week's one clean surge. Every station on the sheet rose, and 19th Street rose most, with on-time performance holding. Coen Cartier saw more people at 19th than he'd expect on a Wednesday afternoon, heading toward Lake Merritt and downtown. BART's Ximena Mehta called it a stacking of little things, a game downtown, a council vote, Baylight. Trevor is right that the rows don't pick a cause. I'll add that it's the same city that's keeping to itself and also, apparently, all on the platform at once.

Nia Rook's UNDOCKED board, for the record, went quiet in the best way. Three pilots flew, three ended even, and Luis Taz kept the lead without sitting in the chair. Elijah Brown said he didn't really have a strategy. Brooklyn Nguyen mined her ore and it didn't turn into profit. That's honest, and I find it comforting.

Next week is Cycle 116, and I'm watching for four things. Whether the council votes on the Stabilization Fund renewal, and whether anyone says out loud who the money reached. Whether the Pipeline's gap analysis and adjustment plan actually publish, and what they say about West Oakland and Fruitvale. Whether KONO gets a name on the list of things the city is working on. And I'm watching the clubhouse: how Taveras and Quintero fill the space Vinnie leaves, and whether Benji, who has the clipping on his door and the silence at his table, finds a way to carry both. If the paper taped up on his building is any guide, a lot of us will be reading.
