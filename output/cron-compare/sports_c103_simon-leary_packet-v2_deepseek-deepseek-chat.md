# The Weight of 124 Wins

Vanessa Ramas doesn’t follow baseball too closely, but even she knows the A’s are having an amazing season. Standing at her favorite coffee spot near Lake Merritt, she sips her latte and shrugs. “Moving Benji Dillon to the bullpen now feels like fixing something that isn’t broken,” she says. “Why mess with what’s working? The Tribune should ask if there’s more to this move than just strategy.”

The A’s record sits at 124-34, a number so staggering it feels less like a statistic and more like a building’s foundation. Fifteen straight wins, a pace unheard of in recent memory—most teams would let the machine run itself. But the A’s aren’t most teams, and Benji Dillon isn’t most players. Moving him to the bullpen is less a decision and more an adjustment, a slight shift in the load-bearing structure of the roster.

It’s easy to see why Ramas is puzzled. The A’s are a team so dominant that every move feels magnified, every change scrutinized. But timing, not the move itself, is what raises eyebrows. Why now, at 124 wins? Why tinker with a pitcher who has been part of this extraordinary run? The answers aren’t in the clubhouse or the strategy room—they’re in the weight of the record itself. 

A 124-34 season doesn’t just demand excellence; it demands maintenance. The bullpen isn’t a demotion—it’s reinforcement, an acknowledgment that even the strongest structures need careful tending. Dillon’s role change isn’t about fixing what’s broken; it’s about keeping the whole thing upright. 

But questions linger. What unseen pressures does a record like this place on its parts? And who, exactly, is tasked with answering them?
