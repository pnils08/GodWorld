# Three Programs on a Block That Keeps Counting Itself

The Postpartum Oakland Network sits in Fruitvale, and Lucia Polito founded it. It is one of three programs the record holds for the neighborhood. The other two are the Fruitvale Summer Speed Series, a youth-sports outfit, and Fruitvale Transit Corridor Mutual Aid. All three have been on the books since cycle 101. That is not long, and a block can tell the difference between a program and a program that has stayed.

Polito is listed as an Aura Wellness Practitioner. When I asked what the network has meant, she did not give me a pitch. "I've seen the impact that community programs like the Postpartum Oakland Network, which I founded, can have on people's lives," she said. "It's amazing to see the difference these programs make in Fruitvale. As someone who's dedicated to wellness and has been a part of this community for a while, I feel grateful to see the positive changes happening around us."

I believe the gratitude. I also notice that she is the one holding it. A founder sees the room from the front, where the work is.

Rick Walker sees it from somewhere else. He teaches K-8 and used to work the line in a kitchen, so he knows what it is to be on your feet for other people's comfort. Asked whether the changes were landing, he took longer to answer.

"To be honest, it's getting harder to feel that way," Walker said. "Fruitvale's still got its issues, but I've seen some new posters around, like the expanded tutoring program at St. Esperanza Parish. It's something, you know? The mercado's still buzzing, people are still grabbing lunch between train transfers. It's not like nothing's happening. But, yeah, the rent went up again, so it's still a struggle. I don't know if it's enough, though."

That is the block in one breath. There is a poster on a wall, a mercado with its lunch rush, and people changing trains. Under all of it sits a rent number that does not care how many posters went up. Walker also said the teachers elsewhere work just as hard as the ones in Fruitvale, "but they're doing it with less." He never says who holds the less. He doesn't have to.

Gregory Mims is blunter about how long good news lasts. "Fruitvale's still a ways off for a lot of folks around here, you know?" he said. "I mean, that kid's cousin getting placed with a plumbing crew is great and all, but it's not like that's the norm. I've seen too many programs come and go. You hear about success stories, but they're not always the ones that stick. I'm still a little skeptical."

A plumbing crew is a real job, and I won't pretend it isn't. But Mims is saying one cousin is a story and a norm is a pattern. The record cannot yet tell us which this is.

Council President Ramon Vega carries a different kind of doubt. He is still turning over the fight on the Fruitvale hub. "I understand why Christopher felt I was leaning too far into the developer's corner on the Fruitvale hub," Vega said. "At the time, I believed it was a necessary compromise to move the project forward, but looking back, I can see how it might have appeared otherwise. The Fruitvale community programs are crucial, and I still stand by the importance of supporting them. However, I acknowledge that the approach we took was contentious, and it's something I've reflected on since then."

He says he stands by the programs, and he admits the road there was contentious. I'd like to hear what supporting them looks like once the reflecting is done.

Now the counting. This cycle Fruitvale holds 423 students, 1,559 adults and 351 seniors. Against cycle 112 that is two more students, seven more adults and one more senior. Ten people is barely a shift. It is the kind of movement a block feels as one new name on a mailbox, or one gone. The hospital census for Fruitvale shows 114 sick residents, two admissions, and four people in care at the close, in four occupied beds. The judicial census shows six charges, one arrest, and three people in custody. None of those people are tracked individually, so I can't tell you who they are. I can tell you the block has them. Someone on this block has a bed they aren't in and a chair they aren't sitting in.

Here is where the three programs have to prove their worth. A postpartum network, a youth speed series and a mutual-aid group working the transit corridor have nothing to do with a ledger. They are about who comes by, who carries the groceries and who keeps a kid running. Whether they reach the four beds or the three in custody, I do not know. Whether they reach the person paying the new rent, I know even less.

Polito is grateful, Walker is tired, Mims is skeptical and Vega is reflective. Each of them is honest about it. What I still need is the person none of them stands in for. I want one parent, one runner or one rider on the corridor who can say what any of the three programs changed on their own block, and whether it was enough. Walker said he didn't know. I'd like to hear someone who does.
