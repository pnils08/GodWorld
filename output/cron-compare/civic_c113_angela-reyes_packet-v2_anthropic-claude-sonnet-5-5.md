# In Temescal, a school the district's growth passed by

Patricia Nolan stands where she has stood for thirty years, across from the school building, and watches the doors. She taught for a living. She is retired now, and she still watches the building.

"I've watched that building for thirty years," she says, "and the thing that breaks my heart isn't the number on the table. It's that the kids inside feel it."

The number is 6.43. It is Temescal's school quality index on the district table, the lowest of the 22 neighborhoods ranked this cycle. It sits just under East Oakland at 6.73 and San Antonio at 6.76. At the top is Rockridge at 8.58. West Oakland, which gained three students this cycle, sits at 8.12.

The table is steady from cycle to cycle, and Temescal's row barely moved. It read 6.51 at the last count. Rockridge also slipped a notch, from 8.63. The enrollment column is the one that moves, and it moved elsewhere. Against last cycle, West Oakland is up three students. Fruitvale, Chinatown, Glenview and East Oakland are each up two. Piedmont Ave is up one. Temescal is not on that list. Its row holds at 313 students.

## What the row says

Temescal's graduation rate is just under 85 percent. Its college-readiness rate is 55 percent. Its teacher-quality figure is 6.88, and its funding line is 11,500.

Rockridge's row reads differently. Its graduation rate is about 94 percent and its college readiness is about 76. Its teacher quality is 9.09 and its funding is 15,000. Rockridge holds 247 students, fewer than Temescal's 313. The per-neighborhood funding on the table adds up to 286,000. Oakland City Schools, with 5,201 employees citywide and growth of 7.94 percent, is the district around all of it.

I also checked the program registry for Temescal. It lists a food pantry run out of the Islamic Center and a transit fund. It lists no school or after-school program for the neighborhood. That does not mean none exists. It means the registry doesn't hold one, so I can't describe one.

## What the people say

Nolan has her own reading of the row. "When I was teaching, we had the same struggles, but there was a sense that the neighborhood was pulling for us," she says. "Now the district grows everywhere else, and Temescal just... sits. The teachers there are still good people, still trying. But you can't pour from an empty cup, and when the funding and attention go to the neighborhoods that are already climbing, the kids here notice. They're not dumb. They know when they're being left behind."

Rick Walker teaches K-8 in Fruitvale, where the table shows two more students this cycle. He was a line cook before he was a teacher, and he talks about the work the way a cook would.

"I see it in my own classroom every day," he says. "When the district grows, the money and the attention follow the growth. Temescal isn't growing, so it's just... flat. The teachers there are working just as hard as we are in Fruitvale, but they're doing it with less. That gap isn't just numbers on a table. It's the difference between a kid getting a new textbook or a worn-out one from three years ago. I've been in that position, and it wears you down after a while."

Salma Navarro is a student in Temescal. The index is not what she and her friends talk about.

"I mean, we talk about it, but not in numbers like that," she says. "Mostly it's just, like, what are we actually gonna do next year? Some of my friends are looking at jobs, some are trying to get into the community college, and a few are just, you know, figuring it out. Nobody's really bragging about stats or anything. It's more like, are we gonna be okay? That's what comes up when we talk."

A retired teacher reads the building through three decades of memory. A Fruitvale teacher reads it through textbooks. A student reads it through the next year. None of them talks about it the way the table does.

## What is still missing

The table can't say whether Temescal's school is failing the neighborhood or the neighborhood is quietly failing the school. This cycle's records don't show it either way. The table gives no class sizes, textbook counts or staffing for the Temescal building. It records no families moving between neighborhoods, and the registry names no school program there. Until a row holds those, the 6.43 stays a number nobody can fully explain. The next record to find is what that 11,500 buys, inside the building Nolan is watching.
