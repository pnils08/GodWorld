# Six in Beds, One Hundred Thirty-Four at Home: Chinatown's Sick Residents and the People Who Carry Them

*By Dr. Lila Mezran, Bay Tribune Health*

Marisio Curazo keeps her lists in a spiral notebook with a cracked cover, and she writes the way she talks, fast and in pencil. On a weekday afternoon in Chinatown she is working through the names that belong on the home-visit column. Who needs a home health aide. Whose prescriptions have to be delivered. Whose door just needs a knock and a check-in. She is an Elder Care Coordinator, and she says the notebook is the only honest count she has.

The official count for the neighborhood this cycle is 140 sick residents, out of 2,201 people living in Chinatown. That is 6.4 percent of the neighborhood, at or over the 6 percent watch bar. The city's own record shows Chinatown carrying the largest number of sick residents of any neighborhood in the city this cycle, and a low-severity health spike is logged here at an impact score of 16. Hospital care in Chinatown is smaller. The census records 3 admissions this cycle and 6 residents in care at the close, occupying 6 beds.

"When I see a number like 140 sick in Chinatown, I don't see a number — I see the families I work with every day," Curazo says. She taps the notebook. "Most of them aren't going to the hospital. They're staying home because that's what we do. The daughter takes the bus from Fruitvale to cook. The nephew calls in late to pick up prescriptions. I'm the one who helps figure out who needs a home health aide, who needs their meds delivered, who just needs someone to check in. The hospital has six of our folks right now — the ones who really couldn't stay home. The rest of us are doing what we've always done: taking care of each other, block by block, door by door."

Across the city, the hospital record is one line in a longer table. The city summary shows 35 admissions this cycle and 71 residents in care at the close, with 71 beds occupied. Chinatown's share of those beds is six. The rest of the 140 sick residents in the neighborhood are not in the hospital census, and the census does not say where they are instead.

Omarion Farah knows the neighborhood from the back seat of a cab, which is where a lot of Chinatown talk goes on. He drives the stretch between the stand and the apartments above the shops, and he says people rarely bring up hospitals in his car.

"People talk in the cab, but not about hospitals much," Farah says. He keeps one hand on the wheel at the light. "They're more worried about who's going to cover a shift or pick up the kids. I see folks staying home, taking care of each other. The ones I hear about going to the hospital are the ones who really can't avoid it. It's a lot of home care, a lot of family stepping in."

Those are two Chinatown voices, one from a desk and one from a dashboard, and both describe the same arrangement: a household absorbs the illness, and the hospital takes only the cases the household cannot. That arrangement is plausible from the neighborhood's own accounts. The ledger does not measure it. Nothing on the record says how many of the 134 residents who are sick and not in a bed are being cared for at home, by whom, or for how long.

Yu Zen, who is in hospital care this cycle after a workplace accident, has spent his working life with claims forms and policy paperwork. He puts the gap in terms the forms do not capture.

"When I was in that hospital bed after my accident, I kept thinking about the numbers I deal with every day as an insurance broker — the claims, the paperwork, the names on the forms," Zen says. "But lying there, I realized the names on the forms aren't the same as the names on the beds. Chinatown's been hit so hard, and I know it's not because those folks are any less careful or any less strong. It's that when illness clusters, it lands on households where someone's already stretched thin — caring for a parent, watching the grandkids, working two jobs. The six of us in hospital care, we've got nurses and monitors and someone bringing us meals. The other 134 — they've got a cousin or an auntie or a neighbor who's doing all that and still trying to keep the shop open. That's who's caring for them. And that's the part that keeps me up at night, because I know that kind of care doesn't show up on any ledger."

Zen is not describing a diagnosis or a clinical outcome for anyone. He is describing where the care sits. Put his account next to Curazo's and Farah's and the picture is consistent: the 6 in hospital care are the people the household could not manage, and the rest are being managed by people who are also working.

That is an interpretation, and it should be read as one. The record does not yet show what any household is doing day to day, whether the caregivers are getting help, or what happens when a shift cannot be covered. The three Chinatown admissions this cycle are also on the census without a discharge record. Whether those residents went home, moved to follow-up care, or are still in a bed is not in the packet.

Down the block, the shops keep their hours, and the people who run them keep their own counts. Curazo closes the notebook and heads for her next door. "The hospital counts the ones it has to take," she says. "We're counting the rest, and nobody's paying us to do it."

The next record the neighborhood needs is simple to name and not yet on file: discharge data for Chinatown's 3 admissions this cycle, and a record of any follow-up care the 6 residents in beds receive once they leave the hospital. Until those rows exist, the 134 people who are sick and not in a bed will stay where Zen and Curazo say they are, which is at home, with someone else holding the shift.
