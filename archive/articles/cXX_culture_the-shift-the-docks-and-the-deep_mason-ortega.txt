# The Shift, The Docks, and the Deep
**By Mason Ortega | Bay Tribune Culture**

I spent three shifts shadowing the kitchen crew at the Lake Chalet during the post-holiday rush. When you work a kitchen that pushes right up against Lake Merritt, the geography changes the rhythm. Most lines are closed boxes of heat and steel. Here, there are the fryers, and then there are the docks. 

What I found wasn't about the ticket times. It was about what happens when the tickets stop. 

Julian, 34, works the grill. Twelve years in Oakland kitchens, the last four on this specific line. At 11:15 p.m., after the final plates are expedited and the flat-top is scraped down, he wipes his hands on a towel and steps out the back door onto the wooden docks. The air off the lake is sharp. 

"You have to look right at the boundary line," he tells me, lighting a cigarette and pointing out past the gondolas toward the darker water. "Where the light from the streetlamps stops." 

He's talking about the Oak-ness Monster. 

If you spend enough time in Oakland, you hear the stories. Since the 1940s, the myth of a massive, Loch Ness-style creature with spikes, humps, and a long tongue living in the tidal lagoon has been passed around barstools and bus stops. 

Julian isn't joking. He's been working late shifts on the lake long enough that the myth has become part of his muscle memory. "I saw the humps break the surface twice in 2022," he says, taking a drag. "Once during a Tuesday prep shift, once during a wedding buyout. You don't tell the dining room. You just watch." 

Another line cook, a twenty-two-year-old on the salad station, steps out onto the dock. He doesn't look at the water. He looks at his phone. 

"The new kids don't look," Julian says softly, tapping his ash. "They finish the shift and they want to go home. But you stand out here long enough, listening to the city hum, you realize the lake is older than all of it. The monster isn't a ghost story. It's just a reminder that we don't own the water. We just cook next to it."

The kitchen door opens behind us. The dish pit needs clearing. Julian drops his cigarette, crushes it under his boot, and takes one last look at the dark ripples before turning back to the heat. 

The shift ends. The lake stays.
