THE CYCLE PULSE — Y3C11
The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

Two doors down from a pole stapled thick with block-party flyers, there's a newspaper clipping taped flat to a building's front door with four strips of clear tape. Kai Marston found it this week. The headline is about Benji Dillon, who lives in the building. A neighbor put it up, and Benji told Kai he was surprised, a little surreal about it, and trying to stay grounded. He didn't pull it down. He had his grocery bag in one hand and his keys in the other, and he looked like a man who'd been stopped four times that week and had decided to be gracious.

Now put that beside what Tanya Cruz heard in the clubhouse. Vinnie Keane made his retirement formal, and Benji, two lockers down, still in his road jacket, said he wasn't at the press conference. He was home, and Rick asked why he was quiet at dinner. He said the passing of the old guard isn't the speeches, it's the silence after, and that no one is left who remembers the bad years the way he and Vinnie do.

That is one man in two rooms. On Rockridge's sidewalk he's the rising name, the icon Anthony Raines says the neighborhood "loves, and he loves it back." In the clubhouse he's a veteran going quiet over dinner because the one person who shared his memory is leaving. The clipping on the door says the city has claimed him. The silence at the table says he knows what that claim costs. Vinnie's own words to Tanya fit the pairing: the voice people talk about "was never just mine," and the clubhouse sees itself "hungry, young, and ready to prove something." Taveras and Quintero get the runway, as Mike Paulson put it, standing in the office doorway looking at the empty chair by the tunnel. A neighborhood tapes up its pitcher's name while the clubhouse works out how to carry a retired hitter's. The city is good at celebrating the person in front of it and slow to ask what the person behind him is carrying.

Names travelling ahead of people was the pattern this week. Tamsin Pruitt's pages on the household ledger gave us Lena Marchetti, a crisis clinician who moved into a rental in East Oakland. Three strangers were asked about her. Dominic Okeke-Brandt in Laurel, Kai Davis in West Oakland and bus driver Evan Lewis all said they'd heard the name and knew nothing else. Evan, to his credit, went straight to the A's trade news and the way people are keeping to themselves lately. I read that as a city a little braced, not a city indifferent. Tamsin's page on Maurice Franklin carries the other side of it. The paper has put Franklin in Rockridge as a retired transit supervisor, and the ledger now has him as a drone fleet coordinator born in 1978. The record doesn't reconcile the two, and I won't pretend to. A name can be on a door or in a ledger and still not be a person you know.

That gap, between a record that names and a record that explains, runs through the civic desk too. Carmen Delaine laid out the West Oakland Stabilization Fund: about a quarter of it is out the door, the renewal ordinance is with the clerk, and the council takes it up at Cycle 116. Marcus Webb and Deputy Mayor Brenda Okoro gave nearly identical sentences, that the closeout is published, the standard holds at 23 days, and renewal is the council's call. Two offices, one answer. Neither said who got the money. The tracker names two households in a single tranche and leaves most of that tranche unexplained. The Fund is meant to serve West Oakland businesses and residents, and nobody has told us which. I'd like that answered before anyone votes.

Luis Navarro found the same silence in the Youth Apprenticeship Pipeline. The Pipeline is a $12.5 million promise to West Oakland, East Oakland and Fruitvale kids, and it's been three cycles without a move. The budget fields read full, the last-disbursement field is blank, and Luis was careful to say that's a field on a sheet, not proof no money moved. The placement-gap analysis is due at Cycle 116. A kid in Fruitvale who wants a trade can look up the program, and for now that is about all the record lets him do.

Jax Caldera, on Telegraph, ran into a different kind of contradiction in KONO. Priya Raghunathan, who dispatches rail for BART, and Dmitri Soto, who runs a container yard, both said the neighborhood is busy, retail is strong and new households are moving in. The map agrees. The city's audit flags decay anyway, on sentiment and housing pressure, and Jax's point is that nobody on the initiative list owns it. A street can be loud, full and bleeding at once. I won't call this a fight between the street and the audit, because they may both be right. The unanswered part is who is supposed to be watching the rent.

Trevor Shimizu's ridership reporting is the week's one clean surge. Every station on the sheet rose, and 19th Street rose most, with on-time performance holding. Coen Cartier saw more people at 19th than he'd expect on a Wednesday afternoon, heading toward Lake Merritt and downtown. BART's Ximena Mehta called it a stacking of little things, a game downtown, a council vote, Baylight. Trevor is right that the rows don't pick a cause. I'll add that it's the same city that's keeping to itself and also, apparently, all on the platform at once.

Nia Rook's UNDOCKED board, for the record, went quiet in the best way. Three pilots flew, three ended even, and Luis Taz kept the lead without sitting in the chair. Elijah Brown said he didn't really have a strategy. Brooklyn Nguyen mined her ore and it didn't turn into profit. That's honest, and I find it comforting.

Next week is Cycle 116, and I'm watching for four things. Whether the council votes on the Stabilization Fund renewal, and whether anyone says out loud who the money reached. Whether the Pipeline's gap analysis and adjustment plan actually publish, and what they say about West Oakland and Fruitvale. Whether KONO gets a name on the list of things the city is working on. And I'm watching the clubhouse: how Taveras and Quintero fill the space Vinnie leaves, and whether Benji, who has the clipping on his door and the silence at his table, finds a way to carry both. If the paper taped up on his building is any guide, a lot of us will be reading.

By Kai Marston | Bay Tribune Culture

# The Clipping on the Door

The flyer on the pole at the corner is for a Rockridge block party that has nothing to do with baseball. Somebody staples a second sheet over the first one, and then a third, until the paper is a stack of curled edges that no one reads anymore. Two doors down, a plain sheet of paper is taped flat to a building's front door with four strips of clear tape, and the printed headline on it is a story about Benji Dillon.

Dillon is the reason people on this block are looking up. He is the A's left-handed pitcher the paper has been following for years, and the ledger has him rising. He doesn't live in the spotlight, though. He lives on a street where people still use their porches and still know each other's dogs.

"To be honest, I've noticed people recognizing me more often around Rockridge and at the grocery store," Dillon says, when I catch him on the sidewalk with a reusable bag in one hand and his keys in the other. "It feels a bit surreal, but I'm trying to stay grounded. My neighbor even taped an article about me to our building's front door, which was a surprise. I'm just trying to keep doing what I do and not let it change me too much."

He nods at the door. The clipping has gone a little soft at one corner from the morning damp. He doesn't pull it off. He doesn't look at it long, either. He looks like a man who has been stopped at the store four times this week and has decided to be polite about all of it.

This is the strange texture of a rising name in a neighborhood this size. A fan can write about a pitcher for a paper without ever having met him, and a block can hang the paper on its door anyway. The clipping is a small act of ownership, and it is also a small act of display. Both things are true on the same doorstep.

Anthony Raines, who covers the A's for this paper and lives on the same side of Rockridge, puts the local read into one breath. "Benji Dillon's a Rockridge icon, and his influence on the local culture is huge. I've seen it firsthand - people around here still talk about his games, his style. He's one of us, you know? The neighborhood loves him, and he loves it back." Raines is new on the culture record this cycle, and his own rise is the quieter story on the block. Here he is describing the neighborhood as the place where a sports figure becomes furniture.

Across the A's clubhouse, the rising and fading names sit in different rows of the same ledger. Darrin Davis, the Piedmont Ave outfielder, is climbing too. "I've seen Benji Dillon's name popping up more often around the A's, and it's clear he's gaining traction," Davis says. "To be honest, I'm not super close to him, but you can tell when someone's rising through the ranks." Davis is careful about distance, and his caution is its own kind of signal. Rising, in this ledger, is a thing people see from outside before anyone inside the room agrees on it.

Vinnie Keane is on the other side of the ledger. He is listed as cooling, the where-are-they-now file for a player whose last appearance was this cycle. The paper's C94 coverage already set the frame: Dillon came back on a one-year deal to make one last run with Keane. So the Rockridge doorstep carries two names at once, one climbing toward a bigger role and one being written into the past, both wearing the same team.

I think about the clipping on the door for the rest of the walk. A printed article taped to a building is a strange kind of shrine. It is not a poster, not a mural, not a show. It is a piece of news that the neighbors decided to keep in view. Who answers for what that display means, when the rising name starts to feel like ordinary weather and the tape loosens and the paper comes down?

Dillon, for his part, says he is trying to keep doing what he does. The block is trying to keep him where it can see him. Nobody on the sidewalk has settled which of those is the story.

By Tanya Cruz | Bay Tribune Sports

I stood in the clubhouse as the formal word landed: Vinnie Keane retires after the 2041 season that closed with 127 AB, a .315 average, 13 home runs, and 1.1 WAR to finish a career of 8229 AB, 436 home runs, and 71.4 WAR.

Vinnie leaned against the old bench near the tunnel, the one the trainers never moved. “The formal part doesn’t change the silence — the silence was already there,” he said. “I walked in and out of that clubhouse for twenty years, and the thing people don’t understand is that the voice they’re talking about was never just mine. It was the sound of the game itself, the way it asks something of you every single day. Now it’s Taveras’s turn to answer that, and Quintero’s, and I’ve got nothing but faith in both of them. The clubhouse doesn’t see itself differently because I signed a paper — it sees itself the way it always has: hungry, young, and ready to prove something. That’s the Oakland way. That doesn’t retire with me.”

Benji Dillon sat two lockers down, still in his road jacket, the one he never hangs up right away. “When Vinnie made it official, I wasn’t at the press conference,” he said. “I was at home, and Rick asked me why I was quiet at dinner. That’s the thing about the last of the old guard stepping away—it’s not the speeches or the highlights, it’s the silence after. You look around the clubhouse and you see Taveras and Quintero, and you know they’re ready, but you also know there’s no one left who remembers the bad years the way Vinnie and I do. The handoff isn’t about stats. It’s about teaching them that the city’s trust is earned in the quiet moments, not just the seventh game. I read the room by watching who’s still in the locker room after the cameras leave. That’s where the real passing of the torch happens.”

Mike Paulson stood in the doorway that leads to the offices, arms folded, watching the space where the chair by the tunnel stayed empty. “The contract being off the books was never really the story — Vinnie was never about the money,” he said. “It’s the silence in the clubhouse that hits you. You walk in and he’s not there, and for a second you forget, and then you remember. That’s the next chapter: not replacing him, because you can’t replace a guy like that. It’s Taveras and Quintero stepping into that space and making it their own. And it’s me making sure they get the runway to do it.”

The lockers stayed half-open. No one moved toward the chair.

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

# The Fund Has Moved $7 Million Out of $28 Million. The Record Names Two Households.

The renewal draft for the West Oakland Stabilization Fund sits in the clerk's hands, and the council takes it up at Cycle 116. By Marcus Webb's account, the paperwork is done. "The closeout is on the record, the renewal draft is in the clerk's hands, and the 23-day standard holds — but renewal belongs to the council, and I will not call their vote before they do," Webb said in the office's Cycle 115 statement.

Brenda Okoro, the deputy mayor whose authority the tracker names for the early disbursements, used almost the same sentence. "The closeout is published, the renewal ordinance is transmitted, and the standard holds at 23 days. Renewal is council's call — I won't call it before they do."

Two offices gave the same answer, and neither one said who got the money. That is the gap this ledger can measure.

**What the tracker holds**

The Initiative_Tracker row for INIT-001 puts the Fund at $28,000,000 in total budget, with $21,000,000 remaining and a last disbursement at Cycle 115. The difference is $7,000,000 out the door. The council passed the Fund 6-3 at Cycle 78. The tracker names Ramon Vega yes, Leonard Tran no, and Elliott Crane yes by remote vote. It does not name the other six seats. Mayor Avery Santana proposed the Fund and signed it the same cycle.

The tracker's milestone notes then trace the money:

- **C84–C90:** 47 checks, about $4.2 million, under Okoro's authority.
- **C109:** a $400,000 tranche, described as "2 household grants, $81,188."
- **C110 through C114:** $400,000 each cycle, with the 23-day processing standard noted along the way. The C112 note adds "cumulative $5M+ disbursed."
- **C114:** $400,000 executed, closeout publication ordered and charter renewal drafting directed.
- **C115:** the closeout ordered, the renewal ordinance transmitted, and the business-and-resident scope and the 23-day standard "preserved pending council action."

The C109 line is the only one that counts households, and it counts two. Taken alone, $81,188 across two grants is a little over $40,000 each. That is arithmetic, not a record of who received what. The same tranche was $400,000, which leaves $318,812 the note does not account for.

The C109 scope correction says the Fund serves "West Oakland businesses and residents." The unexplained balance may therefore have gone to businesses. The tracker does not say. I read the row for a recipient list, an amount per household, a business count and a street, and found none. A search of the paper's prior coverage returned the same gap in last cycle's ledger piece. It also returned a Cycle 104 mayoral statement saying 12 more households had cleared verification and were waiting on disbursement. That statement describes a queue. It is not a payee list.

**The clock against the queue**

The 23-day standard is a speed figure. It measures how fast a file moves from application to check. It does not say whose file moved or whether the checks reached the long-tenure residents the C108 scope note says the charter was written for.

Webb's second statement leans on the queue. "The mood in West Oakland is slipping and the housing pressure is building — which is exactly why the queue does not stop while the paperwork goes to the record." This is the same argument for keeping the checks moving. The problem is that "the record" he points to is a closeout, and the closeout is a document about the process.

Picture the clerk's counter the day the ordinance lands. A drafted renewal sits in a folder with a published C114 closeout clipped behind it. Someone in West Oakland waiting on a file would have to read the closeout to learn whether anyone on their block was paid. By the tracker's own notes, it names two households and one tranche.

**The exposure on the council floor**

Cycle 116 is crowded. Council also has the recorded decision on Commercial Stabilization for Grand Lake ($2.5 million, proposed by Tran). The tracker also schedules a decision on the West Oakland Tenant Defense Fund ($4.2 million). The Tenant Defense row reads "eviction filings outpacing Stabilization Fund relief." That is the proposal's own stated reason, and it implies the Fund's reach is already being questioned from inside the chamber.

The Fund has $21 million remaining, and the renewal ordinance asks the council to keep it alive. Votes at 6-3 made it, and the tracker has not recorded a renewal vote yet. Its renewal fields (vote cycle, amount, outcome) read 108, a blank and "Standing." That is not a finding, only what the row holds today.

**The question the record can't answer yet**

The clerk can hand over the draft. Webb and Okoro have both said they will not predict the vote. The council will be asked to extend a fund with a 23-day clock, a $21 million balance and one recipient line in the public record, covering two households in a single tranche.

When the ordinance reaches the floor at Cycle 116, will any seat ask for the payee ledger first: how many households and how many businesses were paid out of the roughly $7 million, and where? Or will the council renew a fund that is good at publishing closeouts, and not one that can show it is reaching West Oakland?

By Jax Caldera | Bay Tribune Civic

# Who Owns KONO's Rent Pressure When Everyone on Telegraph Says Business Is Good?

Priya Raghunathan was feeding quarters into a dryer on Telegraph when I asked her about decay. She dispatches rail for BART and lives in KONO. She looked at me like I'd asked about a plague.

"I've noticed that KONO is getting busier. Retail is busy and events are drawing crowds. We're also getting new households moving into the neighborhood."

Dmitri Soto runs a container yard, lives in KONO, and was folding towels two machines down. He heard the question and didn't wait for me to finish. "I haven't really seen any decay around here. KONO's been bustling, to be honest. Retail's busy, events are drawing crowds, and we're getting new households moving in. It's all pretty positive from where I'm standing."

They're right, and the neighborhood map backs them. The row for KONO reads retail vitality 9.74 and event attractiveness 10.75. Its migration flow is labeled "Inflow surge," and its trajectory is labeled "growth."

The audit I was handed for this cycle flags KONO as **decay**, and it has two reasons. Sentiment is -0.02, which ranks 12th of 22 neighborhoods, against a city median of -0.015. Housing pressure is 0.5, which ranks 14th of 22, against a city median of zero.

So here's the fight. The street says busy. The map says growth. The audit says decay. All three describe the same blocks.

My read, and it's inference, is that they don't contradict each other the way they look like they do. Look at what Priya and Dmitri are celebrating: new households moving in. Then look at the housing pressure number. The map has the median rent in KONO at $2,062. A neighborhood can be loud, full and bleeding at once. Busy retail doesn't tell you who gets to stay.

The audit's own finding is the part that burns. No active initiative, and no mitigator, owns this break on the record. I checked the running list of what the city says it's working on. Stabilization is paying out in West Oakland. A transit hub is in design in Fruitvale. Grand Lake has a commercial vote ready. KONO's name isn't on any of it.

Compare that to what the paper said before. In C96 Luis Navarro reported KONO went three cycles on generators and candlelight while City Hall filed it as a utility ticket. In C102 the Tribune's retail beat described a retail collapse along Telegraph between 23rd and 27th. Now the map shows retail at 9.74, with crowds and an inflow surge. That's a long way from shuttered bays. Somebody should be able to tell me what changed, who changed it, and whether the people who lived through the dark years are the same people now being counted as "new households."

I can't answer that. The prior comparison the audit needs to validate its own direction isn't in my hands. Nobody at any office has answered the mismatch, either.

I also called a waiter who works in San Antonio, not KONO. Dank Abara is the only person I reached who said the word out loud. "I've heard people talking about KONO neighborhood decay, and to be honest, it's something that's on my mind," he said. "I just know that around here, people are talking about it."

He admits he doesn't know the specifics. Neither do I. But when a neighborhood with a "growth" label gets talked about as decaying by people one neighborhood over, that's a rumor with a reason behind it. The rumor is a rent number nobody is watching.

Priya and Dmitri aren't wrong, and I'm not here to take their good news. I'm here to point out that their good news is the housing pressure. No office on the record has said it owns that.

So who answers for it? Does Councilmember Warren Ashford (D7, CRC), whose district includes KONO on the map, know KONO has a decay flag and no initiative behind it? And if Mayor Santana's office has a plan for this corridor, why is it the one thing in KONO nobody is advertising?

-- Jax Caldera | tipline: JAX-TIPS

# Nineteenth Street Filled Up, and the Rows Do Not Say Why

On the record for Cycle 115, 19th St Oakland carried 16,076 riders. The previous cycle's row had 10,752. The new row also reads "above-average ridership" and "game day crowds" in its notes. The row's on-time figure is 81%, one point better than C114. The gain in riders was 5,324, the largest of any station. The platform took the added people without losing its timetable.

Coen Cartier drives for AC Transit. He did not feel a jump on his own runs, and he said so. "Wednesday's a normal workday for me, so I can't say I noticed any big jump on my runs," he said. "But I did see more folks waiting at 19th than I'd expect on a regular afternoon — that station's been busier lately, and with the council vote and the game downtown, people were moving around more than usual. Most of them got on heading toward the Lake Merritt and downtown stops, not so much out to the edges."

That is a bus operator reading a rail platform from the curb. It is useful testimony because it is modest. He saw a crowd and a direction of travel. He did not see a cause, and the rows do not give him one.

## One station, then eight

The 19th Street number is the loudest, but it is not alone. Eight stations are on the record this cycle, and every one rose:

- 12th St Oakland City Center: 14,526 riders, up 4,515. On-time was 86%, up 9 points.
- West Oakland: 9,519, up 3,491. On-time was 77%, up 3 points.
- Fruitvale: 8,943, up 3,880. On-time was 87%, up 15 points.
- Coliseum: 7,776, up 2,980. On-time was 81%.
- Lake Merritt: 6,780, up 2,684.
- Rockridge: 5,684, up 2,397.
- MacArthur: 4,634, up 1,828.

Together the stations carried 73,938 riders against 46,839 the cycle before. That is roughly 58 percent more people on the same platforms.

The corridors moved in step. The metrics sheet gives I-580 East a traffic index of 89, up 51 from C114. I-880 South reads 98, up 44. I-880 North and I-980 each read 100. Grand Ave, International Blvd and Telegraph Ave all rose. When the freeways and the platforms climb together, the explanation is probably not a platform problem.

## What the factors say, and what they leave out

Ximena Mehta, who handles public relations for BART's Oakland division, offered the stacking theory. "It's not one big thing — it's a bunch of little things stacking up," she said. "We had the Oaks game downtown, the Baylight District vote finally going through, and events all over Lake Merritt, Temescal, and KONO. When you get that many neighborhood pulls happening on the same ordinary Tuesday, the platforms fill up fast. The operators were saying 19th Street felt like a game day crowd even though it wasn't a game day for them. It's just what happens when the whole city decides to move at once."

The 19th Street row backs most of that list: a downtown game day, events in Downtown, Lake Merritt, Temescal and KONO, and Baylight District work marked construction-active. It also lists crowded platforms. The Oaks beat the Magic 125–109 at KONO on a 94 percent house.

The other stations do not all share those causes. Fruitvale's row names no game. It lists events in East Oakland, twice, and the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II visioning, marked design-phase. Elena Soria Dominguez, the hub's planning lead, would not credit the planning work. "I can't say the visioning is what's pulling those extra riders in — we're still in the design phase, no shovels in the ground," she said. She pointed to East Oakland's events and crowds instead. She added that the construction workers on their breaks under the overpass "see it from the platform every day."

Two things in the record cut against a tidy story. The two quotes name different days, a Tuesday and a Wednesday. The metrics rows carry no day name at all. The C114 row for 19th Street also lists "weekend" among its factors, and the C115 row lists none. This cycle is marked an ordinary weekday with no citywide cut. Comparing a weekday to a weekend is a plausible part of the gap. The record does not say how much of it, so this is a reading, not a finding.

## The rows are quiet where it matters

No chaos event touched BART or AC Transit this cycle. No stalled train is on any of these rows, and no station reports a short crew. Fruitvale's on-time figure rose 15 points while its riders rose by 3,880. That is the opposite of strain.

The record carries one reading of the crowding itself: the congestion figure reads 0.12 on every station row. That is below the 0.30 on 19th Street's C114 row. A larger crowd on a calmer system is an odd pairing, and the rows do not explain it.

## What no row can name

If every station moved together on an ordinary weekday, what changed in the city that no single station row can name? The rows give games, events and a council vote, each as a separate pull. They do not say whether the 73,938 is a one-cycle pile-up or a new floor. 19th Street's earlier rows swing between roughly 5,000 and 17,000. If the next ordinary weekday lands near 16,000, the platforms are carrying a different city than the one they were scheduled for. On-time performance will show it first.
