THE CYCLE PULSE — Y2C108
The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

The morning fog that Noah Tan's reporting caught this week — Marc Allie adjusting the thermostat a third time in his Laurel kitchen, Tyrie Groin pulling her coat tighter on the way to work — feels like the right metaphor for what this city is living through in Y2C108. Something is muffling the signal. Money is moving, projects are in motion, teams are on the field, and yet everywhere you turn, Oakland is straining to hear itself think.

Start in West Oakland, where Carmen Delaine spent time at the Community Bakery and came back with something more honest than a press release. Daniel Cloak, flour on his apron, told her he's heard folks talking about the Stabilization Fund — "some hopeful, some skeptical." Irene Fay at the coffee shop said it feels like something that happens somewhere else. Darius Clark said he hasn't seen much change. What Carmen's reporting gives us isn't a picture of failure; it's a picture of a gap — between disbursement and arrival, between announcement and impact. The fund is real. The money is moving. But the people it was designed to reach are still watching from a distance, which means either the communication infrastructure doesn't exist or the on-the-ground delivery is lagging badly behind the paperwork. Neither answer is acceptable three disbursement cycles in.

Luis Navarro found the same gap wearing different clothes over in Fruitvale. He sat with a teacher named Rick Walker, grading spelling tests with a red pen still moving, who told him the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II feels like the council dragging its feet. Then Luis found a baker in Temescal with flour on his forearms saying almost the same words. Then two servers in Rockridge. Four people, three neighborhoods, zero names attached to the project. What Luis is describing isn't just public confusion — it's the sound a city makes when accountability has been designed out of a process. Nobody in those conversations claimed they'd asked and been refused an answer. They're not describing a wall. They're describing a void. That's harder to fix, because voids don't fight back.

The two civic stories sit next to each other this week like a matched pair of warnings. In one, money is flowing but residents can't trace where it lands. In the other, a project is talked about citywide but nobody can name who owns it. Oakland has a transparency problem that isn't about secrecy — it's about structure. The systems for connecting decisions to people don't appear to exist, or if they do, they're not functioning at street level.

Meanwhile, Jordan Velez spent time at the Anthropic construction site on Baylight Avenue, where the old rail yard is giving way to glass and steel. Kelvon Ochoa, an AI safety researcher, noticed more familiar faces at the coffee shops. Brianna Lee, a preservation carpenter with Baylight Construction Authority, described the energy as the district coming alive. And Tariq Jello, adjusting his hardhat, observed with the precision of someone who's watched neighborhoods change before: the coffee shops are busier, the plumbing problems are the same. Velez didn't oversell this, and I'm glad — because the question he left open, about who the district will truly serve as it grows, is the question that connects directly to what Carmen and Luis are reporting. Growth without accountability infrastructure doesn't stabilize anything. It just moves the instability around.

Mason Ortega went to OakTown Social in Temescal and found something this week's civic reporting badly needed: a counter-example. Vanessa Treary and her colleague Vanessa Ramas described a workplace where the rhythms are legible, where the relationships are real, where you know your place in the neighborhood even if that place is just pulling shots. "It's about knowing your place in the neighborhood, even if it's just pulling shots and remembering who takes oat milk," Ramas told Mason. I keep returning to that line. It's not sentimental — it's structural. Those baristas have what the Stabilization Fund and the Transit Hub Phase II currently lack: human beings who know the names.

In Fruitvale, Maria Keen found Lucia Polito at Aura Wellness describing the Postpartum Oakland Network she founded three cycles ago — new mothers navigating sleepless nights, a program built from lived experience rather than policy architecture. Nearby, the Transit Corridor Mutual Aid, the Summer Speed Series. Maria's reporting and Mason's both point toward the same truth: the city's connective tissue is being maintained by people who decided to maintain it, not by systems designed to sustain them.

On the sports desk, the A's are in the World Series and the Oaks are finding their preseason footing. Simon Leary spent time with Vladimir Gonzalez and came back with a quiet piece about what a streak actually looks like from inside a clubhouse that's not celebrating. "How nobody gets too high or too low," Gonzalez told Simon. Veteran Darrin Davis called it "everyone being willing to shift roles without ego." That's the architecture Simon's describing — distributed load-bearing, no single point of failure. Anthony Raines focused on the weight Danny Horn is carrying: a .190 average in forty-two postseason at-bats, zero home runs, a slump sitting in the middle of a lineup that has otherwise caught fire. Manager Deacon Seymour told Anthony the numbers don't tell the whole story. Maybe not. But Game 1 starter Arturo Ramos, with a 1.93 ERA and everything on the line, can't wait for the story to change.

What I'm watching next week: whether anyone in city government responds to the accountability vacuum Luis documented, because that piece ends mid-sentence — and the silence where a name should be is the loudest thing in this paper. I'm also watching the Stabilization Fund's next disbursement checkpoint, and whether Baylight's construction pace starts pulling workers from the same West Oakland neighborhoods the fund is supposed to protect. Those two stories are converging. I want to know if anyone in city hall sees it yet.

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

**West Oakland Stabilization Fund: Money in Motion, But Where’s It Landing?**

At the counter of West Oakland’s Community Bakery, Daniel Cloak leans over a tray of fresh rolls, his apron dusted with flour. “I’ve heard folks talking about the Stabilization Fund,” he says, his tone measured. “Some hopeful, some skeptical, but everyone’s watching to see where it lands.”

The West Oakland Stabilization Fund, now in its disbursement-active phase, was passed to address economic fragility in the neighborhood. Yet, as the money begins to move, residents like Daniel are waiting to see tangible change. The fund’s implementation has sparked conversations across the neighborhood—in bakeries, coffee shops, and community centers—but the consensus so far is cautious optimism.

Irene Fay, a barista at a West Oakland coffee shop, echoes the sentiment. “I’ve heard folks at the coffee shop talking about the Stabilization Fund,” she says, “but honestly, it feels like one of those things that happens somewhere else—like I see the signs about meetings sometimes, but it doesn’t touch my day-to-day yet.”

The fund’s purpose is clear: to stabilize West Oakland by investing in local infrastructure, small businesses, and community programs. Yet, as Darius Clark, another bakery worker, notes, “I’ve heard folks talking about the Stabilization Fund, but honestly, I haven’t seen much change around here yet. It’s hard to tell where the money’s going.”

Talmon Lint, a solar panel installation lead, adds to the conversation. “I’ve heard folks talking about the Stabilization Fund at the community center,” he says, “but honestly, I’ve been so tied up with installations that I haven’t seen much of its impact firsthand.”

The Fund’s disbursement marks a critical step in its lifecycle, but residents are keenly aware that the real test lies in its execution. As West Oakland watches the money flow, the question remains: Will the Stabilization Fund live up to its name, or will it join the ranks of well-intentioned but mismanaged initiatives?

By Luis Navarro | Bay Tribune Civic

## The File Nobody's Holding

Rick Walker is at his desk before the first bell, red pen in hand, grading spelling tests in a Fruitvale classroom that still smells like floor wax. He's got a stack of papers and a former line cook's habit of working fast under pressure. When I ask what's on his mind outside the classroom, he doesn't talk about his students. He talks about a transit project.

"I've heard folks around here talking about the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II," he says, red pen still moving, "but honestly, it feels like the council's been dragging their feet. People want accountability, but it's hard to tell who's really stepping up to make things happen."

That's one voice, one neighborhood. It stops being local when you hear it again in Temescal.

Melton Neilon has flour on his forearms at whatever hour bakers call morning, folding dough on a bench that's seen this motion ten thousand times. He's not talking about bread. "I've heard folks talking about the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II, and it feels like there's a lot of uncertainty about who's really driving it forward," he says. "It's frustrating when you don't know who's accountable or what's being done to make it happen."

Same words, almost. Different oven, different neighborhood, same blank space where a name should be.

Cross the city to Rockridge and the pattern holds. Elio Perez, working the floor on a slow shift, says it plain: "I've heard people talking about the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II, but it feels like the council's been dragging their feet. It's frustrating because we need that kind of investment in our neighborhoods." His coworker Depak Ying, same restaurant, same shift, echoes it back with a different word choice but the identical shape of complaint: "I've heard people talking about the Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II visioning, and it feels like there's a lot of confusion about who's really in charge and what's being done. It's frustrating because it's such an important project for the community."

Four people. Three neighborhoods. Zero names.

Nobody in these four conversations can tell you who owns this file. Not the baker, not the teacher, not either server. That's not an accusation — none of them claim to have asked and been refused. They're describing a rumor mill with no source at the center of it, a project that everyone's heard about and nobody can point to.

I went looking for what fills that gap this cycle. There isn't a civic initiative on the books addressing the signal these four residents are circling. Not a task force, not a working group, not a name attached to Phase II that any of them, or the record I have, can produce.

That silence sits next to the project itself. A transit hub with a "Phase II" attached to its name implies a Phase I finished somewhere, a plan that moved once and now sits somewhere between meetings. Four separate people, unconnected to each other, are all bumping into the same wall: they can hear the project's name, but nobody can hand them the person responsible for it.

Rick Walker goes back to his spelling tests. Melton Neilon goes back to his dough. Elio Perez and Depak Ying go back to their tables. The question doesn't clock out with them.

Who is actually holding the file on Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II?

## Oakland Oaks Struggle in Preseason Opener
The near-empty press row at the Oakland Oaks' practice facility was abuzz with concern as Coach Wilson Shepard addressed the media after the team's preseason opener. "I've been feeling like we're still finding our rhythm, you know? Preseason's about figuring things out, not about winning every game. The guys are putting in the work, and I'm confident we'll tighten up and be ready for the real deal," he said, his voice laced with a mix of optimism and caution.

As the team reviewed the game footage, Austin Reaves, the Oaks' shooting guard, couldn't help but feel a sense of frustration. "I've been feeling like we're still finding our footing, you know? Preseason's a time to work out the kinks, but it's hard not to worry when you're not seeing the wins. I trust Coach Wilson Shepard to get us ready, though," he said, his eyes fixed on the screen.

Adash Stanley, the Oaks' point guard, took a more positive approach, focusing on the excitement building around the team. "I've felt the excitement building around the Oaks, it's like the whole city is waking up with the team. There's a buzz in the air, and I think people are really looking forward to seeing what they can do this season," he said, a hint of a smile on his face.

Trenton Nawan, a resident of Jack London, watched the game with a mix of concern and investment. "I've seen the Oaks struggle in their preseason performance, it's got me a bit worried about how they'll do this season. As someone who lives in Jack London, I'm really invested in seeing them succeed," he said, his brow furrowed in concern.

The Oaks' preseason opener ended in a loss, with a record of 0-1 and a streak of L1. Austin Reaves led the team with 22 points, 6 assists, and 6 rebounds, while AJ Dybantsa added 18 points and 11 rebounds. Despite the loss, the team remains optimistic, with Coach Wilson Shepard emphasizing the importance of preseason as a time to figure things out.

As the team looks to bounce back from their loss, the question on everyone's mind is: can the Oaks turn their preseason struggles around and make a strong showing in the regular season?

By Mason Ortega | Bay Tribune Culture

# The Rhythm That Holds: At OakTown Social, the Baristas Who Keep Temescal Steady

Vanessa Treary's hands move through muscle memory at OakTown Social's espresso machine—tamping, steaming, pouring—while her eyes track the morning regulars filing in. The first is the construction worker who takes his americano black. Then the grad student who always asks for extra foam. Then the older man who sits by the window with the same oatmeal bowl every Tuesday. 

"It’s not glamorous, but there’s something steady about it," Treary says, nodding to fellow barista Vanessa Ramas as they navigate around each other in the narrow workspace. "You get to know people, their routines, their stories. It’s not just about the coffee—it’s about being a small part of their day, something they can count on."

What keeps them coming back to this particular stretch of Temescal is the rhythm—not just of the coffee routine, but of the neighborhood itself. OakTown Social's ledger shows modest growth, but the real metrics are written in the silent exchanges between staff and regulars. 

Ramas chimes in during a lull between rushes: "You know, it's not about glamour—it's about the rhythm of it. The regulars who come in and don't even have to say their order because you already know it. There's something steadying about that, especially in a city that's always changing. Tobias says I could find a job that pays more, but it's not just about the money. It's about knowing your place in the neighborhood, even if it's just pulling shots and remembering who takes oat milk."

At a corner table, Jia Pena watches the dance between baristas and neighborhood. "It's the rhythm of it, I think. The way the neighborhood moves and breathes, and you’re part of that. It’s not glamorous, but it’s steady. You know the faces, the routines, and there’s something comforting in that. It’s like being part of a bigger thing, even if it’s just pulling shots or cleaning the machine."

Seven employees strong, OakTown Social moves to a tempo set by the people who work there and the people who keep coming back. The unspoken question lingers under the hum of the espresso machine: In a city pushing relentlessly forward, who holds space for the rhythms that anchor us?

By Anthony Raines | Bay Tribune Sports

The first thing that jumps out on Danny Horn's postseason card is the zero in the home run column. Through forty-two at-bats this October, Oakland's center fielder is hitting a meager .190, with just three runs batted in and a slugging percentage that would make a light-hitting middle infielder blush. For a player expected to anchor the A's lineup, those numbers are hard to ignore.

"Look, I know my numbers haven’t been where I want them this postseason," Horn admitted in a quiet moment before Game 1 of the World Series. "But I’m grinding every day. This team’s got heart, and I’m gonna do everything I can to help us bring home that championship."

The sentiment is admirable, but the Phillies’ pitching staff won’t care about heart when Horn steps to the plate. His struggles are amplified by the success of his teammates. Eric Taveras is hitting .359 with four home runs. Vladimir Gonzalez has matched Taveras' power output while batting .286. Ernesto Quintero, often hitting behind Horn, has three homers and a .324 average. In a lineup that has otherwise thrived, Horn’s slump stands out like a sore thumb.

"Danny Horn’s been grinding, and I respect that," manager Deacon Seymour said. "The numbers don’t tell the whole story—he’s a guy who shows up every day, puts in the work, and keeps his head in the game. Right now, he’s not finding the gaps, but I’ve seen him turn it around before."

The A’s have managed to win despite Horn’s struggles, but the margin for error becomes razor-thin in the World Series. Game 1 starter Arturo Ramos, who carries a 1.93 ERA into tonight’s contest, knows the stakes. "Numbers don’t always tell the whole story—he’s got the kind of at-bats that wear pitchers down," Ramos said of Horn. "We’re not worried. When it clicks for him, it’s gonna be big."

The question isn’t just whether Horn can turn it around—it’s whether the A’s can survive another series if he doesn’t. With Philadelphia’s rotation looking formidable on paper, Oakland may not have the luxury of carrying a slumping star. For a team chasing its seventh ring, the margin for error has never been smaller.

**The Load That Doesn't Bend**  
By Simon Leary  

Vladimir Gonzalez stands at the edge of the batting cage, glove tucked under his arm, watching the arc of balls meeting bats. The right fielder’s stance is loose, familiar—a player who knows both his place in the lineup and the quiet weight of maintaining it. Around him, the rhythm of swings and catches hums with the steadiness of a team that has won five straight, a team that wakes up each morning to find its record at 3-0 for the cycle.  

This is not momentum. Momentum implies a force gathering itself, a thing that could be spent. What the A’s have built is something more tectonic: a structure where roles shift seamlessly beneath the surface, where each piece bears just enough load to keep the whole upright.  

"You see it in how guys prepare every day," Gonzalez says later, peeling tape from his wrists. "How nobody gets too high or too low. We just keep grinding." His words are less about effort than about trust—the kind that lets a team redistribute its weight without buckling. Across the clubhouse, veterans like Vinnie Keane nod at the same idea. "It’s not just about the wins," he offers. "It’s about knowing your part and playing it right, even when the spotlight’s not on you."  

Here is the unseen architecture: a roster where every player adjusts without protest, where legends like Darrin Davis remember a time when such flexibility was rare. "You know, when you're in the middle of a streak like this, it's not about any one guy carrying the load," Davis reflects. "It's about each of us finding new ways to contribute when the moment calls for it. I've seen pitchers turning into leaders in the clubhouse, rookies stepping up like seasoned vets, and guys like me adjusting how we play to fit what the team needs that day. That's the secret - everyone being willing to shift roles without ego."  

The record, then, is not proof of dominance. It is the byproduct of a deeper equilibrium, one that asks only this: How many ways can a team hold itself together?
