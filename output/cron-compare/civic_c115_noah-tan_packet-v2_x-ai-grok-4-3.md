Ramon Vega stood on the same West Oakland block where the new bench had gone in the day before. The pine boards had already lost their raw scent to the air. Two folding chairs remained at the curb across the street, their occupants still talking as the light held. A third person had joined them, coat draped over one arm, listening without hurry. Vega watched the exchange lengthen past the usual quick nod and pass-by.

He stepped closer to the bench, ran a hand along the backrest, and turned toward the open storefront. “Mild doesn't have to mean weak — I still believe that,” he said. “Five days of this clear, easy weather and you see it everywhere: people sitting longer on the benches, kids staying out after the tutoring program lets go, neighbors actually talking to each other instead of rushing past. That's not weakness. That's the city catching its breath. And the work still gets done — the council business, the follow-through, the fights worth having. I've just learned that you don't have to shout to be heard. Sometimes you show up steady, you listen, and you move the bench. That's still how I see it.”

A delivery van idled at the corner long enough for its driver to step out and stretch both arms overhead before climbing back in. Further down the block, a pair of workers adjusted the last anchor on a second bench frame. The concrete around the first one had already set firm. No one checked the sky. The sequence of clear days had removed the question of whether the light would last.

Vega walked the block once more, pausing where the new benches faced the empty lot. The conversation at the curb continued, voices carrying the short distance without effort. One of the seated figures laughed, the sound flat and ordinary against the storefront wall. Vega nodded to himself, then continued on foot toward the next block where the same pattern of open doors and unmoving chairs had begun to repeat.
