# Laurel keeps its head down while the initiatives go elsewhere

Rosalind Young opens her smoke shop in Laurel the way she has described it: head down, doors unlocked, taxes paid. Out front, the sidewalk is the part of the job she can't control.

"Honestly, I can't say any city council person has ever reached out to me directly about anything," Young says. "I run a small shop, I keep my head down, I pay my taxes. But I do notice things around the neighborhood — the little things that don't get fixed, the street maintenance that seems to lag, the way the sidewalks get cluttered. It's not one big thing, it's just a feeling that nobody's really asking the small business owners or the regular folks what we need. I haven't heard of any specific initiative for Laurel lately, and that silence kind of speaks for itself."

Her feeling is one resident's account, not a finding. I checked it against the records I could open.

**What the record shows**

The Initiative_Tracker, searched on "Laurel," returned one row: the Temescal Community Health Center. Laurel appears there only in a list of 22 neighborhoods, inside a conversion note about how the clinic's effect on Temescal will be measured. It is not listed as an affected neighborhood. No initiative row names Laurel as a place it serves.

The paper's own engine audit for this cycle says the same thing: no civic initiative addresses the Laurel signal surfaced this cycle. The paper's prior coverage, searched on "Laurel," turned up no council action or initiative aimed at the neighborhood. The hits were a ledger entry about a resident wandering Laurel's small shops, a mention of Laurel among districts where student counts moved, and a line about a man's address changing away from it.

The Neighborhood_Map row for Cycle 114 shows Laurel in District 9, with a "Stable" demographic marker and a "steady" trajectory. Median rent is listed at $2,146. Rent takes 0.3 of income there, the same share the sheet shows for Fruitvale. Sentiment reads -0.03, close to flat. The sheet describes a neighborhood holding still, and it does not describe a crisis.

Next door, things are moving. Fruitvale and Dimond both border Laurel on the map. Fruitvale has its Transit Hub Phase II visioning under way. Dimond has a revitalization initiative announced. Laurel has neither a project nor a named sponsor.

**What I can't tell you**

The Packet does not show whether anyone on the council has ever contacted Young, or any other Laurel business owner. Her statement is hers. Nothing I was given confirms or refutes it.

It doesn't show whether anyone has asked the city about the street maintenance or the sidewalks she describes. I have no request, no response, and no date to measure a wait against, so I'm not counting days.

It doesn't name who is accountable. The ledger material doesn't say which office would own a Laurel initiative, or whether the district's council member has been asked about one. I won't invent a duty for anyone. A missing initiative is not proof that anyone neglected the neighborhood, and I'm not claiming it.

Here is what I can say. The city's tracker and the paper's coverage show nothing in Laurel to point to. The neighborhood's own data doesn't flag an emergency. Young says the lack of attention is how it feels from behind her counter. Those two things can be true together, and the gap between them is where a question belongs.

For the council, that question is plain. If a Laurel shopkeeper wants to know who in city government is responsible for asking her what the block needs, where does she start? The records I opened don't say.

The sidewalk out front is still cluttered, and Young still has the shop to run. Who is supposed to come ask her?

# The Oaks’ 5-1 start now has two very different readings

**Selena Grant | Bay Tribune Sports**

The Oaks’ 104-100 loss to the Pacers ended a perfect start, even after Oakland erased a 20-point deficit. One game later, the Oaks answered with a 135-75 rout of the Raptors. The record is 5-1, with a one-game winning streak, but the back-to-back results expose the central question of an expansion build: how much of the early success is repeatable, and how much depends on the game breaking their way?

The Indiana loss was not an empty performance. Adash Stanley scored 19 points, collected five rebounds and four assists, and made five of 11 three-point attempts. Austin Reaves also scored 19, adding six rebounds and seven assists without a turnover. AJ Dybantsa had 15 points and five rebounds, while Wendell Carter Jr. went 5-for-6 from the field for 10 points and seven rebounds. The Oaks came back from 20 down, but the recovery stopped short of a win.

That distinction matters. A comeback can show that a roster has enough creation to repair a bad start. It does not establish that the same roster can avoid the bad start. The record supplies evidence of resilience in Indiana, not an explanation for the 20-point hole.

Toronto supplied the opposite kind of evidence. Reaves scored 20 points on 8-for-16 shooting, including four of six from three-point range. T. Jerome posted 19 points, seven rebounds and seven assists. Stanley added 16 points, seven rebounds and six assists while making all four of his three-point attempts. Carter finished with 14 points and 15 rebounds, including five offensive rebounds, and Dybantsa scored 12 points on 5-for-8 shooting.

The difference between the games is too large to treat as a simple continuation of form. The Oaks’ top contributors were productive in both results, but Toronto brought a broader and more efficient statistical profile. The first game required recovery; the second never became competitive. Neither result, by itself, identifies which version is the foundation.

The clearest development line belongs to Harry Hayward. The second-round rookie scored eight points, grabbed four rebounds and made three of four shots, including both of his three-point attempts, in 13 minutes. That is a small workload, but it is a concrete contribution in a team whose franchise stability is listed as growing. For an expansion club, a useful rotation piece is not decoration; it is part of the answer to how the roster survives uneven nights.

The Oaks now have a 5-1 record and the league’s most recent evidence is a 60-point win. The front office still owes the harder answer: what factors produced the swing from a 20-point deficit and a four-point loss to a 60-point rout, and which number should define the build—the recovery, the margin, or the rotation that made both possible?

# The Board in the Trailer Says 9.5 Percent. Enzo Walker Is Counting Something Else.

*By Jordan Velez, Business Reporter*

Enzo Walker is a plumber at Coastline Construction in West Oakland. His working day takes him past the board in the job-site trailer, where the company's numbers are posted where the crew can see them. This cycle the numbers are good. The Business Ledger lists Coastline at 221 employees, a 9.5 percent growth rate and $187.1 million in annual revenue, up $341,000 from last cycle. By any spreadsheet, the firm Walker works for is winning.

"Look, I see the numbers on the board in the trailer — 9.5% growth, revenue up, all that. And sure, we're busy. I'm not complaining about work," Walker said. "But the numbers don't tell you what it's like on the ground. The buildings going up, they're not for us. They're for the tech folks coming in, the ones from Oakmesh and Civis. I fix pipes in places that didn't exist last year, and the people who used to live on those blocks? They're gone. The work's changing, alright — but it's changing in a way where I'm not sure the folks signing the checks even notice who's not at the table anymore. The ledger says growth. The street says something else."

The ledger lists 19 named workers at five West Oakland businesses this cycle, and every one of those five firms is up. Three are construction outfits. Coastline is the largest. Northgate Construction, run by managing partner Helena Voss-Adeyemi, is listed at 95 employees, 8.8 percent growth and $78.8 million in revenue, up $133,000. Mariner Construction is listed at 165 employees and 7.2 percent growth. Those three crews include plumbers Hazel Reed, Kenton Pearl, Maeve Pillai and Yadira Rao, electricians Elijah Brown and Jude Hess, laborer Elijah Roberts and carpenter apprentice Minh Raimon.

The other two firms are the reason the sites are busy. Oakmesh Systems, founded by Marcus Tan, is listed at 46 employees and $9.5 million in revenue. Its growth rate is 34.1 percent, more than three times Coastline's. Civis Systems, Elias Varek's urban-systems intelligence firm, is listed at 41 employees and $60.2 million in revenue. Its roster includes digital twin architects Sam Fazio and Catherine Santos and drone fleet coordinator Maurice Franklin.

The pay gap shows up in the ledger rows themselves. Coastline's average salary is listed at $85,000. Northgate's is $76,000. Oakmesh's is $135,000. Walker installs the plumbing, Northgate's electricians pull the wire, and the engineers who move in at the higher number are the ones bidding on the apartments nearby. Desmond Achebe, Coastline's managing partner, sees the problem from his own payroll.

"What keeps me up at night isn't the growth on the ledger — I can read a spreadsheet. It's the quiet stuff the numbers don't show," Achebe said. "I walk a different street home sometimes, just to see what's actually happening on the block, and I see the new folks settling in from Chinatown, the Stabilization Fund money finally moving. That's good. But I also see the workers — my plumbers, the electricians, the carpenters at the other shops — and I know a 9% growth number doesn't tell you whether a guy can afford to stay in the neighborhood he's helping rebuild. The contraction isn't loud. It's the guy who's been on the crew for twelve years quietly looking at rents across the bridge. That's what I'm watching. The growth is real, but if it's not growing for the people doing the work, it's just a taller building on the same shaky ground."

That is an unusual thing for an owner to say. In my experience, a managing partner with a strong quarter usually talks about backlog. Achebe talked about retention, and what he described isn't on any ledger yet. Losing a twelve-year journeyman doesn't change a revenue line. It changes who is still at the site gate at 6 a.m. a year from now.

Tan, whose company is the fastest-growing on the list, sounded like a man who knows people are watching his door.

"I can't speak for the whole neighborhood, but I can tell you what I see from my own door. Oakmesh is growing, and that growth means we hire more people, we pay more taxes, we take up more space. That's real," he said. "But the neighborhood isn't a ledger. It's the people who've been here through the ups and downs. What do we owe? We owe it to not forget that. We owe it to hire locally when we can, to be a good neighbor, to not let our success push anyone out. And what does the neighborhood owe us? I think it owes us a fair shake — the same chance to grow that it gives everyone else. It's a two-way street, and we're all still figuring out how to walk it together."

"When we can" is the phrase I would ask him to define. It is a promise with no number attached.

Not every block in the city is growing. The ledger's contraction list this cycle is concentrated in Ivy Hill. Ivy Hill Market shed three workers and is at negative 4.5 percent growth. Ivy Hill Cafe, Ivy Cleaners and Ivy Hill Family Dental each shed one. Down in the Baylight District, Marine Systems lost one worker. Nothing in the record ties those losses to West Oakland's gains. Still, it is the same city in the same spring, and the ledger shows it moving in two directions. The growth is in construction and civic tech. The losses are at a grocery, a cafe, a cleaner and a dentist, the businesses people use every day.

My mother's laundromat on 7th Street would have shown up on that second list, if it had lasted long enough to be counted.

That leaves the question Walker raised in the trailer. Coastline's 9.5 percent is a real number, and the revenue is too. But the board doesn't show what that growth is worth at the end of a plumber's shift, measured against the rent on the blocks he's plumbing, or who gets to count it as theirs.

# At Rockridge pickup, the strongest school record in the city slips by five hundredths and the hallways keep their hours

Mei Wong stands at the after-school door in Rockridge with the day's last snack count in her head. She is an after-school program aide, and her job at pickup is mostly questions. Parents ask whether their kid ate a real snack. They ask who sat with whom. She knows every answer, because the teachers here, she says, remember every child's name.

Ask her about the district's numbers and she says so plainly. "I can't speak to the enrollment numbers or the per-student funding figure — that's not something I see in my day-to-day," Wong said. "What I can tell you is what I see at pickup: parents asking if their kid ate a real snack, teachers who remember every child's name, and a community that shows up. The after-school space here has always felt tight, but it's the people who make it work. If the index slipped a little, I wouldn't be surprised if it's because we're all stretched thinner, not because the kids or the staff care any less."

The index did slip. Rockridge's school quality index reads 8.53 on the district table this cycle, down from 8.58 at C113. That is still the highest of the 22 neighborhoods. Baylight District follows at 8.24, then Piedmont Ave at 8.22. The table also puts Rockridge's graduation rate at 94.2%, college readiness at 75.03% and teacher quality at 9.1. It lists 248 students and funding of 15,000.

Enrollment moved the other way. Rockridge gained one student against C113. West Oakland gained three, Grand Lake and Chinatown two each, and Piedmont Ave and Brooklyn one each. Enrollment is the number that moves from cycle to cycle. The quality table mostly holds still, so a five-hundredths dip is the kind of movement that gets noticed.

Gloria Liu, the principal, did not treat it as an alarm. She reads it from inside the building. "A one-point drop on a scale like that — I'll be honest, it doesn't change what happens in the building on Monday morning," Liu said. "The kids still come in, the teachers still teach, and I still walk the halls. The district watches that number, sure, but I watch the kids. If anything, a dip like that makes me look closer at where we might be slipping — maybe it's a cohort thing, maybe it's a resource thing — but it doesn't mean I'm suddenly going to pull books off shelves or cut a program. We're still the strongest school in the city, and that's not because of a number. It's because of the people here."

She offered "cohort" and "resource" as possibilities, not findings. The table does not say what moved the number, and this story will not guess.

Maya Torres-Dillon teaches high school science in Rockridge, and she holds the strength and the slip in the same hand. "I read that 75% as both," she said. "It's a strength we should be proud of — that's a real number, and it means most of our kids are leaving ready for what's next. But if I'm being honest with you, as a teacher, I don't get to treat any number as a finish line. That 8.53 slipping, even a little, that's the kind of thing that gets my attention. It's not about panicking over a decimal point. It's about asking what that one student who just enrolled needs, and whether we're actually serving every kid in that building, not just the ones already on track.
