# Before the Shuttles Come In

Most of this city's morning work happens before anyone has made the coffee. This cycle I wanted to hear what that hour sounds like from the sidewalk. I started with a small detail in the record. In four neighborhoods, people watched the morning shuttles roll along the waterfront into the Baylight District, and each one timed a walk to get there first.

I'll be straight about what I have. The assignment was five walkers in five neighborhoods: Uptown, Temescal, Laurel, KONO and Glenview. I have four voices on the record. The Temescal walker didn't make it into my notebook, and I won't guess at what they'd say. What follows is what the four told me and what the ledger holds.

I can't tell you the five walkers share a route, a shift or a reason. As far as I can tell, they don't. They only have the watching in common, and what they did about it.

## Uptown: 5:30

Tre Mingo is a port worker. He starts early. "I'm up around 5:30 AM," he told me. "I'm walking toward the port, trying to beat the shuttles that head into the Baylight District."

He doesn't dress it up. "If I didn't beat them, I'd probably be stuck in the crowd, and I'd be late for my shift. It's not a big deal, but I like getting to work on time."

*It's not a big deal.* I hear that sentence from a lot of people who keep this city running. The work is the reason they're out before dawn, and they'd never call it a sacrifice.

His street is not quiet. Uptown has plenty of energy right now, and rent is the thing people keep bringing up. Some neighbors say out loud that they're planning to leave. A man who is up at 5:30 and headed to the port has his own stake in whether this block stays affordable. He didn't bring it up with me, though, and I won't put it in his mouth.

## Laurel: a bit earlier

Melo Arnez is a plumber. He works in the Baylight District too, and he moved his clock instead of his route. "I was watching the shuttles because I wanted to time my walk to beat them," he said. "I head into the Baylight District for work, and it's a pain to get stuck behind a bunch of people getting off the shuttle."

His fix was small. "Now I leave a bit earlier, and I get to where I'm going without the crowd. It's made my day a bit smoother, to be honest."

Laurel, by the record, is hushed, with little change on the surface. Melo is a good example of how a hushed neighborhood still holds a daily habit like his. He wasn't asking anyone for anything. He found a gap in the morning and stepped into it.

## KONO: the smoke

Mart Johns is a nurse aide, so his shift involves other people's mornings. He keeps a regular route and likes it. "It's a nice walk along the waterfront, and it's good exercise before my shift starts."

Then he said the thing the postcard version leaves out. "The smell of smoke from the recent blaze still lingers in some areas, but it's not too bad. I've gotten used to it. I just focus on getting to where I need to be."

The neighborhood record says the same thing in its own flat way. KONO's smell of smoke reminds everyone of the recent blaze, though life on the block carries on as usual. A newcomer from Jack London has also settled in.

I find beauty in moments like this one. A nurse aide walks to work through a faint burnt smell. He has made peace with it, and he's out there so somebody else can be cared for later.

## Glenview: who's out there

Liliana Salazar is an EV mechanic. Her answer wasn't about the crowd at all. "When I watch those shuttles go by, I think about Jacob Perez," she said. "He's a delivery driver around here, and I see him sometimes in the morning when I'm out walking."

She was careful about it, and I liked that. "It's not that I think about his route specifically, but I know he's out there, making deliveries. I guess I just wonder how his day's going."

The ledger has a delivery driver named Jacob Perez in Glenview, which fits. I did not reach him. His day is his own, and all I have is her wondering.

Glenview is described as quiet and unremarkable. Public works crews are busy on the streets, and Parkridge Cleaners is buzzing about some local spotting. I find that line comforting. Even in a quiet neighborhood, people wave at each other from a distance, and one of them keeps another in mind.

## What I take from it

*This part is my interpretation, not the record.* Four people in four neighborhoods looked at the same moving thing along the waterfront and each made a private adjustment. One left earlier, one walked toward the port, and one kept walking through the smoke. One watched the shuttles and thought about a colleague on the road.

The record doesn't tell us who runs the shuttles or who rides them. It doesn't tell us why they run when they do. I'm not going to guess. What I can say is that the people in this piece don't treat the morning as a spectacle. They treat it as a job to get to on time, and they speak plainly about it. Tre said so himself: it's not a big deal, but he likes getting to work on time.

I'll keep looking for the Temescal walker. If you're out early there, with the health center opening its doors and the block party chatter still in the air, stop me. I'll be the one with the notebook.
