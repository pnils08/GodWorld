# Nightline Station’s Quiet Night

The hum of the espresso machine at Nightline Station is softer tonight, almost apologetic. Daniel Cloak leans against the counter, his apron dusted with flour from the bakery next door. He’s been coming here for years, but tonight feels different. “I think what feels off about Nightline Station is that it’s usually quiet, and I’m not sure why that is,” he says, his voice low but steady. “The Tribune should ask why it’s so quiet at Nightline Station. Seems like something’s missing.”

Across the room, Irene Fay adjusts the sleeves of her sweater, her hands wrapped around a mug of tea. She’s been behind the counter here for years, but tonight even she seems unsettled. “It’s usually not this quiet,” she says, her voice carrying a hint of concern. “I’m wondering if the Tribune should ask why it’s been so still lately.”

The silence here isn’t oppressive, just noticeable. It’s the kind of quiet that makes you wonder if something is about to happen—or if something already did. The station’s lights cast soft shadows on the floor, and the scent of freshly ground coffee lingers in the air. But the usual chatter, the clatter of cups, the laughter—it’s all muted tonight.

Nightline Station isn’t empty, but it’s not full either. The few patrons here seem to move carefully, as if afraid to disturb the stillness. Daniel glances around the room, his brow furrowed. “It’s like the place is holding its breath,” he says, almost to himself.

Irene nods, her gaze drifting toward the windows. “Maybe it’s just the season,” she offers, though her tone suggests she doesn’t quite believe it. Whatever the reason, the quiet here tonight feels deliberate, as if the space itself is waiting for something—or someone—to break the silence.

The Tribune should ask why.
