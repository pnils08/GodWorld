# The Long View from Ninth Street: Hal Richmond and the Weight of the Ledger

In the early mornings before the trucks from the produce terminal finished backing into the narrow alleys off Webster, Hal Richmond would stand by the window of his third-floor apartment in Chinatown, waiting for the water in his kettle to come to a boil. Below him, the metal security gates of the herb shops and bakeries were already rattling upward on their oiled tracks. Red lanterns strung along the cornices caught the first gray wash of dawn coming off the estuary.

At sixty-two, with more than thirty-one years on the masthead of the *Bay Tribune*, Richmond had developed a relationship with the city of Oakland that was measured in eras rather than news cycles. His byline had introduced him as a sports reporter, then a senior columnist, and eventually as the Senior Historian—a title that conferred neither extra administrative authority nor relief from filing on deadline, but simply acknowledged what the newsroom had long known: Hal Richmond was where the paper kept its memory.

He poured the boiling water slowly over the grounds, breathing in the dark roast against the faint, familiar scent of star anise and salted cabbage drifting up from the street. On the kitchen table lay a clean legal pad, an ink pen with the chrome worn down to brass, and the morning run of the paper. In cycle Y2C52, he had caught himself disagreeing with a Tribune column out loud to an empty kitchen; in Y3C1, looking across the kitchen table at the gray light, he had found himself quietly wondering whether the work still mattered the way it used to.

That question was not born of fatigue. Richmond’s net worth sat comfortably over seven million dollars, his household was secure, and his standing across the East Bay was immovable. Rather, the question belonged to a man who had spent four decades watching Oakland build, tear down, reassemble, and celebrate itself, only to realize that the city’s official narratives moved faster than human grief or gratitude could settle.

### The Architecture of the Long Desk

The *Bay Tribune* sports desk in Downtown Oakland had never been an altar to serenity, but over the course of cycles Y2C79 through Y2C105, it had functioned as the nerve center for the most scrutinized generational transition in professional baseball history.

When the dynasty that anchored Oakland’s sporting identity began its inevitable fracture—when the farewell season of designated hitter Vinnie Keane (POP-00001) coincided with left-hander Benji Dillon (POP-00018) negotiating his final one-year pact, and the sudden mid-season trade of veteran infielder Martin Richards rearranged the dugout hierarchy—the younger writers reached for hyperbole. They filed dispatches packed with panic, contract percentages, and trade-analyzer graphics.

Richmond wrote essays.

"You learn, if you do this long enough, that farewell seasons have a particular weight to them," he had written in cycle Y2C80. "They press down on everything. The at-bats carry more. The walks to the plate carry more. Even the off-days carry more, because there are fewer of them left, and everyone in the ballpark knows the count."

When Mike Paulson (POP-00527), the forty-eight-year-old general manager of the Athletics and head of basketball operations for the expansion Oaks, sat in the Tribune’s corner interview room in cycle Y2C94, he did not face a stenographer. Richmond had spent weeks charting Paulson’s operational philosophy—the cold arbitrage of trading beloved veterans against the municipal romance of walking a championship parade.

"I’ve covered Mike Paulson for years," Richmond had noted after the GM announced his dual-franchise mandate. "I’ve heard him talk about championships, rotations, player development, and organizational philosophy. What you learn is the difference between noise and structure. Noise is loud; it fills press conferences and drives the radio callers. Structure sits in the back of the house, reviewing balance sheets, checking manifests, and ensuring the foundation holds."

That profile, alongside his deep analytical dive into Elliot Abraham’s operational machinery in cycle Y2C102, demonstrated why Richmond held a unique place in the city's press corps. While others chased the quotes, Hal chased the blueprint.

### Chinatown Rhythms

Despite the institutional weight he carried downtown, Richmond remained anchored in Chinatown. He walked its perimeter every evening, moving easily among the multigenerational households that had outlasted every zoning boom and speculative wave that crossed the East Bay flatlands.

In cycle Y3C1, crossing 9th Street near a lantern-lit storefront, he recognized a face from thirty years back—a former clubhouse attendant who now sold winter melons from a crate on the sidewalk. They did not speak of home runs or municipal bonds; they nodded with the quiet economy of two men who remembered what the waterfront looked like before the towers rose.

His neighborhood was also home to a younger contingent of Oakland’s sports and media ecosystem. Selena Grant (POP-00591), the thirty-six-year-old beat reporter who had arrived from Chicago to cover the Oakland Oaks for the *Bay Tribune*, lived only a few blocks away. Richmond occasionally crossed paths with Grant at the local bakery counter or near the market street where neighbors swapped news. Where Grant brought the sharp, energetic urgency of a mid-career beat writer tracking roster movements and press conferences, Richmond provided the ballast—the reminder that an expansion basketball team losing four straight preseason contests in cycle Y3C3 was merely the opening measure of a twenty-cycle symphony.

Down the block on 8th Street lived Jose Colon (POP-00599), the twenty-eight-year-old catcher for the Athletics. In cycle Y2C50 and Y2C51, Richmond had watched Colon navigate the quiet, unglamorous responsibilities of a big-league backstop—picking up produce, walking the perimeter of the rising Coliseum District Development (Baylight), and standing in the pharmacy line under the paper lanterns like any other resident of the district. When Colon had caught himself wondering in cycle Y3C2 whether he had become the adult in the room, Richmond had merely smiled from across the street. He had seen a dozen young catchers undergo that exact calcification.

"A catcher in Oakland never gets to be young for very long," Richmond once remarked to an editor over coffee. "The staff ages, the signs get complicated, and by thirty they look like they’ve spent a decade running a port terminal."

***

### The Ground Beneath Baylight

In cycle Y2C50, Richmond had taken an afternoon to walk the perimeter of the Baylight District construction site—the massive $2.1 billion redevelopment rising where the old Coliseum grounds met Elmhurst. The city had staged a carnival on the rebar and survey stakes for the hundredth cycle celebration, an image that had stayed with Richmond long after the music died down.

Standing by the chain-link fence, listening to the pile drivers driving steel into the marshy soil, he had tried to picture what the 35,000-seat open-air stadium and 3,200 residential units would feel like in five years. To the civic boosters and the council, Baylight was the triumph of prosperity-era planning: modern, engineered, seamless. To Richmond, who had sat in the damp concrete press box of the old stadium in 2031 when Vinnie Keane hit the home run that kept the city standing on its feet for nine full minutes, the ground carried echoes that no architectural rendering could capture.

"Keane and Dillon have been in that position for so long that most of the children in that line have never known a version of Oakland sports without them," Richmond had written in cycle Y2C100, reviewing the farewell autograph lines. "They became, gradually, part of the city’s idea of itself. Not celebrities—something more load-bearing than that."

In cycle Y3C3, walking home in the evening air past the Jack London crowds he deliberately avoided, Richmond turned the corner onto his street. The brass latch on his building's foyer door, which had stuck every winter since cycle Y2C80, had finally been repaired that morning near a lantern-lit storefront. The small mechanical click of the bolt sliding smoothly into the strike plate gave him an unexpected sense of satisfaction.

He climbed the stairs to the third floor, sat down at his desk, and uncapped his pen. The playoffs were approaching; the A's were set to host the Yankees in the ALDS; the Oaks were establishing their baseline; and the city of Oakland was preparing to turn the page once again. 

Hal Richmond opened his notebook and began to write.
