Three times this week, someone in Oakland told me something was moving, and three times I went looking for the motion.

Start with West Oakland. Marcus Webb told the Finance Committee that seventy-nine households have been stabilized in nine months, and he put it the way a man does when the auditors are in the room: families with checks in hand before the notices arrive, every dollar moving through compliance. Carmen Delaine reported the fund's pace, a little over three weeks from application to money. Mayor Santana called it "relief without bureaucracy." Those two ideas sit uncomfortably together. A fund that is proud of how well it passes scrutiny is not a fund without bureaucracy. It may be a good bureaucracy, and I'd rather have a careful one than a reckless one, but let's not pretend it's something else.

There is also a gap in the arithmetic nobody has explained. The fund was sized at $28 million. Carmen's reporting has about $81,000 in household grants out the door by the end of last cycle, and the same reporting puts the remainder at $23 million. Those figures don't reconcile. Maybe the difference is money committed but not yet paid, or money in another category. Maybe it's a reporting slip. Webb and the Finance Committee can tell us which. Denise Carter said it plainly, "When storefronts go quiet, families go next," and if that's the thesis, residents deserve a clear answer on how much of the money is actually reaching them.

Carter's line took on weight when I read Trevor Shimizu's transit reporting. Lake Merritt station lost thousands of riders this cycle, the steepest drop of the eight stations he compared, and its trains ran later than before. Jerald Jensen, who cleans that station, put it in human terms: fewer people means fewer tips, fewer smiles, fewer connections. "The lifeblood of a place starts to thin out." Trevor is careful to say the record points to fog and events but can't tell us which mattered. I'll honor that caution. What I can say is that the quiet is real to the people who work in it.

The split in Trevor's numbers is what stayed with me. Fruitvale gained riders, over thirteen hundred of them, even as its trains ran later. West Oakland held roughly steady and its on-time performance dropped six points. People are going to Fruitvale. Which brings me to Luis Navarro's column, and the sentence he caught three officials saying across three cycles. Mayor Santana at the gavel: Mayer & Associates listens to Fruitvale merchants first, designs second. Santana again in open session, nearly word for word. Elena Soria Dominguez on the hub record, with the tamale vendors and panaderías. This cycle Rose Delgado called Phase II's blueprints a "covenant." And the project is still sitting at Visioning, where it has been for three cycles.

Luis was fair about it. He didn't call anyone a liar. He said what he doesn't have: when the listening sessions happened, who sat in them, which office owns the next step. I'd add only this. The riders are arriving in Fruitvale now. The merchants have been promised they'll be heard. The people designing the future of that corridor have said the same sentence for three cycles, and a sentence repeated is not progress made.

Then there's the Oaks, where the opposite problem is playing out. Nobody is overpromising. Everyone is hoping. Talia Finch found Jessie Hess in a Dimond bar, holding her drink in both hands, wanting to believe and unable to. The expansion club finished preseason 0-5, and by Talia's count 0-9 once rookie camp is included. P Slayer caught Hess outside the arena, shoulders tight, saying she was hyped about the starting five and then watched the Heat game. P Slayer called the exits-before-the-buzzer scene "the sound of the same story starting again." That's the sharper take. Talia's is the more generous one, and I think it's the truer read: Daniel Cloak says the Oaks are genuinely reaching out to the community and that counts for something. Melton Neilon, who bakes sourdough, says the Oaks aren't what keeps him up at night. His regulars are. I wouldn't wave that off. A club can't ask people to care before it has given them a night worth retelling, and a baker's regulars have already gotten theirs.

Selena Grant's board read gets at the practical problem. Austin Reaves, AJ Dybantsa and Adash Stanley all put up lines, and Stanley had eleven assists in the finale. The lines are fine, and the record is zero wins. Her point is that the front office owes the public a decision about which production is foundational and which is situational. I'll go further: that decision is the whole job. One small thing for the record: the feed carried both a 17-point and a 20-point line for Reaves in different games, and I'd like someone to confirm which belongs where before we build anything on it.

Across the bay, the Athletics are winning and the question is why. Tanya Cruz sat in a clubhouse where John Ellis and Benji Dillon both said "electric," Ellis with tape still on his fingers, the team at 4-1 in the playoffs. Both used the word "trust." She asked whether there are undercurrents beneath the wins, and the honest answer is that nobody gave her any, which tells you either that there aren't any or that nobody's volunteering. Elliot Marbury's data desk piece on Danny Horn is the measured counterweight. Horn's 2041 sits outside anything he'd done in his five prior recorded seasons: more power, fewer swings and misses, and a full season of at-bats behind it. What Elliot can't say is whether that's rare for a center fielder, because no peer's line sits beside it. I appreciate a desk that leaves a blank blank.

Mason Ortega gave me my favorite moment of the week, and it's the smallest. Colin Phillips gets to Nino's before first light, scoops the flour, shapes the loaves, and has the racks full before the morning rush. "It's steady work, not glamorous." He's also learning pastry. I keep coming back to him because his work has what the rest of this week's talk is missing: a deliverable, on a schedule, that somebody picks up warm.

By Carmen Delaine | Bay Tribune Civic

# Nine Months, Seventy-Nine Households: The Stabilization Fund Moves at a 23-Day Pace

**By Carmen Delaine**

Marcus Webb sat in the Finance Committee chamber with a single line of data that carried the weight of displacement pressure: seventy-nine households stabilized in nine months. He framed the number not as a victory lap but as evidence of traction. "Seventy-nine households stabilized means seventy-nine Oakland families with checks in hand before the displacement notices arrive," Webb said, his tone calibrated to the auditors tracking compliance. Every dollar, he added, moves with the compliance that passes the Finance Committee's scrutiny.

The West Oakland Stabilization Fund, proposed by Mayor Avery Santana and signed into law in Cycle 78 with a 6-3 council vote, is now moving checks through the real machinery of bureaucracy. Cycle 110 marked the ninth month of active disbursement, and the Initiative_Tracker logs the arc: $400,000 approved for Month-Nine, with a 23-day average processing window achieved. The fund's total envelope was $28 million. It has disbursed, in visible tranche, $81,188 in household grants by the end of Cycle 109, and now carries $23 million remaining.

The name of the game, as framed by the officials steering it, is speed wrapped in compliance. That friction—moving cash without creating audit liability—is where the story sits this cycle.

Denise Carter, City Council District 1, had already staked her position earlier in the cycle on where that friction resolves. "When storefronts go quiet, families go next—so we're taking the Stabilization Fund to where the need is moving," she said. The language was deliberate: the fund is not a static account waiting for applications; it is a tool meant to track and intercept displacement as it moves. The implication was that West Oakland's commercial core and its residential stability are yoked.

Mayor Santana pressed the speed narrative. "Nine months in, we're proving that relief without bureaucracy keeps neighbors in their neighborhoods," she said in her cycle-110 office record. The claim nested two things: that the mechanism was working, and that other cities or prior efforts had let bureaucracy be the obstacle. Brenda Okoro, in her own statement, refined it further. "Nine months in, the Fund's design is holding—targeted relief that flows to tenants before displacement does," she said. The sequence matters—relief first, displacement second, not the other way around.

What the tracker shows: a 23-day average processing window in Month Nine. What it does not show, and what no official present offered: how many applications were received, how many were denied, what the demographic footprint of the seventy-nine households looks like, or whether the fund is keeping pace with displacement pressure in the neighborhood or falling behind it.

The Finance Committee scrutiny that Webb cited is real—it appears in the Initiative_Tracker as a control gate. The vote itself, in Cycle 78, was narrow: 6-3, with Ramon Vega voting yes, Leonard Tran voting no, and Elliott Crane voting yes remotely. The remaining seats were not named in the record supplied. The fund was designed for West Oakland, the economic policy domain was explicit, and the disbursement phase is active now.

The next scheduled action is Month-Ten disbursement review in Cycle 111. That checkpoint will show whether the 23-day window holds, whether the pace of cash flow accelerates or stalls, and whether the remaining $23 million carries the same gravity in the tenth month as it did in the ninth.

The extraction question Webb's own language raised but could not answer: Are seventy-nine households enough to hold the line in West Oakland's commercial districts before the next wave of displacement notices arrive?

By Talia Finch | Bay Tribune Sports

# The Oaks Still Have to Become Somebody’s Team

**Talia Finch | Bay Tribune Sports**

Jessie Hess is holding a drink in both hands in an unnamed bar in Dimond, watching the screen with the sound down. The room has the particular quiet of people waiting for a reason to lean forward. The Oaks have supplied the result; the people around them are still deciding whether to supply the belonging.

“Honestly, I'm still trying to process how the Oaks did in their preseason games,” Hess says. “I was really looking forward to seeing what they could do, but that loss to the Miami Heat was a letdown. I want to believe they can turn it around, but it's hard to shake off the feeling that it's going to be a tough season.”

The final preseason loss left the Oaks at 0-5 in preseason and winless at 0-9 through rookie camp and preseason. The room mood is frustrated, and the fan read is low. Those numbers sit on the screen like a tab nobody wants to pick up.

The question in Dimond is not only whether the Oaks can win. It is whether a club can ask people to care before it has given them a night they want to retell.

Daniel Cloak keeps the door open. “I still feel that way about the Oaks, they're really making an effort to be part of the community and that's something positive. I've seen it with the preseason games, they're trying to reach out and it's great to see.”

That is the second story in the room, quieter than the losses: an effort can be visible and still not be enough to make a fan. The Oaks are trying to reach the neighborhood while the neighborhood is trying to work out what, exactly, it is being reached for.

Melton Neilon has a different measure. “Honestly, I still don't really know what to make of the Oaks analysis. As a sourdough baker, I'm more focused on getting my bread just right than following sports news. I've got my regulars to think about and making sure they've got their favorite loaves. That's what keeps me up at night, not the Oaks' preseason games.”

Rick Walker has heard the talk but has not found a clean answer in it. “Honestly, I'm still trying to keep up with the news like everyone else, but it's tough when the team's performance is all over the place. I've heard some folks talking about Oaks analysis around the neighborhood, seems like people are trying to make sense of what's going on with the team.”

Wilson Shepard has reassured that the full team has yet to suit up as one. That leaves the Oaks with an opening, but not yet an attachment. Can the first games turn effort into a reason to come back?

The screen keeps glowing above the bar. Hess lowers her drink. Nobody cheers. Nobody leaves.

By Luis Navarro | Bay Tribune Civic

## Three Cycles, Same Sentence

Rose Delgado keeps a set of rolled blueprints behind her desk at the District 3 office, and she still talks about Fruitvale Transit Hub Phase II like it's about to move. "Phase II's blueprints belong to the merchants who'll live with them—we're signing more than a contract, we're signing a covenant," she told her office's record this cycle. It's the kind of line you say when something is close. The tracker on the initiative says otherwise. Visioning for Phase II sits in design-phase, and it has not advanced in three cycles.

That gap — between the language of motion and the absence of it — is the story this cycle.

Start with what the firm's name is doing in three different mouths. Mayor Avery Santana, at the gavel: "Mayer & Associates gets the nod because they listen to Fruitvale merchants first, design second." Santana again, in open session, almost word for word: "Mayer & Associates listens to Fruitvale merchants first, designs second — and now we move to blueprints." And Elena Soria Dominguez, on the transit hub record itself: "Mayer & Associates doesn't just draw blueprints—they listen to the tamale vendors and panaderías that make Fruitvale Fruitvale."

Three officials, three cycles, one sentence. The firm listens first, designs second. Fruitvale merchants are heard. And still the row on the initiative tracker hasn't moved past Visioning.

I'm not calling that a lie. I'm calling it a pattern worth writing down. When the same assurance gets repeated instead of updated, the repetition itself becomes the record. Delgado's covenant language is the newest version of it — "more than a contract" — delivered at the same stage the project was sitting at when Santana first used nearly identical words about the same firm listening to the same merchants.

What I don't have is a timestamp on when Mayer & Associates' listening sessions happened, who in Fruitvale sat in them, or what in those blueprints changed after. Nobody's record puts a date on when "design second" becomes design — period. The quotes describe a posture. They don't describe a deliverable.

I also don't have a named office that owns the next step, or anyone on record explaining why three cycles of the same framing haven't produced a Preliminary Schematics review that holds. Delgado calls it a covenant. Santana calls it blueprints, twice, in nearly the same breath across two different sessions. Soria Dominguez calls it tamale vendors and panaderías. None of them call it stalled. The tracker does.

Fruitvale is still waiting on a transit hub whose design phase has outlasted three officials' worth of the same sentence about listening first. The sentence hasn't changed. Has anything behind it?

# The Quiet at Lake Merritt Is Showing Up in the System

**By Trevor Shimizu**

At Lake Merritt station, Jerald Jensen works through a quieter public place. He knows the difference between fog that merely changes the view and a thinner stream of people moving through the station. This cycle, the station recorded 5,896 riders, down 2,588 from the previous cycle. Its on-time performance also slipped, to 84 percent from 86 percent.

Jensen, a janitor, said the change is visible beyond the count.

> “You know, I’ve been hearing chatter about Lake Merritt station being quieter lately. It’s not just the fog—though that doesn’t help. People are talking about how it’s hitting the transit workers hard. Less foot traffic means fewer tips, fewer smiles, fewer connections. It’s tough when the lifeblood of a place starts to thin out.”

That is the human reading of a technical condition: fewer riders do not create an empty station, but they alter the work around the trains. The people who clean, direct, serve and maintain a station encounter the system at its edges. A ridership decline becomes fewer brief exchanges, fewer small transactions and fewer reasons for a person to remain in the space.

Lake Merritt moved down more than any other station in the cycle’s eight-station comparison. The change did not arrive with a single reported closure or interruption. The available record identifies fog and events in Lake Merritt and Chinatown, but it does not establish which of those conditions mattered most. The station’s numbers show the result, not the cause.

The broader pattern is uneven. MacArthur lost 2,034 riders and fell six points in on-time performance, to 78 percent. Coliseum lost 1,701 riders and fell seven points, to 76 percent. Rockridge lost 1,699 riders and fell three points, to 75 percent. At 19th St Oakland, ridership fell 999 and on-time performance fell seven points, to 79 percent. At 12th St Oakland City Center, the decline was smaller—363 riders—with on-time performance down four points, to 81 percent.

But Fruitvale moved in the other direction. It gained 1,321 riders, reaching 11,735, even as on-time performance fell three points to 78 percent. West Oakland gained 45 riders, to 8,680, while its on-time performance fell six points to 75 percent.

That split is the important infrastructure fact. The system did not simply become less used everywhere. Across the eight stations and ten corridors measured, total ridership fell to 91,564 from 99,582. The station-level changes point to a network that is carrying different kinds of pressure in different places. Lake Merritt’s decline is the sharpest, but Fruitvale’s increase prevents a clean explanation based only on fog or general movement.

For workers, the distinction may not make the platform feel less quiet. Jensen’s observation places the cost in the space between the official measures. On-time performance can describe whether service is arriving within its target. Ridership can describe how many people pass through. Neither number captures the social density that Jensen describes: the smiles, tips and connections that make a station more than a transfer point.

The next question is operational rather than dramatic. If Lake Merritt’s lower ridership continues, what will distinguish a temporary thinning from a change in how the station is being used? The current numbers show the condition. They do not yet show its duration, its cause or what transit workers will be asked to absorb next.

## DATA DESK: THE HORN LINE

Danny Horn's 2041 season carries one number that outranks every other mark on his nine-year dossier: a home run every 11.1 at-bats, worked from a full 621-at-bat season of 148 games. It is the densest power rate the Athletics center fielder has posted in the six seasons for which full lines are on record, and it did not arrive at the cost of swinging more. His strikeout rate that same year — one every 9.6 at-bats — is likewise the loosest of the six, meaning 2041 is the first season in which Horn hit for more power and swung through less at the same time, at a sample size too large to dismiss as a hot month.

The dossier lists nine seasons for Horn; six carry full lines on this record, running 2036 through 2041. The other three are not part of this slice and are not filled in here — they stay blank rather than estimated.

Read in order, the six seasons form a climb and then a break. 2036 was thin by his own standard: 323 at-bats, a home run every 26.9 trips and a strikeout every 4.1 — the shape of a player still finding at-bats, not a full season's identity. The four years that followed established a working range. 2037 sat at one home run every 18.1 at-bats with strikeouts every 5.4. 2038 ran 21.1 and 5.6. 2039, his best of the middle stretch, tightened to 14.3 and 5.9 across 614 at-bats — 198 hits, 43 home runs, a .322 average. 2040 loosened back out to 19.0 and 5.0 over 607 at-bats, with strikeouts climbing to 121, the highest total on the six-year line even though the rate stayed inside his established band.

Then 2041: 621 at-bats, 210 hits, 56 home runs, 113 runs batted in, 80 stolen bases, a .338 average, an .715 slugging mark. Every rate on the card moved in the same direction at once — more power, fewer empty swings, more hits falling in, more bases taken. No season between 2036 and 2040 approaches that combination at that at-bat count. That is the hard claim the record carries: 2041 sits outside the range Horn established across his previous five recorded seasons, and it did so on a full year's workload, not a partial one.

No peer center fielder's season line is part of this record, so this memo cannot say whether Horn's 2041 density is ordinary or rare for the position this cycle — that comparison is blank here, not answered.

The cycle's feed entry adds a small, separate data point: one home run and two runs batted in logged this cycle, in playoff action, with the feed's own notes indicating Horn had struggled across the season before that stretch. That note describes the lead-up to the number, not the season total above, and it is kept separate from the 2041 full-season line rather than folded into it.

What would kill the claim that 2041 is a new level rather than a one-year outlier is specific: a 2042 line that falls back inside the 2037–2040 band — a home run rate drifting back toward one every 14 to 21 at-bats, a strikeout rate sliding back toward one every 5 to 6 — would put 2041 where 2036 already sits, as the outlier year on an otherwise steady six-season record, not the new shape of Danny Horn's game.

# The Oaks’ First Board Read Is a Blank One

**Selena Grant | Bay Tribune Sports**

Austin Reaves finished with 17 points and five assists, while AJ Dybantsa had 22 points and six rebounds. The Oaks still lost their fourth straight preseason game, leaving the expansion franchise at 0-4.

That result established the first problem with reading this roster through individual production alone. The scoring lines are present. The record is not. Reaves supplied a creation line, and Dybantsa supplied the larger scoring total, but the Oaks’ board still showed another loss. For a first-year club, that distinction matters: a promising player line can describe development without yet describing a functioning team.

The final preseason game made the same point in a different arrangement. Adash Stanley finished with eight points and 11 assists. Dybantsa had 11 points. The feed also recorded a 20-point line for Austin Reaves. The Oaks lost again, this time to finish 0-5 and winless as a franchise between rookie camp and preseason.

The useful question is therefore not whether the Oaks have players capable of producing numbers. The record already answers that. The question is whether those numbers connect across games. Stanley’s 11 assists point to distribution; Reaves’ scoring lines point to a primary offensive role; Dybantsa’s 22-point and 11-point games show a young scorer whose output moved from one result to the next. None of those facts, by themselves, explains how the lineup is supposed to hold together.

That is the expansion-board read from Dimond: the club has individual evidence, but no winning evidence yet. The five-game record does not prove that the roster cannot work. It does establish that the current build has not converted its best statistical lines into a result. The franchise’s stability remains uncertain because the record offers no settled answer about which production is foundational and which is situational.

The matchup against the club where Paulson ran the front office last season adds an unavoidable layer to the ledger. The Oaks lost that game, too. The significance is not that one opponent can define an expansion team; it is that the loss leaves the organization with no preseason result to counter the cold start.

A turnaround begins with a decision the front office still owes the record: which number is the starting point for the build? Is it Stanley’s distribution, Reaves’ scoring, Dybantsa’s ceiling, or a structure that has not yet appeared in the results? Until the answer produces something beyond another individual line, the Oaks remain 0-5, and the key to the turnaround is still an open question.
