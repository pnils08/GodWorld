# Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship Keeps Its Doors Open Downtown

Eunice Marston has the habit of stopping in the aisle before she sits down. On a weeknight at Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship, she walks the length of the sanctuary first, past the rows of folding chairs, and only then takes a seat near the front. Her hands are full of paper she has not yet read. The building is quiet in the way a room is quiet after a day of use, and she seems to be listening for what is left of it.

"As a faith leader, I believe our congregation's role in the community during times of crisis is to offer support and be present for those affected," Marston said. "We've seen this in action at Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship, where we've opened our doors to provide a sense of community and comfort."

The record shows the fellowship doing exactly that. In C107, the congregation opened its doors for community support, with 72 people in attendance. In C109, it held a holy day observance for Epiphany, and 237 people came. Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship is a Protestant congregation in Downtown, with a membership of 450, founded 189 years ago. It is a historic mainline congregation with a social justice focus, and it is the one on this beat that Marston leads today.

Across the street from the fellowship's own work, the neighborhood has its own version of the same thing. Amir Delia, a home health aide who lives and works Downtown, was walking past the building recently when he heard singing from inside. "I saw a choir performance at Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship when I walked by the other day," he said. "It was nice to see people gathered like that."

He did not go in. He kept walking. The sound followed him for half a block, and that was the whole of his encounter with the place. It is a small thing, and it is also the kind of thing a neighborhood notices.

Imie Calero, a civil rights attorney who works in Downtown, has been watching the same activity from the other direction. "I've seen Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship hosting events in our neighborhood, like a choir performance that drew a small crowd," Calero said. "It's great to see community activities happening around here."

Calero's words put the fellowship inside the neighborhood's daily life rather than behind its walls. The choir was not a program announced to the city. It was a sound that reached people on the sidewalk, and it drew a crowd that was small, but present.

The fellowship is not the only congregation on the record doing this kind of work this cycle. Bay Pure Land Buddhist Temple in Chinatown opened its doors for community support in C109, with 41 people attending. Cathedral of the Living Word in Lake Merritt marked Epiphany in C107, C108 and C109, with attendance of 1,185, 1,077 and 1,265 on those three observances. St. Esperanza Parish in Fruitvale recorded 427 people for traditional Epiphany rituals in C107 and 374 for its Epiphany celebration in C108. Downtown also has the Lake Merritt Mindfulness Sangha, a Buddhist community that meets in the same part of the city as the fellowship.

Marston's sentence about other faith communities in the area is the one she returns to. "I think it's essential for faith organizations to be available and welcoming, just as we've observed other faith communities doing in our area." She does not say the congregations coordinate with one another, and the record does not say they do. What the record shows is several rooms in the city's faith life opening their doors at roughly the same time, with people walking past them.

Marston closes the folding-chair row on her way out, and the light from the street comes through the windows and stops short of the pews. The record can say how many came through the doors and what the congregation named its days. It cannot say what a person carried out of a room like this, or what the room asked of them after. Which leaves the question the beat keeps returning to: how can the faith community continue to support each other and the broader community in the face of ongoing challenges?
