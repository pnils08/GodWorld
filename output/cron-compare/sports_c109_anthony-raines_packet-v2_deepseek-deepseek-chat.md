**Pablo Almanzar’s Commanding Seven and the Architecture of October Wins**  
*By Anthony Raines*  

The first thing that jumps out when you look at Pablo Almanzar’s Game 3 line is the seven strikeouts against just two earned runs over seven innings. That’s not just a quality start—it’s the kind of efficiency that shortens bullpen meetings and extends playoff series. The right-hander scattered five hits, never letting the Phillies string together momentum, while his catcher Jose Colon and infielder Ernesto Quintero provided the necessary offense with a home run each in a 4-2 victory.  

“Man, Pablo was locked in,” Colon said afterward, his shin guards still streaked with dirt. “Seven strong innings, just two runs? That’s the kind of performance that wins you games in October.”

What stands out isn’t the velocity (the dossier lists his fastball sitting 92-94 mph) but the command. Seven strikeouts against zero walks suggests a pitcher who leveraged his full repertoire—the slider especially—to keep hitters guessing without falling behind.  

Small samples in October always warrant caution. One start doesn’t rewrite a pitcher’s identity, and Almanzar isn’t suddenly an ace because of seven strong innings. But variance tends to show its teeth in walks and hard contact, neither of which materialized here. When a pitcher with his profile—he’s not a flamethrower, not a groundball artist—generates that many swings and misses without free passes, it’s usually a sign of sequencing and location, not luck.  

The question now is whether the A’s can bank on this version of Almanzar in a potential Game 7. His regular-season logs don’t suggest sudden reinvention—his true breakout would require sustaining this command over multiple starts—but October has room for pitchers who peak at the right time. If he can replicate even 80% of this efficiency in another outing, the A’s rotation suddenly looks deeper than its paper projections.  

For one night at least, the math was simple: seven innings, two runs, and a blueprint for stealing playoff games without overpowering stuff. That’s architecture, not accident.
