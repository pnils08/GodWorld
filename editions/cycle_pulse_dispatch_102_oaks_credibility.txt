Before a Game Is Played, the Oaks Take a Hit — and the Man in Charge Finally Answers

By Anthony Raines | Bay Tribune Sports

The Oaks took Draymond Green in the expansion draft, then traded him to Portland for Jerami Grant days later — and this past cycle, on his own podcast, he said the organization is garbage, that players don't respect its head of basketball operations as a basketball man, and that the man in question, Mike Paulson, should stick to baseball.

Neither Paulson nor Elliot Abraham, the general manager Paulson brought over from his own staff in Chicago this past cycle, has said a word about it. That silence held through an arena announcement that added its own weight to the cycle: the new building in the Baylight District will not be ready until after the All-Star break, the Oaks will play their inaugural stretch at Oakland Arena in the meantime, and owner Elias Varek said the team is weighing naming rights among local businesses. That search has nothing to do with the naming rights already sold on the new Athletics stadium — Civis Systems, the urban-intelligence firm Varek founded, holds those. Two buildings, two deals, and the owner was careful this cycle to keep them apart even while little else about the operation stayed tidy.

Varek was not careful about defending his front office. Asked about Green's charge, he waved it off as noise — "some kid with a mic," not a threat to an operation that is, in his framing, still pouring concrete. "Front offices don't fight in the alley," he said. "They build the damn arena." But he did not stop there. Unprompted, he went further, drawing a line most owners don't draw in public: "If that exec can't take a punch? He shouldn't be in the room. But that's between him and me." Nobody asked him whether his own head of basketball operations could take a public hit like this one. He raised the question himself, and answered it by measuring whether the operation could survive a punch rather than closing ranks around it. That is daylight, and Varek put it there himself, in print, before his franchise has played a game that counts.

The daylight is the story, and it did not open this cycle. This paper has been tracing the gap since the tech-expo panel where Varek first floated hiring the Athletics' general manager to run his new team — "I will absolutely be making that phone call," he said then. Paulson's answer at the time was a flat denial: "I have not received that call." This paper kept asking. It was only later, cornered by the assembled press in a Minnesota clubhouse after an A's loss, that Paulson's account of the call changed — and shrank — to exactly two words: "we spoke." Whatever was actually said in that call has never been confirmed by either man since. It sat there for cycles as the one specific, unconfirmed thing at the center of an otherwise well-covered expansion story — a courtship nobody would put on the record, a hire nobody would describe. It was still sitting there when Baylight's own director, Keisha Ramos, took three cycles to formally name Varek's conflict of interest — Oaks ownership stacked on Civis equity stacked on Baylight equity stacked on the Athletics' stadium naming rights — and built a four-part recusal wall around it. It was still sitting there when Paulson, in his first offseason running the Oaks, traded the fifth pick and Herbert Jones and an unprotected first-round pick three years out to move up for AJ Dybantsa — the kind of move a front office makes when it is treating its inaugural draft class like Year Four of an established program, not Year One of a new one. And it was still sitting there when Abraham arrived from Chicago with nothing on the public record about how that year actually went for him beyond Paulson's own account of it. A two-city front office built on trust between two specific people works exactly as well as that trust holds. Green didn't invent any of that. He said it crudely, on a podcast, from Portland. This paper had already written most of it down.

What changed this cycle is that Paulson answered — not on the show, but to this paper, on the record, at length, for the first time since those two words in the clubhouse. "Look I appreciate Draymond is unhappy with how things may have played out but we were up front about the situation, he is in Portland now and is a future hall of famer." No counterpunch. The man who called his franchise garbage gets called a future Hall of Famer in return. On the respect question, Paulson didn't claim any: "Regarding the respect, that's something I try to earn each day and don't expect it to be given to me. Sustained success gets you respect." And on the charge itself, the only honest answer an expansion team that hasn't played a game yet can give: "Now The Oaks garbage? I guess we will see......" Set next to two words in a clubhouse and a phone call nobody would confirm, that is a man who has stopped declining to answer. It reads like restraint because it is restraint — not a clapback, its opposite.

Not everyone in Jack London Square is waiting on the front office to sort this out. Blair Patel, a ship repair foreman who has spent enough winters on the estuary to watch a "revitalization project" stretch like his grandmother's taffy, doesn't have a basketball opinion so much as a patience problem. "I don't care if they play in a damn parking lot — just win games, pay people fairly, and stop acting like these shiny new buildings are doing us favors. Half the folks who work the docks with me can't afford to step foot in those arenas anyway." Of Green: "Nothing Oakland loves more than somebody who talks reckless and backs it up. But if he's just flapping his—"

The Oaks have not played a game. They have already had their credibility tested in public, by a man they traded away days after they drafted him, and the answer their head of basketball operations gave was not denial. It was a daily accounting he says he hasn't earned yet, and a promise, unfinished on purpose, that the rest remains to be seen.
