# Chinatown Has the Most Sick Residents in the City. The Hospital Ledger Names None of Them.

**Dr. Lila Mezran, Health**

Akua Adeyemi works quietly in Chinatown. She is a plumber, and she says the job has taught her to read a house before she opens her toolbox.

"I go into a lot of homes, and you can tell pretty quick when someone's down," she said. "It's not the mess or the dishes — it's the quiet. The TV off in the middle of the day. A chair pulled up by the window where somebody's been sitting a long time. Sometimes the medicine gets moved to the kitchen counter so it's right there where someone'll remember it. And you learn to work quiet, not to bang pipes, because somebody's sleeping in the next room."

She says she sees more of this in Chinatown than anywhere else she works. The neighborhood demographics row for cycle 115 is consistent with what she describes. Chinatown has 135 sick residents out of 2,205, which is 6.1%, at or over the 6% watch bar. That is the highest count of any neighborhood in the city. The row puts it two lower than cycle 114. East Oakland is next at 117, then Ivy Hill at 115, Fruitvale at 109, and Laurel and San Antonio at 107 each. Citywide, the ledger counts 1,771 sick residents across 22 neighborhoods.

A drop of two does not change the clinical picture. A neighborhood that sits over its watch bar for another cycle is still a neighborhood under strain.

## What the care trail shows

The care census follows the count from illness to the hospital bed. For Chinatown, 135 sick residents led to 3 admissions this cycle. At the close, 6 Chinatown residents were in care and 6 beds were occupied. The census lists none of them as tracked. All six are "other residents," meaning people the record counts but does not name.

The comparison neighborhoods look much the same. Adams Point has 82 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Dimond has 90 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. East Oakland has 117 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Eastlake has 86 sick, 2 admissions and 4 in care. Citywide there were 34 admissions this cycle, none tracked. At the close, 69 people were in care and 69 beds were occupied. Thirty-five people left. The cycle summary puts the city's illness rate at 4.0%. That figure is the model's citywide dial. It is not a Chinatown number.

The named rows are few. Four residents appear on the hospital and cause records, and none lives in Chinatown. Yu Zen of Rockridge was admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. Deacon Seymour of Lake Merritt was admitted the same cycle after a workplace accident. Maurice Franklin of Rockridge was admitted in cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu. The cycle summary's recovering list names Howard Travis, who caught up on sleep and woke up feeling like himself again on Piedmont Ave.

I won't read more into those rows than they hold. A name on the hospital ledger means a person is known to the record. It does not mean that person is sicker, or more deserving of attention, than someone without one.

## What the record cannot say

The record cannot say where Chinatown's other sick residents are being cared for, or how. It cannot say whether the gap between 135 sick residents and 6 occupied beds is mostly milder illness, or care that happens elsewhere, or people who have not sought care at all. The sheets don't answer that, and I am not going to guess.

Adeyemi describes what she sees in the houses she works in. "People here take care of their own — you'll see a neighbor bring soup, or a cousin come by after their shift. It's not always the hospital that carries the load. It's the family that rearranges the whole house around one sick person."

Seymour has been on the other side of the door. He spent time in a hospital bed himself, and the ledger confirms his admission. "I know what it's like to have your name on the door, to have people checking on you," he said. "But I also know that bed I was in—that was one bed, and there are a lot more filled by folks nobody's naming. Chinatown's carrying more sickness than anywhere else in this city, and if the ledger doesn't show them, that doesn't mean they're not there. It means nobody's counting. And when nobody's counting, people get missed. That's the part that keeps me up at night."

A hospital ledger is built to follow people who come through a hospital door. A neighborhood count is built to show how many are sick on a block. Chinatown sits where the two stop lining up: the most illness in the city, and a care trail that names no one.

## The question that stays open

If the person recovering in a Chinatown front room is never admitted, never named and never tracked, who sees them? Who checks that the medicine on the kitchen counter is the right one, and that the cousin coming after a shift is not the only one coming?

The record names no one from Chinatown on the hospital ledger. What would it take for the neighborhood's sick to be seen, counted and cared for by name? The Packet does not say, and no office on the record has yet offered an answer.
