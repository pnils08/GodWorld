# Chinatown's 137 Sick: Six Beds Hold Part of the Count, Kitchens Hold the Rest

Akua Adeyemi's work takes her into other people's kitchens and bathrooms. A plumber in Chinatown sees the inside of a household, and she says the signs of a sick person in the house are rarely spoken.

"I see it in the little things, honestly," Adeyemi said. "A bottle of cough syrup moved from the bathroom to the kitchen counter, so it's right there where someone'll remember it. Or a chair pulled up by the window, where someone's been sitting a long time, just watching the street. Sometimes the whole house gets rearranged — the bed moved downstairs, or the living room turned into a bedroom, because stairs got too hard. You don't have to ask. You just see it."

The record for cycle 114 gives the size of what she is describing. Chinatown carries the most sick residents of any neighborhood in the city: 137, out of 2,213 residents. That is 6.2%, at or over the 6% watch bar. It is three fewer than the cycle before. East Oakland is next at 119, down two. Ivy Hill has 118, also down two.

A smaller number sits beside the 137. The hospital census for Chinatown shows 3 admissions this cycle. Six residents were in care at the close, and six beds were occupied. Three residents left care. None of the six is a tracked resident. The record holds them as other residents, with no names and no stories, so this paper has none to offer.

The previous cycle's census row for Chinatown reads the same way: 3 admissions, 6 in care at the opening and 6 at the close, 3 exits. That is a steady, low flow. The sick count is large and the bed count is small. The census does not say how the two relate, and this paper will not guess.

Other neighborhoods follow the same pattern at smaller scale. Adams Point has 84 sick residents, 2 admissions and 4 beds occupied. Dimond has 93 sick, 2 admissions and 4 beds. Downtown has 75 sick, 2 admissions and 4 beds. East Oakland has 119 sick, 2 admissions and 4 beds. Citywide, 35 people were admitted this cycle, 70 were in care at the close and 70 beds were occupied. Thirty-six left.

Jax Caldera, a freelance accountability writer who lives in Chinatown, has watched what happens in the space between those figures. He does not claim to explain the ledger. He describes the block.

"Honestly, I see people push through more than they go in," Caldera said. "It's not stubbornness — it's that the hospital's not close, and a lot of folks here work jobs where taking a day off means losing pay or losing a shift. I've watched neighbors ride out fevers with herbal tea and a heating pad because the alternative was a bill they couldn't explain. The ones who do go in are usually past the point of choice. That's the part that gets me — by the time someone here decides to see a doctor, it's not a precaution anymore, it's an emergency."

Those are one neighbor's observations. The census records who was admitted, not who stayed home, and it cannot test what Caldera describes. But his account, Adeyemi's and the census row all point at the same blank. The hospital count records the people who came through a door. It has no line for people who are sick in a bed downstairs, minded by a relative.

Yu Zen, a commercial insurance broker who lives in Rockridge, knows the other side of that door. The hospital ledger lists him as in hospital care, admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. He has thought about who stands behind a patient.

"When I was in that hospital bed after my accident, I learned something about who shows up for you," Yu said. "It's not the building with the big sign — it's the people. Chinatown's got the most sick right now, and I know that neighborhood doesn't have the hospital footprint the rest of us take for granted. But I've seen how those communities work — the families, the shopkeepers, the elders checking on each other. That's where the caring happens when the beds are full. I think about that every morning now when I walk past the hospital cart for coffee. The city's at 70 beds, all full — that's not a number to me anymore. That's 70 people hoping someone shows up for them the way my family showed up for me."

Yu is one of four residents named on the hospital and cause records this cycle. Deacon Seymour of Lake Merritt was also admitted in cycle 105 after a workplace accident. Maurice Franklin of Rockridge was admitted in cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu. None of the four lives in Chinatown, and none of the named rows touches its sick count.

The sick count has fallen three since last cycle. That is movement in the right direction, and it is slow. The record does not say whether the people who dropped off the count recovered, moved away or were admitted. A drop of three against a base of 137 says little about a household's week.

Interesting is doctor-speak for concerning, and here the interesting figure is the gap. The sheet counts 137 sick residents and 6 beds. Three people were admitted, and no row says who looked after the other 134. Chinatown's households, shopkeepers and elders are doing work that no census line captures.

Several records are still missing. No record shows how many of the 137 were seen by any clinic at all. None shows how many missed shifts or pay to stay home. None shows whether any Chinatown resident delayed care and was admitted later. Until the city counts the people providing care at home, the gap between 137 and 6 stays unmeasured. The question stays with the Health Department and the hospital: who does the caring, and is it being counted?
