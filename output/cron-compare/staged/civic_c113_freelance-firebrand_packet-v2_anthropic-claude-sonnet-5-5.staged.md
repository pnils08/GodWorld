# Chinatown's Lunch Spot Is Shut, a Medical Team Is on the Sidewalk, and the Roster Has Nothing to Say

I was standing at a Chinatown corner when the medical team went past me. Timmie Hall was already on the curb. The lunch spot behind her was dark, with a notice taped to the door.

"I've noticed some changes around here, like the lunch spot on the corner being closed suddenly," Hall told me. "And just now, there was a medical emergency on the sidewalk. It's not usually like that here."

Hall is a refugee resettlement case worker and lives and works in Chinatown. She knows what usual looks like here, and a small crowd gathered around a stranger on the pavement isn't it.

Two doors down I found Mei Chen, who runs a gallery in the neighborhood. She had walked to the same lunch spot and found the same notice. "To be honest, it's a bit worrying to see some of our local businesses struggling," she said. "I've been going there for years. The medical emergency on the sidewalk doesn't help the vibe either."

Then she said the part I keep chewing on. "I'm hoping the Oakland Alternative Response Initiative continues to make a positive impact, it's been great to see residents feeling safer."

I have the dispatch roster for that program. It names three places: West Oakland, Fruitvale and East Oakland. Chinatown isn't one of them. Chen is hoping for help from a program whose roster doesn't list her block. She may be right that it's working somewhere. I can't tell you it's working here.

Now the numbers. The city's neighborhood map has Chinatown's sentiment at -0.06, which ranks 20th of 22 neighborhoods. The citywide median is 0.03. Housing pressure is 1.5, ranked 14th of 22, against a citywide median of 0. Retail vitality reads 7.78, ninth of 22 and above the 7.165 median. The audit that flagged this neighborhood recorded retail vitality down 2.07.

So the map is fighting itself. Retail ranks above the median while a lunch spot closes overnight with a notice taped up. The same row labels Chinatown's trajectory "growth," with a start of C112. Sentiment sits near the bottom of the city and housing pressure sits above its middle, while the label says growth.

Someone picked that word, and I'd like to know who.

The audit also says no active initiative, or anything that works as one, owns this break. That means none of the programs in play claims Chinatown's slide. West Oakland has a stabilization fund and a tenant defense fund. Grand Lake has legislation filed. Ivy Hill and Dimond have announcements. Chinatown has a lunch spot with paper on the door.

I asked two people in Glenview what they'd heard. Neither has been to Chinatown lately. Liliana Salazar is an EV mechanic. She told me, "I've seen the news about Chinatown, and to be honest, it doesn't surprise me." She said it "feels like it's been on a downward slide for a while" and called it "a shame, really."

Wren Choi, a youth mentor, was more pointed. "It sounds like it's not doing great," Choi said. "I don't know what's going on there, but it makes me wonder if anyone is paying attention to it."

Two people across the city haven't set foot here and can already tell the neighborhood is sliding. The city's own records say the same thing. The people paid to answer have said nothing I can print.

The map puts Chinatown in District 2, which is Leonard Tran's seat on the council, and Tran is an independent. I can't say whether anyone has told him about this corner. I can say nothing in the record names an office that owns the slide.

Hall watched the medical team work and then went back to her day. The notice stayed on the door.

The map says decay, and the program roster doesn't answer it. Who is responsible for addressing the decay in Chinatown?
