#### **Temescal's 314 Students Stayed Put, and the Table Ranks Them Last**
*By Angela Reyes, Education Desk*

At 3:05 p.m., the front doors of Temescal’s school open and the hallway empties toward the street. **Jun Leu**, a student there, walks home without carrying the district's metrics in his head:
> *"A typical day is pretty normal, I guess. I walk to school, sit through classes, eat lunch with my friends, try to pay attention when the teacher's talking... I don't really think about numbers or ratings or whatever. I just know my teachers show up and try, and most kids are just trying to get through the day like anywhere else."*

On the district's school quality table, Temescal sits at **6.3**—the lowest of the city's 22 neighborhoods, dipping slightly from 6.36 last cycle. While enrollment moved in other neighborhoods—West Oakland and East Oakland each gained three students, Glenview gained two, and Rockridge and Piedmont Avenue added one each—Temescal’s enrollment held perfectly flat at **314 students**. Roughly 84 percent graduate, 54 percent test college-ready, and per-student funding sits at \$11,500 with teacher quality at 6.84. By comparison, Rockridge sits at the summit above 8.4 with 249 students, graduation above 94 percent, and \$15,000 in funding.

**Patricia Nolan**, who taught for thirty years and now lives in Temescal, sees what the score flattens:
> *"I see the same kids I always saw. The ones who show up early to help the janitor, the ones who pass notes in the back, the ones who ask the question that stops the whole lesson because they actually want to know. The index doesn't see any of that. It sees a number... That 6.3 — it doesn't tell you about the girl who reads to her little brother every night because their mom works late, or the boy who fixes the classroom door hinge because he notices it's loose. Those kids are there, all 314 of them, and they're not flat numbers."*

**Rick Walker**, a K-8 teacher and former restaurant line cook, sees the staff-side burden:
> *"I see that 6.3 and I think about what it means to teach in a place that isn't growing. When the district puts money and attention where the numbers are moving, a flat school just... sits there. Those 314 kids and their teachers aren't a statistic to me — they're showing up every day doing the work with less, and nobody's knocking on their door asking what they need."*
