# Uptown's Two Speeds: The Block Moves Faster Than the Store

*By Jordan Velez, Business Desk*

Cedric Nakamura works the counter at Night Walk Records, a six-person shop in Uptown. On the business ledger this cycle the store took in $641,000 over its run, up $933 from last cycle. On the same blocks, Oakland Tech Collective, the coworking hub where Priyanka Raman is principal, carries 62 employees and $60.7 million in revenue. It added $154,000 in the same stretch.

Both numbers are going up. That is the good news, and it is real. What the record does not settle is whether they are rising the same way.

"From behind the counter, you see two different crowds," Nakamura said. "The folks walking past on Telegraph are mostly new — younger, on their phones, heading to the tech offices or the pop-ups. The ones who actually come in are the regulars, the ones who've been here since before the rent signs went up. We get a few curious walk-ins, sure, but the people who buy records are the same faces I've seen for years. The block's moving faster than the store is, if that makes sense."

It makes sense to anyone who has stood behind a register while the sidewalk changed outside the glass. A record shop lives on people who stop. Foot traffic only counts when it walks through the door.

**The Ledger, Block by Block**

Uptown's ledger this cycle names seven workers at four businesses. Read it from the top and the shape is plain.

At the top are the offices. Oakland Tech Collective shows growth of 13.2 percent. Its roster includes cybersecurity analyst Dexter Patel, IT support specialist Derek Obi, writer Michael Creighton and Sarah Corliss, a capacity analyst with DigitalOcean, plus six more. Ridgeline Studio, the architecture firm led by principal Naomi Castellanos, has 61 employees and $32.6 million in revenue. It shows growth of 14.7 percent and added $92,000 since last cycle.

Below them are the rooms that feed the block. Art Walk Cafe, the cafe-gallery, has 30 employees, $2.9 million in revenue and growth of 8 percent. It added $4,000 this cycle. Gallery Night Lounge also has 30 employees and growth of 8 percent, with $3.2 million in revenue and a $5,000 gain. Night Walk Records shows growth of 7.6 percent.

Nobody on this list is shrinking, which is not true everywhere. Across town, Ivy Hill Market shed a worker since last cycle and its growth has turned negative, down 3.7 percent. Uptown is not that story.

But the small shops are growing at roughly half the rate of the offices. Their dollar gains are counted in thousands, not hundreds of thousands. Over a few cycles, that gap is the difference between a block that rises together and a block where the people bringing in the money are not the people pouring the coffee.

**The Back of the House**

Brady Lopez washes dishes at Art Walk Cafe. He sees the block's mood in the sink before anyone sees it on a spreadsheet.

"Things have picked up some, sure," Lopez said. "The lunch rush gets longer, and the coffee machine doesn't stop. But I'm in the back, elbow-deep in suds. I see the plates coming in, not the balance sheets. The bigger places, they've got their own rhythm, their own crowds. I just keep up with the dishes. It's a good job, steady. It pays the bills, and that's what matters."

Steady is a word people use when they have seen the alternative. A longer lunch rush means more plates. Whether it means more hours, a raise or another person in the back is a question the cafe's ledger line cannot answer. It can only report that the room grew a little.

**The View From the Other Side**

Derek Obi fixes laptops and network drops at Oakland Tech Collective. He walks the same sidewalk as Lopez and Nakamura, and he knows which side of the gap he stands on.

"I work at Oakland Tech Collective, so I'm on the side that feels the block moving," Obi said. "The building's got new people coming in all the time, and there's a hum to it. But when I walk past Art Walk Cafe or Night Walk Records, I see the same folks, the same pace. They're not standing still, but it's a different rhythm. I think the tech places bring a certain energy, but the small spots — they're the ones that make the block feel like a neighborhood. You watch them, and you wonder if they feel the same push we do, or if they're just holding on."

That is the question a business desk should be asking out loud. The offices bring payroll and people. The cafe, the lounge and the record shop give those people somewhere to be once they leave their desks. If the small places are the reason Uptown feels like a neighborhood, it is fair to ask who benefits when the neighborhood gets more valuable. The answer is not settled by asking.

My mother ran a laundromat on 7th Street, and her customers kept coming right up until the building sold. Regulars are loyal. Loyalty does not pay a landlord who has watched the office tenants next door.

Nakamura's regulars are still coming in. Lopez's machine is still running. The block is moving. Does a rising Uptown lift Night Walk Records and Art Walk Cafe with it, or does it just make the rent go up?
