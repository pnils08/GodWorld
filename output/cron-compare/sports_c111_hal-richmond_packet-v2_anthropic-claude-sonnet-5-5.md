# The Ledger Closes Before the Ink Is Dry

Mark Aitken finished the two thousand forty-one regular season with thirty-three home runs, one hundred fifty-three hits and eighty-seven runs batted in, across one hundred thirty-eight games and five hundred seventy-two plate appearances. He hit two eighty-three and was worth three point two wins above replacement. His career line also holds a Rookie of the Year award from two thousand thirty-two, and Gold Gloves from two thousand thirty-two, two thousand thirty-eight and two thousand thirty-nine.

That is a long river for one name. The first bank is a rookie in the spring of thirty-two, and the water has run nine seasons to the line above. This is the cycle that belongs to the off-season. The city's sports ledger carries the Athletics under a plain editorial note for cycle one hundred eleven, and nothing else is stirring. No game is being played and no score is waiting. In the Baylight District, as in every district, the season has become a thing that happened.

I have learned to distrust that stillness. When you have watched one franchise for most of your working life, you know the off-season is not rest. It is the part of the year when the roster becomes a question.

## The other lines

Aitken is not the only name on the page. Desmond Morton played eighty games in two thousand forty-one and came to the plate three hundred thirteen times. He hit two ninety-one with twenty-two doubles, seven home runs and forty-four runs batted in, for two point five wins. Frank Reyna played forty-six games, a fraction of a season, and hit three seventeen with fourteen home runs and thirty-four runs batted in. His two point four wins were gathered in a space where others gather one.

Carmen Mesa made ten starts, won five and lost three, and threw fifty-six innings, allowing forty-four hits. The record credits her with one point three wins above replacement. It is a small sample, and I will not dress it as more. But a season is built of small samples that have been stacked and survived.

I give you these figures as evidence of time passing, not as grades. No scout's card is being filled out here. Four people played baseball for this city in the year two thousand forty-one, and the record kept what they did. The record is why I am sitting here, and it is all I am doing.

## What the old ledgers taught me

When I reach for a measure of what a championship era feels like, I reach backward, past anything this city's own books hold. In the early nineteen seventies, the Athletics won three straight world championships, in nineteen seventy-two, nineteen seventy-three and nineteen seventy-four. Reggie Jackson was named the American League's most valuable player in the middle year, after hitting thirty-two home runs. Catfish Hunter and Rollie Fingers stood on the same grass. For three autumns it looked as though nothing could pull that club apart.

Then it came apart. I do not mean it collapsed on the field. It dispersed. Hunter was declared a free agent in the winter after the third title and signed elsewhere. In the spring of nineteen seventy-six, Jackson was traded away. A team that had seemed permanent turned out to be a lineup of men with separate futures, and the clock ran on each of them.

The writers who covered those teams did not know, during the parade, that the parade was the last full photograph. We rarely do. The photograph looks like the first of a series.

## The question the winter asks

The desk's own framing speaks of a seventh championship and of a roster facing volatility, with Mike Paulson's name attached to the winter's reckoning. I cannot tell you what that volatility is, because the record I hold does not say. I will not invent a trade, an injury or a contract to make this column feel more urgent than the facts allow. I know that a thirty-three-home-run season, a Gold Glove shelf and a few small, fine samples are what the record gives me. They are also what a front office has to work with in a cold month.

So I will ask the question the way an old man asks it, without claiming to know the answer. Is the seventh championship the crest of this cycle, the last full photograph? Or is it the beginning of the dispersal, the quiet winter in which a club that looked permanent finds out it was only a lineup of separate futures? Who answers that, and when, is a matter the line card leaves open. I would sooner leave it open than close it falsely.

## What I do know

I know that a dynasty is not a single year. It is a run of years that each felt ordinary while they were happening. Aitken's rookie award is nine seasons behind him now, and his two thousand forty-one numbers sit in the same ledger as that first season. The ledger does not tell you which line is the last good one. It only records them and goes on to the next page.

In nineteen seventy-four the Athletics won their third in a row, and I suspect everyone who loved them assumed there would be a fourth, or at least a long afterglow. There was a long afterglow in memory. On the field there was a different club within two summers. That is the lesson I carry into any off-season, this one included. The men and women on the page are real, and their figures are real. The thing we call an era is a story we tell about them afterward, once we know where it ended.

Mesa's fifty-six innings, Reyna's fourteen home runs in forty-six games, Morton's twenty-two doubles and Aitken's long river are not a verdict. They are what remains of a season that has now been placed on the shelf. The next page is blank, and I do not yet know whose names will be on it.

If the winter turns out to be the break, it will be a break of continuity. The same grass will be there in the spring, but not the same hands, and the old ledgers will be all we have of the ones who left.
