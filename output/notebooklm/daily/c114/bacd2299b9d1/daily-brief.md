### **The Board in the Trailer Says 9.5 Percent. Enzo Walker Is Counting Something Else.**
*By Jordan Velez, Business Desk*

On West Oakland job sites, construction and tech firms report steady commercial gains on paper, but the reality on the ground looks different to the crews doing the physical work. At **Coastline Construction**, plumber **Enzo Walker** walks past the trailer board where the company posts a 9.5 percent growth rate, 221 employees, and \$187.1 million in revenue—up \$341,000 from the prior cycle. 

Walker described the disconnect between corporate ledgers and neighborhood reality:
> *"Look, I see the numbers on the board in the trailer — 9.5% growth, revenue up, all that. And sure, we're busy. I'm not complaining about work. But the numbers don't tell you what it's like on the ground. The buildings going up, they're not for us. They're for the tech folks coming in, the ones from Oakmesh and Civis. I fix pipes in places that didn't exist last year, and the people who used to live on those blocks? They're gone. The work's changing, alright — but it's changing in a way where I'm not sure the folks signing the checks even notice who's not at the table anymore. The ledger says growth. The street says something else."*

Nearby, managing partner **Helena Voss-Adeyemi** leads **Northgate Construction** (95 employees, 8.8% growth, \$78.8M revenue), while **Mariner Construction** carries 165 workers. Alongside plumbers **Hazel Reed**, **Kenton Pearl**, **Maeve Pillai**, and **Yadira Rao**, electricians **Elijah Brown** and **Jude Hess**, and carpenter apprentice **Minh Raimon**, trade crews balance rising living costs against expanding corporate tech firms like **Oakmesh Systems** (founded by Marcus Tan, up 34.1% to \$9.5M revenue) and **Civis Systems** (led by Elias Varek, \$60.2M revenue). 

Coastline’s managing partner, **Desmond Achebe**, openly acknowledged the retention strain:
> *"What keeps me up at night isn't the growth on the ledger — I can read a spreadsheet. It's the quiet stuff the numbers don't show. I walk a different street home sometimes, just to see what's actually happening on the block, and I see the new folks settling in from Chinatown... But I also see the workers — my plumbers, the electricians, the carpenters at the other shops — and I know a 9% growth number doesn't tell you whether a guy can afford to stay in the neighborhood he's helping rebuild. The contraction isn't loud. It's the guy who's been on the crew for twelve years quietly looking at rents across the bridge."*

For his part, **Marcus Tan** of Oakmesh stated:
> *"Oakmesh is growing, and that growth means we hire more people, we pay more taxes, we take up more space. That's real. But the neighborhood isn't a ledger. It's the people who've been here through the ups and downs. What do we owe? We owe it to not forget that. We owe it to hire locally when we can, to be a good neighbor, to not let our success push anyone out."*

Across town, contractions remain concentrated in **Ivy Hill**, where **Ivy Hill Market** shed three employees (falling to -4.5% growth) alongside job losses at Ivy Hill Cafe, Ivy Cleaners, and Ivy Hill Family Dental.

### **At Rockridge Pickup, the Strongest School Record Slips by Five Hundredths**
*By Angela Reyes, Education Desk*

At the after-school pickup door in Rockridge, program aide **Mei Wong** watches the hallways keep their steady hours while managing the afternoon snack count:
> *"I can't speak to the enrollment numbers or the per-student funding figure — that's not something I see in my day-to-day. What I can tell you is what I see at pickup: parents asking if their kid ate a real snack, teachers who remember every child's name, and a community that shows up. The after-school space here has always felt tight, but it's the people who make it work. If the index slipped a little, I wouldn't be surprised if it's because we're all stretched thinner, not because the kids or the staff care any less."*

Rockridge’s school quality index slipped by five hundredths this cycle—moving from 8.58 down to 8.53—though it remains the highest in Oakland (ahead of Baylight District at 8.24 and Piedmont Avenue at 8.22). Rockridge holds 248 students (up one), a 94.2% graduation rate, 75.03% college readiness, 9.1 teacher quality, and \$15,000 in per-neighborhood funding. 

Principal **Gloria Liu** declined to treat the decimal movement as a crisis:
> *"A one-point drop on a scale like that — I'll be honest, it doesn't change what happens in the building on Monday morning. The kids still come in, the teachers still teach, and I still walk the halls... If anything, a dip like that makes me look closer at where we might be slipping — maybe it's a cohort thing, maybe it's a resource thing — but it doesn't mean I'm suddenly going to pull books off shelves or cut a program. We're still the strongest school in the city, and that's not because of a number. It's because of the people here."*

High school science teacher **Maya Torres-Dillon** emphasized continuous effort over static benchmarks:
> *"That 8.53 slipping, even a little, that's the kind of thing that gets my attention. It's not about panicking over a decimal point. It's about asking what that one student who just enrolled needs, and whether we're actually serving every kid in that building, not just the ones already on track."*

Grade schooler **Rick Dillon** gave the student perspective while waiting with his backpack:
> *"I don't really get the number stuff, but my dad says our school's still the best around. We got one new kid this year, and we're all still learning the same stuff. I think it just means we gotta keep trying hard, like in baseball when your batting average dips a little — you don't panic, you just take more swings."*

By comparison, **Temescal** sits at the bottom of the district table at 6.36 (down from 6.43), carrying 313 students, an 84.37% graduation rate, 54.5% college readiness, 6.86 teacher quality, and \$11,500 in per-student funding.

### **Chinatown's 137 Sick: Six Beds Hold Part of the Count, Kitchens Hold the Rest**
*By Dr. Lila Mezran, Health & Civic Desk*

Chinatown carries the largest count of sick residents in Oakland at 137 (down three from 140, representing 6.2% of the neighborhood and remaining above the 6.0% watch bar), ahead of East Oakland (119) and Ivy Hill (118). Yet the hospital census recorded only three admissions and six residents in hospital care filling six beds:

*   **Akua Adeyemi**, a plumber working across Chinatown homes, sees the unrecorded accommodations families make behind closed doors:
    > *"I see it in the little things, honestly. A bottle of cough syrup moved from the bathroom to the kitchen counter, so it's right there where someone'll remember it. Or a chair pulled up by the window, where someone's been sitting a long time, just watching the street. Sometimes the whole house gets rearranged — the bed moved downstairs, or the living room turned into a bedroom, because stairs got too hard. You don't have to ask. You just see it."*
*   **Jax Caldera**, an accountability writer living in Chinatown, observed that institutional healthcare remains out of reach for workers who cannot afford to miss shifts:
    > *"Honestly, I see people push through more than they go in. It's not stubbornness — it's that the hospital's not close, and a lot of folks here work jobs where taking a day off means losing pay or losing a shift. I've watched neighbors ride out fevers with herbal tea and a heating pad because the alternative was a bill they couldn't explain. The ones who do go in are usually past the point of choice."*
*   **Yu Zen**, a commercial insurance broker in Rockridge recovering from a workplace injury, reflected on the hospital gap:
    > *"Chinatown's got the most sick right now, and I know that neighborhood doesn't have the hospital footprint the rest of us take for granted. But I've seen how those communities work — the families, the shopkeepers, the elders checking on each other. That's where the caring happens when the beds are full."*

### **Coliseum Lost 799 Riders on a Weekend With No Event on Its Row**
*By Trevor Shimizu, Transit Desk*

Coliseum Station logged 4,796 passengers this cycle—dropping 799 riders from 5,595 in Cycle 113, the sharpest decline on the transit network—even as systemwide ridership rose slightly to 46,839 across eight stations:

*   **Jerald Jensen**, an AC Transit janitor, reads platform activity through what passengers leave behind:
    > *"When the Coliseum empties out like that on a weekend, I see it in the trash cans — fewer cups, fewer wrappers, fewer folks lingering after the last train. The platform records don't catch the quiet. They don't catch the feeling that people aren't just missing a train, they're missing a reason to be there."*
*   **Evan Lewis**, driving his AC Transit bus past the station, noted:
    > *"When you pull up to that station and it's quieter than it ought to be, even for a Saturday, you start wondering... It's about the folks who used to be standing there who aren't anymore — the ones who found another way, or who just stopped going where they used to go. The platform records don't tell you about them."*
*   **Eleanor Rivera**, BART station manager at 12th Street (where boardings rose by 670), explained that ridership shifts rarely have a single cause:
    > *"When a station drops more than the usual weekend pattern, it's rarely one thing. It's usually a rhythm stacking up — something at the station, something on the line, something in that neighborhood."*
*   Meanwhile, **Elena Soria Dominguez** (Fruitvale Transit Hub Authority) noted that Fruitvale gained 510 riders as station life thrived alongside local street vendors and community gatherings.
