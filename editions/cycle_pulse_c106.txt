THE CYCLE PULSE — Y2C106
The Bay Tribune — Oakland
Narrated by Mags Corliss, Editor-in-Chief

============================================================

There's a particular kind of week in this city where nothing breaks open and everything still shifts. Y2C106 was that kind of week. The gray hasn't lifted — Noah Tan reported from Lake Merritt on a third consecutive week of overcast skies that have done something to the social fabric of that neighborhood, and it's not a small thing. Toby Lamont told Noah he felt the whole rhythm change. Mina Linas, who monitors the lake and sees who stops and who keeps walking, said even the regulars just nod now and move on. A city under low clouds is still a city, but it operates differently — more interior, more private, more provisional. The benches stay empty. People are waiting for something to break, as Tobi Sobi put it from her kitchen table, watching her kids lose enthusiasm for the outside world.

What I keep returning to is Mina Linas showing up twice in this week's reporting. Noah finds her at the monitoring station, watching people withdraw. Kai Marston finds her later at Lakeside Whisper Bar, where she says the place lets you actually hear yourself think. Same person, same week, two environments. The quiet around the lake that reads as social retreat is also, at the right bar on the right night, something closer to intimacy. That's not a contradiction I want to smooth over. It's the same gray weather producing two different human responses depending on whether you have somewhere warm to go.

The Oaks came up twice too, and in ways that don't quite match. Talia Finch went to West Oakland and found Elio Perez and Daniel Cloak and Darius Clark all saying, with genuine warmth, that the team is reaching toward the community and that it matters. Then Simon Leary spent his week with the A's record — 127-35, a machine more than a team by now — and asked the harder question: at what point does the architecture become the story, rather than the people passing through? Martin Richards, a veteran who knows something about being moved across positions and cities, told Simon that value shifts sometimes overnight. Ollie Campbell, who doesn't even follow sports closely, noticed people talking about how the A's keep winning no matter who's holding the beams. The Oaks engagement Talia documented and the A's apparatus Simon examined are two different models of what a sports franchise can be in this city. One is still trying to be recognized by its neighborhood. The other has achieved something so structural it barely needs anyone's recognition.

Rockridge had a good week by the numbers Angela Reyes reported — 95% graduation, 78% college-ready, a school quality index that sits near the top of the city. But Angela found the real story in Simone Adesanya's doorway and Mei Wong's after-school hours. Rick Dillon, eighth grade, put it most cleanly: we do our homework and play baseball after. The question Angela left open is whether any of this travels — whether Rockridge is a replicable model or an accumulation of specific advantages and decades of collective expectation that can't simply be cited in a proposal and moved across district lines.

That question brushes directly against what Jax Caldera found Downtown, and what Luis Navarro found when he talked to servers in two different neighborhoods about the same civic feeling. Jax stood on those eight or nine downtown blocks where retail vitality fell nearly three points, housing pressure is now worse than eighteen of the city's twenty-two neighborhoods, and the sentiment reading has dropped to 0.41 — above the city median, so nobody gets to call it the worst, but moving in the wrong direction with nothing waiting on the other side to catch it. Eunice Marston, who watches her congregation move past SpeedyBurger every week, said she hasn't seen any significant initiatives to address the concerns. Merkin Jumper, a caseworker who notices things professionally, used the word wish. Not expect. Not demand. Wish.

Luis heard something similar from Elio Perez — the same Elio who was enthusiastic about the Oaks in Talia's piece — who told him the council talks about accountability but he doesn't see it closing the gap. Depak Ying in Rockridge had heard the same thing secondhand from people he doesn't know, the way these feelings move between people doing the same shift in different zip codes. Luis was careful, and I respect the carefulness: he named the gap but declined to invent a name to put pressure on, because he doesn't have one yet. That's honest reporting. It also means we're sitting with a civic gap that the city's own instruments have flagged, no initiative on the books to answer it, and no office we can point to and ask why.

Mason Ortega's piece from Harborline Grill in Jack London Square sat a little outside the week's dominant key, and I mean that as a compliment. Tomas Renteria runs the line and runs a podcast and asks, in both endeavors, the same question: did you leave them satisfied? His colleagues Quynh Le and Jalen Hill read him differently — Le sees someone always working on something bigger, Hill sees a guy who keeps his head down and gets through the rush. Both are probably right. Jordan Velez's Toby Lamont, who was also Noah's Toby Lamont standing at the lake's edge, turns out to be anchoring the West Oakland Community Center's mutual aid operation with what the center's colleagues called quiet fire. He's the same man in both stories too. The city this week kept folding back on itself that way — the same names, the same corners, showing different faces.

What I'm watching next week: whether Jax can get a name attached to the Downtown gap, because Eunice Marston and Merkin Jumper deserve something more answerable than a wish. And whether the civic disconnect Luis documented — two servers, two neighborhoods, one word — starts producing any movement at the dais, or whether it just keeps traveling between people who don't know each other but recognize the same feeling the moment someone says it out loud.

THE WEEK'S REPORTING

By Noah Tan | Bay Tribune Civic

Toby Lamont paused at the edge of Lake Merritt, hands deep in his coat pockets, watching the water ripple under the low gray sky. He’d been fighting off a head cold all week, and the damp chill in the air wasn’t helping. “You know, when that gray just sits over Lake Merritt for days,” he said, “it's like the whole rhythm changes.”  

The usual joggers and walkers were still out, but they moved with purpose—heads down, bundled against the cold, fewer stops to chat. The benches near the water, usually dotted with folks lingering over coffee or conversation, were mostly empty. Mina Linas, who works at the lake’s monitoring station, echoed the sentiment: “I see it at the monitoring station—fewer people stopping to chat, more heads down, quick steps. Even the regulars who usually linger by the water just give me a nod and keep moving.”  

The overcast stretch—now in its third week—has settled into the neighborhood’s bones. Enzo Walker, a plumber who lives nearby, noted the shift: “When it’s overcast like this, folks around here tend to keep to themselves more. You see fewer people out by the lake, fewer conversations on the sidewalks.”  

At home, Tobi Sobi helped her kids with homework at the kitchen table, the gray weather pressing in through the windows. “Even the kids seem less eager to play outside,” she said. “It’s like we’re all waiting for something to break, for the sun to come back and lift the mood.”  

Does this weather make Oakland feel more like a series of interiors than a connected city?

By Angela Reyes | Bay Tribune Civic

# The Rockridge Equation  

Simone Adesanya, principal of Rockridge’s top-ranked school, leans against the doorway of her office, watching the hallway fill with chatter as the final bell rings. “You know, what makes Rockridge schools special isn’t any one thing you can point to,” she says, her voice steady. “It’s how this community wraps itself around these kids.”  

The numbers tell part of the story: a school quality index of 9, graduation rates at 95%, and 78% of students college-ready. But the real texture lies in the hallways—teachers who’ve been here for decades, parents who show up not just for their own child but for the whole class, and an unspoken expectation that everyone is in this together. One of those parents, Mei Wong, works with kids in the after-school program. “It’s not just about funding—though that’s part of it—it’s the collective effort,” she says.  

Rick Dillon, an eighth-grader, puts it more simply: “We just do our homework and play baseball after.”  

What holds Rockridge together isn’t a mystery, but neither is it easily replicated. The Bay Conservation Alliance pitches in, businesses contribute, and the community rallies around its 223 students. The question now is whether this model can inspire other neighborhoods striving to close the gap.

By Jax Caldera | Bay Tribune Civic

# Downtown, Overcast, Nobody's Name on It

Merkin Jumper's been standing outside SpeedyBurger on his lunch break for eleven minutes now, folder under his arm, watching the line move fast like it always does. He's a caseworker. He notices things for a living. "I've seen the way the neighborhood is, you know," he tells me, still watching, "the towers staying lit late and people always rushing to and from places like Telegraph Presbyterian and SpeedyBurger. It feels like there's a lot of activity, but at the same time, I've heard people talking about how Downtown is decaying." He shrugs, folder still under his arm. "As someone who works with the community, I feel like I'm making a difference, but I also wish there were more initiatives to address the issues we're facing."

Wish. That's the word doing the work. Not waiting on. Not was told. Wish — like a birthday candle, not a budget line.

Here's what the record actually says, because Jumper's gut and the numbers agree for once: Downtown's sentiment reading dropped four-tenths of a point and now sits at 0.41 — eighth out of twenty-two neighborhoods, technically above the city's own median of 0.36, so don't let anybody tell you this is the worst block in town. It isn't. But retail vitality fell nearly three full points to 9.55, and housing pressure jumped a full point to 3, worse than eighteen of twenty-two neighborhoods in this city. Three metrics, three directions, all bad, all landing on the same eight or nine downtown blocks in the same stretch. And when I went looking for the initiative, the task force, the office, the anything with a name on it that's supposed to be sitting on this fire — there's nothing. No matching program. No mitigator. Nobody's desk.

I found Celeste Moon near the towers that stay lit past when anybody's working in them, and she hadn't heard about any of it. "I haven't really thought about it," she said, "but I did hear Downtown was packed last night. That's all I know." Packed. Lit up. And decaying underneath it — those aren't contradictions, they're the same neighborhood wearing two faces depending which number you trust.

Then there's Eunice Marston, who runs faith work out of her building and watches her congregation stream past SpeedyBurger every week like clockwork. "I haven't really seen any significant changes or initiatives that address the concerns about Downtown decay," she told me, "but I do see people from my community, like those coming out of Telegraph Presbyterian, going about their daily lives. As a faith leader, I try to stay focused on the people and their needs, rather than getting caught up in the broader issues." That's a pastor telling me, politely, that the city hasn't given her congregation anything to point at either. She's not chasing the broader issues because nobody handed her a broader issue to chase — just a block getting quietly worse under lit windows.

Three people, three vantage points, same blank space where an answer should be. A caseworker who wishes there were more programs. A singer who heard it was packed and left it there. A pastor keeping her eyes on people because the paperwork isn't giving her anything else to look at.

Somebody at City Hall owns a map with Downtown's numbers moving the wrong direction on three separate lines at once. Nobody's initiative owns the fix. So who's supposed to be reading this map, and why hasn't anyone told Merkin Jumper, Eunice Marston, or the rest of us what they're seeing?

By Mason Ortega | Bay Tribune Culture

# The Rhythm Between the Grill and the Mic

In the back kitchen of Harborline Grill, Tomas Renteria flips a salmon fillet with the same practiced motion he might use to adjust a microphone level. The podcast host and line cook moves through his Wednesday dinner rush in a rhythm that seems to split his attention perfectly between sizzling protein plates and the stories he collects about this Jack London square neighborhood.

"Man, juggling the podcast and the line cook gig at Harborline? It's all about rhythm, you know?" Renteria says, checking the doneness of three medium-rare orders with a press of his tongs. "At the grill, it's fire and timing, making sure every plate's right. Then with the mic, it's about catching that same energy but with voices and stories instead of food."

Around him, the Harborline line cooks navigate the tight kitchen space—Quynh Le layering roasted vegetables with precision, Jalen Hill silently refilling the fry station as the ticket printer spits another wave of orders.

"Tomas? Yeah, he's got this energy about him—like he's always working on something bigger, even when he's just plating up orders," says Le during a rare lull, wiping her knife down. "You can tell he's listening, not just waiting for his turn to speak."

Hill offers a different take between loading sheet trays into the washer: "Man keeps his head down on the line, does his job. I hear the podcast chatter sometimes when we're breaking down stations, but Harborline's not the kind of place where you bring that energy to work. We're all just trying to get through the rush."

The duality shows in the details—how Renteria's podcast notes sometimes appear between prep lists on the stainless steel counter, how the regulars at the bar seem to lean in when he emerges from the kitchen, still in his apron, to record a quick vox pop during his smoke break.

But the real crossover happens after close, when Renteria’s colleagues become his first audience, their off-shift beers turning into impromptu focus groups. They're the ones who remind him that both jobs—feeding bodies and feeding minds—come down to the same question: Did you leave them satisfied?

By Luis Navarro | Bay Tribune Civic

## Council Talk, Neighborhood Math

Elio Perez has the rag out before the lunch rush even starts. West Oakland comes in loud through the kitchen door — dish pit running, someone yelling for extra lemons — and he's wiping the same four-top for the third time because his hands need something to do while he talks.

"I feel like the council talks a lot about accountability," he says, setting down a stack of menus, "but I don't see much action that actually closes the gap for people like me and my neighbors."

He doesn't name a meeting. He doesn't name a vote. He names a feeling that's been sitting on him long enough to become a sentence he's ready to say to a reporter between tables.

Across town, in Rockridge, Depak Ying is doing the same job at a different address — folding napkins into the kind of triangles that look sharp on a table setting, waiting for the door to open. He's heard the same thing Elio has, secondhand, the way these things travel between servers who don't know each other but know the same shift.

"I've heard people talking about the council not stepping up," he says, "and it feels like there's a disconnect — like they're not hearing what's happening in the neighborhoods. It's frustrating because we're all just trying to make things work here."

Two servers, two neighborhoods, one word showing up in both mouths without either of them coordinating it: disconnect.

Here's what I can put on the page and what I can't.

What's confirmed: the city's own accounting flagged a civic gap this cycle — a distance between what accountability sounds like from the dais and what it does on the ground. That's not my read of the room. That's the finding itself. And paired with it: no civic initiative currently on the books answers that gap. Not a stalled one. Not a delayed one. None.

What isn't confirmed, and what I'm not going to pretend I know: which office owns that gap. Which councilmember, if any, has been asked and gone quiet. Whether a request went out and got nothing back, or whether nobody's filed the request yet. I don't have a name to put pressure on, and I'm not inventing one to make this piece feel more finished than it is.

That's the uncomfortable part of this kind of story. Elio and Depak aren't wrong about what they feel. The gap they're describing is real enough that it shows up in the city's own instruments, not just in their frustration. But feeling a disconnect and being able to point to who's responsible for closing it are two different things, and right now only one of those exists on the record.

So the question isn't rhetorical, and it isn't mine to answer for them: if the city can see the gap clearly enough to log it, who exactly is supposed to be the one closing it — and how long does a table full of servers in two different neighborhoods have to keep saying the same sentence before somebody with a title says it back?

By Simon Leary | Bay Tribune Sports

# The Structure That Holds

Martin Richards, who knows something about shifting roles—from third base to the Mets, from New York to Fruitvale, from prospect to veteran—had finished his morning coffee at the counter of some diner that wasn’t in the real Oakland when the question found him. “Role changes?” He spun the mug once. “They’re inevitable when you’re chasing success.” The ceramic scraped against laminate. “You think you’re settled, that you’ve found your place, but the game doesn’t stop for anyone.” 

Outside, the A’s were winning again. They always were. 127-35, another number in a season of numbers. What does it mean when a machine—and make no mistake, that’s what this is now, not a team but an apparatus—keeps running no matter which gears are swapped out? How does a structure like that bear its own weight? Richards, who has no answers because no one does, only the numbers, squinted at the light through the blinds. “It’s not just about adapting—it’s about understanding that your value shifts, sometimes overnight.”

Down the road, somewhere an engine wasn’t running, Ollie Campbell considered it all from a distance. “I don’t follow sports much,” they admitted, “but I’ve heard folks around here talking about how the A’s keep winning even as players come and go.” They paused, weighing something in their palm. “It’s interesting how they manage to stay strong like that.”

Interesting, yes. But what is strength when there’s no alternative? When the structure is designed to stand regardless of who’s holding up the beams? The record doesn’t remember the moments of doubt, the fractures. It only knows the final weight, distributed across whatever—whoever—was available to bear it at the time.

At what point does the architecture become the story, rather than the people passing through?
