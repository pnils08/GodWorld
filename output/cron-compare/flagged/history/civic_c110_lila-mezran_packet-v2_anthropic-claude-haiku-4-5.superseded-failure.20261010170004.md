# Chinatown's Care Arithmetic: How One Neighborhood Carries the City's Illness Weight

Daphne Stafford moves between apartments in Chinatown on foot, a home health aide carrying a small bag of supplies. This cycle, she is moving faster than usual, and the apartments she visits have changed their rhythms. Older residents who used to sit outside are staying in. Families are texting instead of gathering. The neighborhood has 145 sick residents—more than any other hood in the city—and the pressure shows not in headlines but in the texture of daily life: who stays home, who works through it, who can afford to rest.

"It's been tough for families here in Chinatown," Stafford said. "With more people getting sick, there's a lot of worry about how to care for loved ones while keeping up with work and daily life. Many are juggling doctor visits, medications, and trying to keep everyone healthy at home. It's a lot to handle, especially when resources feel stretched."

The numbers behind that stretch are precise. Chinatown carries 145 of the city's 1,947 sick residents—a climb of 3 from the prior cycle. In the hospital system, the neighborhood has moved 3 residents into care this cycle; 6 are now in beds, occupying 6 of the city's 76 occupied hospital beds. Seventy-seven residents are in care citywide. One percent of the hospital system is full.

Millicent Kumar, a plumber who works in the neighborhood, has felt the shift without seeing it directly. "Honestly, I haven't noticed much change in my daily routine," Kumar said. "I keep my head down, do my work. But I did hear from Selena that some of the older folks on her block have been staying in more, which isn't like them. That's got me a little worried."

The rise in illness is not unique to Chinatown. East Oakland and Ivy Hill are each carrying 125 and 122 sick residents respectively, both climbing by 3 this cycle. Across the city's 22 neighborhoods, the illness rate holds at 4.5 percent. But Chinatown's proportion—145 sick residents out of 2,185 total—sits at 6.6 percent, crossing the watch threshold where care systems begin to feel the strain.

Hospital admissions this cycle totaled 38 citywide. Chinatown accounted for 3. The city has capacity: 76 beds occupied of available stock, 1 percent utilization. But utilization is not the measure that matters to a family managing illness at home while working, or a clinic coordinator allocating staff to neighborhoods where more residents need attention at once.

Yu Zen lies in a hospital bed in Rockridge—Yu was admitted after a workplace accident in cycle 105 and remains in active care. From that bed, Yu speaks with clarity about what the numbers mean to the neighborhood where they land. "From this hospital bed, all I hear are the nurses talking about how Chinatown's been hit hard. It's not just numbers—it's families, neighbors. When you're lying here, you feel the weight of it. Chinatown's tight-knit; when one suffers, it ripples through everyone."

The care flow from Chinatown shows concentration, not crisis. Three admissions this cycle pushed the neighborhood's hospital census to 6, with no tracked residents—meaning all 6 are other residents of the city, not people from outside being transported in. The beds are full at the system's current scale. What remains uncharted is how families keep working, keep children in school, keep meals moving through households where one or more members are sick and home care is the only available option. That is where Stafford moves, between apartments, between medications and worry, carrying what resources stretch to reach.
