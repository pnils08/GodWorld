The A's parade rolled through Downtown, the double-decker bus carrying Mike Paulson, Vinnie Keane, and Benji Dillon at the front, waving while the crowds pressed close. 

Back inside the clubhouse the bus engine had long gone quiet, yet the three of them still stood near the lockers with confetti caught in their sleeves. Mark Aitken spoke first, voice low. 

"You know, the bus was loud — the music, the chanting, the guys spraying champagne everywhere. But when it quiets down, when we're just rolling through Downtown and the fans are still out there waving, that's when it hits you. I look over at Vinnie and Benji and I know they're taking it all in, every last wave, because this is it for them. And me? I'm taking it in too, but for a different reason. I don't know what's next. None of us do. So yeah, the laugh is easy because we just won a championship — that's real. But the quiet moments on that bus, when everyone's just staring out the window at the crowds? That's where the weight sits. We're not just celebrating a win. We're saying goodbye to something, whether we're ready to or not."

Benji Dillon nodded, staring at the floor. 

"Yeah, there's a moment. When the bus finally stops and the engine cuts, and you hear the crowd still going — that's when it lands. Not the confetti, not the waving, not the noise. It's the quiet after the noise, when you're standing there with your teammates and you realize you don't have to do it again. That's when it hits. That's when you feel the weight of everything you've carried, and it's finally okay to put it down."

Mike Paulson leaned against the doorframe, arms folded. 

"You know, the parade was something else — seeing the whole city out there, kids on shoulders, old-timers crying. But I'll tell you, there's a moment on that bus where you look down the line and you see Vinnie and Benji laughing, and you know it's the last time they're doing that as players. And Mark's right there with them, and you don't know if he's coming back. That's the part nobody sees. The confetti's still in your hair, and you're already doing the math in your head — who stays, who goes, who's got one more year in them. It's heavy. You want to just sit in the moment, but the moment's already slipping, and you're the one who's got to figure out what comes next. That's the undertow.
