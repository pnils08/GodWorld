# The Weight We Hung on John Ellis

I stood on the BART platform at 12th Street after Game 4, and nobody wanted to say his name. The train rumbled in, doors opened like a sigh, and the dozen or so green and gold faithful stumbled aboard silent. Nobody replayed the 9-4 mess. Nobody compared notes on the bullpen. Everyone was carrying the same thing: the face of John Ellis, head down, walking off the mound in the fourth, and the sound of Twins bats that felt personal.

I walked up Broadway. The Short Porch was half-empty, a few stools occupied by people staring at their phones as if the box score might change. A man in a faded Ellis jersey sat at the end of the bar, nursing a beer like it owed him money. I didn't ask his name. I didn't need to.

John Ellis spoke to us this week, and his words haven't left my head since. "Sometimes it feels like we're carrying the whole city on our shoulders."

You read that and you want to tell him to shake it off. Some fans will say exactly that: it's one bad start, the playoffs are long, the team won the next night. They'll point to Pablo Almanzar's one-hit shutout in Game 5 — nine innings of white-knuckle silence, a debut that felt like the second coming — and say, *See? The A's are fine. October's still alive. Stop catastrophizing.*

I'm not catastrophizing. I'm asking what it costs to be the name this city pins everything on, and whether all of us should have known better.

Because we did this. We spent two seasons turning John Ellis into the arm that would carry Oakland back to glory. Every big start felt like audition and verdict rolled into one. He wasn't allowed to be a young pitcher figuring it out; he was the answer to a generation of almosts. And when he left Game 4 with the stadium groaning, I don't think it was the Twins' bats that broke him. It was the weight.

The team bounced back. Almanzar was everything we dreamed and more — 9 innings, 1 hit, 3 strikeouts, the kind of night where the neighborhood chatter Pablo Almanzar described becomes a citywide roar. "It's like the whole city is invested in it," he said. And he's right. But that investment cuts both ways. We pour it into the players, and when one of them buckles, we act surprised. We shouldn't be.

Eric Taveras, who went 3-for-7 with two homers and three RBIs across the two games, put it plainly: "The pressure to perform is always there, and when we don't do well, it can be hard to shake off the feeling of disappointment." Jessie Hess, a fan who's been riding this roller coaster as long as I have, said, "When they lose, we feel it too."

We feel it. He feels it. Everyone involved feels the same freight-train of hope and dread. But Ellis is the one who had to stand on the mound while it roared past. And I keep thinking: maybe we shouldn't hand a young man the keys to the city and call it love.

I'm not giving up on John Ellis. The series isn't over, the season isn't over, and I'd still want the ball in his hand if there's a Game 7. But I'm done pretending the cost is invisible. The mark of a good season can't be just how far we go — it has to be whether we still recognize the people we asked to get us there.

Game 5 gave us a reason to believe. Almanzar gave us a gift. But Game 4 should haunt us, because what we asked John Ellis to carry belongs to all of us. Next time, Oakland, maybe we carry some of it ourselves.

— P. Slayer
