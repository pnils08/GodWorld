# Chinatown's 140 Sick, and the Six Who Are in a Bed

Marisio Curazo works the phone and the sidewalk. She is an elder care coordinator in Chinatown. When the neighborhood's number came up this cycle, 140 sick residents, she did not start with the number.

"When I see a number like 140 sick in Chinatown, I don't see a number. I see the families I work with every day," Curazo said. "Most of them aren't going to the hospital. They're staying home because that's what we do. The daughter takes the bus from Fruitvale to cook. The nephew calls in late to pick up prescriptions. I'm the one who helps figure out who needs a home health aide, who needs their meds delivered, who just needs someone to check in. The hospital has six of our folks right now, the ones who really couldn't stay home. The rest of us are doing what we've always done: taking care of each other, block by block, door by door."

The sheets give a sense of the size of that work. The Neighborhood_Demographics row for Chinatown at Cycle 113 shows 140 sick out of 2,201 residents, or 6.4%. That is at or over the 6% watch bar. The neighborhood's 395 seniors are in that population. The row does not say how many of the sick are seniors, so no one should read the 395 as a count of the ill.

The paper's own earlier figure puts the count in direction. The Packet records Chinatown at 140, down 2 from Cycle 112. It is still the highest of the city's 22 neighborhoods. East Oakland follows at 121, then Ivy Hill at 120. Citywide the sheet counts 1,845 sick residents. The world record for the cycle also carries a low-severity health cluster in Chinatown. It is the lightest category the record uses. It still sits on the neighborhood with the biggest count.

## The Trail From Sick to Bed

The Care_Justice_Census hospital rows show how few of the sick end up in a bed. In Cycle 113 Chinatown logged 3 hospital admissions, all coded as illness. There were 0 injury admissions, 0 heat, 0 mental-health-crisis and 0 substance-treatment. At the close, 6 residents were in care and 6 beds were occupied. Three people left during the cycle.

I read the Cycle 112 rows too. They show the same shape: 3 admissions, 6 in care at the close, 3 exits. The six did not grow or shrink between the two closes, so the six at the end of C113 are not a fresh wave. The ledger tracks none of them by name. All 6 are counted as other residents, and the census names no tracked Chinatown resident in a bed.

That leaves a gap of 134 between the sick and the beds. The number comes from subtraction, not from the census, and the census does not describe those 134 people. Omarion Farah, a Chinatown cab driver, hears about that gap from the back seat.

"People talk in the cab, but not about hospitals much," Farah said. "They're more worried about who's going to cover a shift or pick up the kids. I see folks staying home, taking care of each other. The ones I hear about going to the hospital are the ones who really can't avoid it. It's a lot of home care, a lot of family stepping in."

## What a Bed Teaches

Yu Zen is a commercial insurance broker who lives in Rockridge. The hospital ledger lists him as in care and active since Cycle 105, after a workplace accident. He is not one of Chinatown's six, and no row ties him to the neighborhood. He has still spent time in a hospital bed. He says he read the neighborhood's number against what he saw there.

"When I was in that hospital bed after my accident, I kept thinking about the numbers I deal with every day as an insurance broker — the claims, the paperwork, the names on the forms," Yu said. "But lying there, I realized the names on the forms aren't the same as the names on the beds. Chinatown's been hit so hard, and I know it's not because those folks are any less careful or any less strong. It's that when illness clusters, it lands on households where someone's already stretched thin — caring for a parent, watching the grandkids, working two jobs. The six of us in hospital care, we've got nurses and monitors and someone bringing us meals. The other 134 — they've got a cousin or an auntie or a neighbor who's doing all that and still trying to keep the shop open. That's who's caring for them. And that's the part that keeps me up at night, because I know that kind of care doesn't show up on any ledger."

Neither the census nor the hospital ledger says why illness gathers in one neighborhood. They give no cause for the Chinatown cluster. Yu's account of who it lands on is his reading, not a finding.

Other names sit on the same cycle's record. Deacon Seymour of Lake Merritt has been in hospital care since Cycle 105 after a workplace accident. Maurice Franklin of Rockridge has been in care since Cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu. Elsewhere on the lighter end, the record has Victor King of Rockridge nursing a sore back, Cee Lee of Lake Merritt dealing with a seasonal health concern, and Elle Lilo of Jack London finally shaking a cold. Citywide, the hospital rows show 35 admissions in Cycle 113, none of them tracked residents. At the close, 71 were in care in 71 occupied beds, and 37 left. Chinatown's 3 admissions are part of those 35.

## What the Record Still Owes

The census says 3 Chinatown residents left care this cycle. It does not say where they went. It does not say whether they went home to a household like the ones Curazo describes, or whether a home health aide, a delivery of medication or a check-in was waiting. It does not say whether the six in beds have a plan for leaving them. The care Yu describes in a cousin's kitchen and a neighbor's doorway has no row at all.

The next record needed is the discharge data for Chinatown's 3 admissions. After that comes whatever follow-up care the 6 in beds receive when they leave. Someone should also count the households taking in the other 134, so that Curazo's block-by-block work is seen in the numbers and not only on the sidewalk. Hospital and public health officials would be the ones to answer. The Packet names no office holding that count.
