- Deacon Seymour (POP-00528): "I'll tell you what my chain looked like, because it's the only one I can speak to from the inside. I got hurt at work, somebody called it in, the ambulance came, and I was admitted. Simple as that. I didn't have to think about whether the ambulance would come, or whether the hospital would take me, or whether I could afford the bed. I just had to lie there and let it happen. That's the part I can't stop thinking about when I look at the numbers out of Chinatown. My accident didn't make me tougher or smarter than anybody else. It made me lucky. And luck isn't a system. If I'd lived a few blocks over, if my job didn't carry the insurance it does, if I didn't have people who knew my name and could make a call — that chain looks different. It looks like waiting. It looks like hoping. It looks like a neighborhood carrying its sick the way Chinatown is carrying its sick, with no name on a ledger to show for it. I don't know who's in those six beds. But I know what it feels like to be in a bed and have people check on you. And I know what it would feel like to be in a bed and have nobody even know you're there."
- Maurice Franklin (POP-00801): "The days before I went in, I kept telling myself it was just a bad cold. I'd wake up, cough, check my temperature, and head to work anyway — you do that, you know? You've got a job, people depending on you, and the card statement's already sitting there. So you push through. By the time I couldn't catch my breath walking from the car to the front door, I knew I'd waited too long. But that's what it takes for a working person to get to a bed — you don't go until you can't stand, and then you hope there's a bed waiting. I was lucky. I got one. But I think about the folks in Chinatown, all those sick people, and how few of them show up on that hospital list. It's not that they're not sick. It's that they can't afford to stop. I know that feeling in my chest, and I know what it costs to ignore it."
