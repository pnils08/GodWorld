**A Weeknight Open Door**

By Elliot Graye

The heavy front door of Telegraph Presbyterian Fellowship stayed propped open past eight on a weeknight, a wedge of yellow light spilling onto the sidewalk. Inside, the sanctuary smelled of coffee and damp coats, folding chairs arranged in a loose semicircle near the pulpit. A stack of blankets sat on the front pew. Seventy-two people came through that evening — some alone, some with children — drawn by a congregation that had decided to open its doors for community support. Pastor Eunice Marston stood near the entrance, greeting each person with the unhurried calm of someone accustomed to watching ordinary evenings turn into places where a city leaves its weight for a few hours.

“I haven’t had any direct involvement with the Bharat Mandir of the East Bay,” she said, pausing as a young man adjusted a sleeping toddler on his shoulder. “But as a faith leader in Downtown, I appreciate the various initiatives and organizations that bring people together and foster a sense of community.”

That instinct to open doors echoed across the city. Temple Beth Or in Rockridge welcomed 104 people three cycles ago in a similar gesture. The Lakeshore Unitarian Universalist Fellowship activated its emergency assistance fund for 53. New Covenant Pentecostal Assembly in West Oakland served 157, and the Temescal Islamic Center expanded its pastoral counseling to 94. Meanwhile, the Epiphany observances drew vast numbers — 1,077 at the Cathedral of the Living Word and 374 at St. Esperanza Parish — while a quieter service the week before had pulled 1,185.

Amid the surge of crisis response, the Bharat Mandir of the East Bay in Montclair offered a different texture. Founded in 1990 and led by Pandit Anand Krishnamurthy, the Mandir has a congregation of 400 rooted in South Indian tradition, its life built around cultural programs rather than emergency events. On a weeknight, its courtyard is often still, its low roofline carrying the day’s last light. The Mandir has not been listed among this season’s crisis responders, but its steady, decades-long presence raises the question: What can a city learn from the quiet continuity that outlasts a crisis as surely as the door that stays open only until the immediate need passes?
