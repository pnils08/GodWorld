# 12th Street Is Running Better. The People Are Somewhere Else.

*By Trevor Shimizu*

At 7:40 at 12th St Oakland City Center, Joel Wren was looking at a quieter station and a smoother service. The station recorded 8,293 riders in Cycle 111, down 15,192 from the previous cycle. Its on-time performance improved to 86%, five points higher than before.

Those two measurements are supposed to describe the same transit system. Here they do not.

“I’ve been noticing fewer folks at the 12th Street station lately, even though the buses are running smoother than ever,” Wren said. “It’s strange—people used to pack in there, but now it feels quieter. Maybe it’s the Baylight construction approvals making folks rethink their routes. Hard to say, but something’s shifted.”

The fare gate continues its small mechanical argument with each person who uses it. The schedule can be better while the station becomes less useful. On-time performance measures whether service arrives when expected. Ridership measures whether people still choose to be there. A system can improve the first measure and lose the second.

Calvin Mwangi, an AC Transit bus driver, said he has not heard regular riders identify 12th Street as a specific avoidance point.

“Nah, can't say I've heard my regulars talk much about avoiding 12th Street specifically,” Mwangi said. “Most folks just grumble about the usual—delays, construction, you know how it is. But no, nothing about the council vote coming up in conversation.”

That distinction matters. The record does not establish that Baylight District construction caused the missing riders. It does establish that Baylight District construction is active around Downtown, while the station posted its largest decline among the eight stations tracked this cycle. Construction is a plausible explanation, not a completed diagnosis.

The same separation appears elsewhere. Nineteen St Oakland fell by 10,950 riders while its on-time result slipped to 77%. West Oakland fell by 5,974 while on-time performance rose nine points, to 84%. Fruitvale fell by 7,557 while on-time performance rose seven points, to 85%. The pattern is not limited to one station or one kind of schedule.

Ray Neal, an AC Transit Bus Operator, described the operational symptom without assigning it a cause.

“You know, it’s quieter now. Fewer folks waiting, fewer folks boarding. Feels like the energy’s shifted somewhere else, but I can’t quite put my finger on where.”

Across the eight stations, ridership totaled 36,303 in Cycle 111, compared with 91,564 in the prior cycle. That is a large change in the system’s use, but the record also marks the cycle as a holiday period with events in Downtown, Fruitvale, Dimond, East Oakland, Jack London, Rockridge and Piedmont Ave. Those conditions complicate any clean explanation. A quieter platform can be the result of construction, a holiday pattern, changed destinations, or several pressures operating together.

Evan Lewis, a bus driver, put the gap between the dashboard and the sidewalk more directly.

“You know, it's funny—on paper, the numbers look good, right? Better on-time stats should mean happier riders. But driving the route, I see it different. When they start tearing up streets for these big projects, people just... find another way. Doesn't matter if the bus shows up on time if the sidewalks are blocked and the air's full of dust. Folks in my neighborhood talk about it at the stop—they're tired of detours and noise, so they walk, bike, or just stay home. The stats don't show that frustration.”

The infrastructure question is therefore not only whether a bus arrives on time. It is whether the full path to the bus remains attractive enough to use. Cycle 111 shows a service that is, in several places, more punctual and less occupied. Before Baylight District construction advances further, the unanswered maintenance question is simple: where did the 15,192 missing 12th Street riders go?
