# From the Tribune archive: The Fund Has Moved $7 Million Out of $28 Million. The Record Names Two Households.

What The Bay Tribune published before today about the people and places in Carmen Delaine's story "The Fund Has Moved $7 Million Out of $28 Million. The Record Names Two Households.". Earlier coverage, not today's news.

In Cycle 78, Mayor Avery Santana proposed and signed into law the \$28 million West Oakland Stabilization Fund after the City Council passed the initiative on a 6–3 vote (*The Cycle Pulse — Y3C8*; *Lore: avery_santana_deep_dive (Y3C10)*). Designed to protect legacy flatland residents from displacement amid encroaching industrial development, the fund placed administrative oversight on the fourth floor of City Hall under Deputy Mayor Brenda Okoro (*Lore: avery_santana_deep_dive (Y3C10)*). When bureaucratic delays initially held up relief, Santana intervened directly to release funds to families awaiting assistance on administrative spreadsheets (*Lore: avery_santana_deep_dive (Y3C10)*).

In Cycles 108 and 109, as the fund entered its active disbursement phase, West Oakland business owners like baker Daniel Cloak questioned where the money was landing, noting that residents had long watched civic promises move slower than they sounded. In Cycle 110, Economic and Workforce Development program director Marcus Webb testified before the City Council Finance Committee that seventy-nine households had been stabilized in nine months under a strict 23-day processing standard, emphasizing audit compliance and speed so families received checks before eviction notices arrived.

By Cycle 112, the mayor’s office issued statements celebrating consecutive \$400,000 monthly disbursements and crediting Okoro’s staff for upholding the 23-day turnaround. However, Webb privately noted that six household files remained stalled awaiting vendor title searches, warning that while administrative spreadsheets reported the program as ahead of schedule, spreadsheets never have to sit in a kitchen and explain delays to families on the brink of losing their homes. Throughout these cycles, reporting consistently highlighted that official municipal ledgers tracked aggregate dollars and speed metrics while omitting payee names, household identities, and clear neighborhood outcomes.
