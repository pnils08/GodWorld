# Chinatown's Line Is Shorter Than Its Count

By Dr. Lila Mezran

Mai Tse walks to school past the line every morning. She is a student in Chinatown, and she has a view of the clinic that most people in the city would not get.

"I see the clinic lines every morning when I walk to school," she says. "Mostly, it's older people who go in, and sometimes parents with little kids. I don't see many people my age there. Some people wait for a while, but others just look at the line and walk away. I don't know why they leave—maybe it's too long, or they don't think they need it."

The clinic is the community clinic on 8th Street. Niani Oakley, a plumber who lives in Chinatown, knows it the way neighbors know a corner store. She describes the place as a first stop, not a last one.

"Most folks around here, if it's not an emergency but they need more than home remedies, they'll head over to the community clinic on 8th Street. It's close, and they know how to handle the usual stuff without making a big deal out of it."

Those two accounts, a student watching from the sidewalk and a tradesperson who knows the block, are the closest thing this cycle's record offers to a view from the street. The rest of the record is in numbers, and the numbers say Chinatown is carrying more illness than any other neighborhood in the city.

The census counts 144 sick residents in Chinatown this cycle. That is one fewer than the cycle before, and it still puts the neighborhood at the top of the city's list. East Oakland follows at 123, Ivy Hill at 122, and Fruitvale at 118. Across 22 neighborhoods the total is 1,907. Chinatown's 144 comes to 6.6 percent of its 2,193 residents, at or above the 6 percent watch bar the record sets.

Then the record turns to the hospital, and the count gets smaller. Chinatown produced 3 admissions this cycle. At the close, 6 Chinatown residents were in care, occupying 6 beds. The census counts all six as other residents, with none tracked by name. Citywide, the hospital took 37 admissions, held 75 in care at the close, and filled 75 beds. Thirty-nine residents left care during the cycle.

Read plainly, 144 sick residents and 3 admissions leave most of Chinatown's illness outside a hospital bed this cycle. That does not tell us what happened to those people. Some may have been seen at the clinic on 8th Street. Some may have stayed home and recovered. The record does not say which, and it does not say how many went without any care at all.

Two residents who are in hospital care now, both listed in Rockridge, have heard about Chinatown from their beds. Yu Zen was admitted at cycle 105 after a workplace accident. Maurice Franklin was admitted at cycle 106 after severe seasonal flu. Neither lives in Chinatown, and neither is described in the record as sick with anything connected to the neighborhood. Both talk about it anyway.

Yu Zen: "From where I'm lying in this hospital bed, all I hear are the nurses talking about how Chinatown's been hit hard. It's not just numbers—it's families, neighbors. When you're lying here, you feel the weight of it. Chinatown's tight-knit; when one suffers, it ripples through everyone."

Maurice Franklin: "Lying here in the hospital, you hear things. Nurses talking, other patients' families visiting. Chinatown's been hit hard this season—seems like every other conversation is about someone else down there getting sick. It makes you realize how connected we all are, even when we're in different neighborhoods."

The two hospital patients hear the count as talk in the corridor. Mai Tse sees it as a line that some people join and some walk away from. Niani Oakley sees it as a clinic that handles the usual things without drama. None of them has the full picture, and the record does not fully provide it either.

Chinatown's count is one fewer than last cycle. The hospital trail is three admissions long. The gap between those two numbers is where a household makes its decisions: whether to take a sick child to the clinic, whether to wait out a fever at home, who stays back from work to cover the shift.

The question this cycle's record leaves open is when recovering at home stops being a choice a household makes and becomes something the system has stopped tracking. Who should answer that, and how the city would know which residents are on the far side of that line, the record does not say.
