# Three Doors Opened in C117, and Only Two Said Why

Three congregations in three neighborhoods logged crisis responses in the Faith Ledger this cycle. Masjid An-Nur Oakland held a community grief gathering. Temple Beth Or held a vigil for community healing. Adams Point United Methodist activated an emergency assistance fund. I asked each what it was answering. Two gave me an answer. The third did not, and I'm leaving that gap in the piece.

## East Oakland: a room, some tea, a circle of chairs

The ledger puts the grief gathering at Masjid An-Nur at 56 people. It is the masjid's first crisis-response row. The earlier entries were community programs: a food pantry in C111, a seniors lunch in C110 and C115, and after-school tutoring in C114.

I assumed the gathering grew out of the seniors lunch. Aziz Rahimi corrected me. He also corrected my title for him: "It's Pastor, not Imam," he said, though the registry lists him as Imam.

"That lunch is about keeping folks connected, a little joy, a hot meal," Rahimi said. "This was something heavier. It came from the families over on the 23rd Avenue side, the ones who lost people to the violence last spring."

He said a couple of grandmothers came to him "after the second funeral in a month" and asked for a place where people "could just sit with it, not have to perform strength." The masjid opened its community room, put out tea and set chairs in a circle.

"It wasn't a program I built. They built it. I just unlocked the door."

He said the aunties came, and so did young men from the corner crew, a mechanic from down the block and a few teachers from the school. I have no incident record for the violence he describes. That account is his and the grandmothers'. The ledger carries the event and its size, and nothing about a cause.

## Adams Point: "Neighbors standing on our steps"

The ledger puts the emergency assistance fund at 50 people. It is the second crisis row for Adams Point United Methodist. The first was C94, when the church opened its doors for community support.

Rev. Daniel Han said no single event opened the fund. "It was more like the ground getting drier and drier," he said. He described people who had lost hours or whose rent went up twice in a year, "folks who are doing everything right and still coming up short." A couple of families came to the church asking whether anyone knew of work. "That was the moment," he said.

The fund pays for what the pantry truck can't cover: a utility bill, a bus pass for a job interview, a week of groceries for a family waiting on a first paycheck. He was frank about the limits.

"We're a congregation, not a foundation," Han said. If the fund runs past a couple of months, he said, the church will have to lean on the Interfaith Council and the city.

Deputy Mayor for Community Development Brenda Okoro called Adams Point the one that worries her most. She said the neighborhood's sentiment reading is -0.06 and its migration is -3. It has 80 unemployed and 81 sick adults on her sheet, she said, and "the ONLY relief valve on the ledger is the congregation's own emergency fund." She said she is flagging Adams Point to her office as a triage priority.

## Rockridge: the vigil has no stated cause

Temple Beth Or's vigil for community healing drew 91, the largest of the three. The week before, the temple's food pantry served 89.

I can't tell you what the vigil was held to heal. My question to Rabbi Naomi Sterling was framed around a different congregation, and her answer doesn't speak to Beth Or's vigil. She said she couldn't speak for the gathering I named and wouldn't "fabricate a memory of a specific vigil just because it makes for a cleaner story." That refusal is the part of her answer that stands. I will ask again, and I'll name the right house.

What she did say concerned her own congregation and the last few months. "The people who come to our pantry and the people who stand with us in prayer are often the same people—just at different points in their week," she said. "Some come for the food, some come for the silence, and some come because they don't know where else to be."

She also pointed to a Tuesday night grief group and an interfaith potluck with a neighboring AME church. Neither is tied to the C117 vigil in the record.

## Are the three connected?

Not by any event on record. Okoro said the three ledger entries are separate, with no shared event ID. Her sheet carries one crisis arc this cycle, the Downtown Transit Snarl. She said it is resolved and doesn't touch these neighborhoods.

"If there was one triggering incident, it is not in my records," she said. She said she is asking Masjid An-Nur and Temple Beth Or directly what they were responding to, and will publish their answers when she has them.

Okoro does draw a pattern: "they are absorbing stabilization work the city budget doesn't reach." That is her reading, and I can't confirm it from the record. The congregations' own accounts fit it in two places. Han's fund is paying rent-and-utility gaps, and Rahimi's room became a place to grieve after two funerals in a month. Neither leader tied his work to the other's.

Robert Jaston, who leads New Covenant Pentecostal Assembly in West Oakland, wasn't one of the three crisis rows. His pantry is logged as an ordinary community program this cycle. I'm including him because he described the same strain. He said last month's pantry crowd was the biggest since it opened, with some people driving in from East Oakland because their own pantries have waiting lists. "It's not one type of person anymore," he said.

Rahimi's words may be the plainest summary of the three: "That's it. That's the whole story."
