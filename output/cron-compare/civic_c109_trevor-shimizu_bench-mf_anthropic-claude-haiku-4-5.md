# 12th Street Moving: Ridership Surge and the Crew That Keeps It Running

Eleanor Rivera stood on the platform at 12th Street Oakland City Center early in the morning cycle, watching the flow. The station had changed its rhythm. More people. More pressure. More of everything that meant her team had to adapt faster.

"At 12th Street, we've definitely felt the uptick in ridership," Rivera said, her eyes tracking the arrivals and departures across the display boards. "It's not just numbers—it's people adjusting their routines, and us adjusting right along with them. The team just... adapts. No big announcements, just everyone doing their part to keep things moving smoothly."

The numbers were real. Ridership at 12th Street had climbed to 23,848 this cycle, up 5,472 from the prior one. On-time performance had ticked up four points to 85 percent. Across the transit network—eight stations and ten corridors on the record—the city had moved 99,582 riders, a jump of nearly 29,000 from cycle 108. Game day downtown. Events stacked at Lake Merritt. The construction planning vote hanging over the Baylight District. All of it converged on the platforms.

But numbers don't mop spills or clear trash or check brakes on buses before dawn.

Jerald Jensen saw the wear first. The janitor at 12th Street moved through the platform each morning, tracking what the surge looked like on the floor.

"You wanna know about 12th Street? I see it in the wear and tear—more coffee spills to mop up, more trash cans overflowing by noon," Jensen said. "Used to be you could catch your breath between rushes, but now? It's constant. Folks pouring in for work, for games, for whatever's happening downtown."

Joel Wren, mechanic for AC Transit, had been watching the buses run hotter. More riders meant more load. More load meant more strain on every system underneath.

"You know, I've been seeing more folks hopping on the buses lately—especially down at 12th Street," Wren said. "It's good to see people out and about, trusting the system again. But what really gets me is the crew keeping those buses running. Those mechanics and drivers? They're the ones putting in the long hours to make sure everyone gets where they need to go, especially with all these extra riders. Makes you appreciate the work that goes on behind the scenes."

Coen Cartier drove one of those buses. He felt the surge every trip.

"I've seen more folks hopping on the buses lately, especially around 12th Street," Cartier said. "It's been busy, but it feels good to know we're helping people get where they need to go. The team's been working hard to keep things running smoothly, and it's paying off."

The spike wasn't confined to downtown. Coliseum station gained 4,859 riders. Fruitvale rose 3,756. MacArthur added nearly 3,000. Nineteen stations across the network showed movement upward, with on-time performance climbing at most stops—19th Street Oakland jumped ten points to 86 percent.

Only Rockridge broke the pattern. Its on-time rate dropped two points to 78 percent, even as ridership climbed by 2,121. One platform's gain was another's ceiling.

On the street level, the highways barely moved. I-880 North held at traffic index 100, same as the prior cycle. I-880 South ticked up five points. Telegraph Avenue jumped eleven points—game day pressure and event traffic stacking on top of the baseline flow. MacArthur Boulevard climbed ten points to 77, a significant lift but still short of gridlock. Grand Avenue eased down five points to 73, the only major corridor to loosen.

The system was absorbing the surge. For now. But absorption and sustainability are not the same condition. Two weeks of game days and civic events had pushed transit ridership up 41 percent. The platforms were designed for what they carried. The buses ran their routes on time. The crew showed up and moved people from point to point. But the margin between smooth operation and strain is not infinite, and it shrinks faster than most systems plan for. When Fruitvale Transit Hub moves from design-phase to construction, when the Baylight District construction vote moves from planning to ground disruption, when the next sequence of events stacks on top of the current load—the question becomes not whether the system adapts, but how much adaptation it can hold before something has to give. That remains to be seen.
